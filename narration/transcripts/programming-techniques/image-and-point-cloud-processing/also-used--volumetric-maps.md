Volumetric maps. 

This page explains how a robot turns many depth pictures into one 3D map of the space round it. It answers five questions, and the first four of them follow the order in which the map is built. How is space cut into small cubes, and how is each cube marked as free, occupied, or not yet seen? How does a program combine many noisy depth readings of the same cube into one answer? Why do most maps store their cubes in a tree instead of a plain grid? What are the truncated signed distance function and the Euclidean signed distance field, and why do planners like them? And finally, which libraries do this, and which of them are used most?

It is for a reader who knows what a depth picture and a point cloud are. While a depth picture holds one distance for each pixel, a point cloud is the list of 3D points you get by turning each depth pixel into a point. It also helps to have read about voxel downsampling, which cuts space into the same small cubes this page uses.

One depth picture only shows the surfaces in front of the camera at one moment. But a robot arm needs more than that, because it has to remember the shelf behind it after it has turned away. It also has to know that the space under its gripper is empty, and to know which space it has never seen at all. A volumetric map holds all of this, and "volumetric" means that it describes the space itself, cube by cube, and not only the surfaces.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. A volumetric map cuts space into small cubes and, for every depth reading, marks the cubes the camera saw through as more likely free, and the cube where the reading ended as more likely occupied, so that many readings add up to one steady answer. 

For example, imagine you walk into a dark room with a torch. Each time you shine it, the beam passes through empty air and stops on a wall or a chair. This means you learn two things from each beam: the air along the beam is empty, and something solid stands where the beam stopped. But corners you never shine the torch at stay unknown. After a few minutes of shining the torch round, you have a good picture of the room in your head, even though no single beam showed you much. A volumetric map does the same with the thousands of beams in each depth picture.

The next part of the page explains how it works. The map is built in six steps. Steps one to four build an occupancy map, which says for each cube whether it is free, occupied, or unknown. Then steps five and six build two other kinds of map from the same readings. While one of them gives a smooth surface, the other gives the distance to the nearest obstacle, which is what many planners read.

All the examples here describe a slice of a table seen from above, 64 centimetres by 64 centimetres, cut into cells of 1 centimetre. A real map is 3D, with cubes instead of squares, but every step works the same way. The table holds a box, a round tin, and a wall at the back.

Step one is to cut space into voxels. A voxel, short for volume pixel, is one small cube of space. The map cuts the robot's work space into voxels of equal size and keeps a few numbers for each one. The size of a voxel is called the resolution, and for a table-top arm it is usually 1 to 2 centimetres. Because a smaller voxel shows more detail, it also needs more memory and more time. But a plain grid of voxels grows fast, because a cube of space 1 metre on each side, cut into 1 centimetre voxels, has one million voxels. Step four will show how a tree cuts that number down.

Step two is to cast a ray for each depth pixel. Each depth pixel says that along this line from the camera, the first surface is at this distance. So the program follows that line, called a ray, from the camera to the measured point. This is called ray casting, and along the way it collects two kinds of voxel. First, it collects every voxel the ray passes through before the end. The camera saw through them, so they are evidence for free space. This is called a miss. Second, it collects the voxel where the ray ends. Something is there, so it is evidence for occupied space. This is called a hit. But voxels behind the end point get nothing, because the camera cannot see behind a surface, so they stay unknown. Unknown is not the same as free, because the space behind a box might hold another object.

A diagram shows a depth camera's rays over a table, and the free, occupied, and unknown cells after one view and after two. It shows the real scene and every tenth ray of the camera. The camera has a 70 degree view and 141 rays, and each ray's reading has a small random error of about 3 millimetres. After the first camera view, 933 cells are free, 74 are occupied, and over 3,000 are unknown. Most of the map is unknown, because the camera only sees a wedge of the table, and the areas behind the box and the tin are hidden. Then, a second view is added from a camera position on the right, which sees round the tin and along the right of the table. As a result, free cells rise to 1,200, occupied cells to 109, and unknown cells fall to 2,787. But the inside of the box and the tin stays unknown in both maps, because no camera can see inside a solid object. 

However, the occupied cells are not perfect lines. Where a ray grazes a surface at a shallow angle, the small reading error puts its end in the cell next door. Step three is how the map copes with that error, by combining readings with log-odds. 

A single reading can be wrong, because a depth camera makes stray points at the edges of objects, and a hand can pass through the view for one frame. So a map does not say "free" or "occupied" after one reading. Instead it keeps, for each voxel, a probability: a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely the voxel is to be occupied. Every voxel starts at 0.5, which means "no idea". Then each new reading changes that probability a little. The most common library, OctoMap, uses default values where a hit means the voxel is occupied with probability 0.7, and a miss means it is occupied with probability only 0.4. This means that after one hit, a voxel's probability is 0.7, and after two hits it is 0.845. After one hit and then one miss it is 0.609. 

But multiplying probabilities together is awkward and slow, so the map stores each voxel's value as a log-odds number instead. The odds of a voxel are its probability of being occupied divided by its probability of being free, and the log-odds is the natural logarithm of the odds. This is useful because, to combine a new reading, you simply add a fixed number. For example, every hit adds 0.847 to the voxel's log-odds, and every miss subtracts 0.405. A log-odds of 0 means a probability of 0.5. Therefore a positive log-odds means "probably occupied", and a negative one means "probably free". A voxel that was never touched by any ray stays at exactly 0, which marks it as unknown. 

There is one more rule, because the log-odds is clamped. It is not allowed to go above positive 3.511, which is a probability of 0.971, or below negative 2.0, which is a probability of 0.1192. Without this cap, a voxel that was seen many times would become so certain that it could hardly change again. 

A diagram shows why that cap matters, by plotting a cell's chance of being occupied over 10 hits and then 25 misses, with and without the cap. Because a cup stands on the table for 10 depth pictures, its voxel gets 10 hits. Then the arm lifts it away, and every following picture gives the voxel a miss. Without a cap, 10 hits give a log-odds of 8.47, which is a probability of 0.9998. Then it takes 21 misses before the voxel reads free again. At 30 pictures a second, the map would show a ghost cup for most of a second after the cup has gone. But with the cap, the log-odds stops at 3.511 after the fifth hit, and it then takes only 9 misses to read free. In other words, the cap costs a little certainty and buys a map that keeps up with a changing table.

Step four is to store the map in a tree. Most of a work space is either empty air or unknown. But a plain grid spends the same memory on a big block of empty air as on the edge of an object. So a tree avoids that waste. An octree is a tree of cubes, and the whole work space starts as one big cube. If everything inside it is the same, it stays one cube. But if not, it is cut into 8 equal smaller cubes, and each of those is checked in the same way, down to the smallest voxel size. In 2D, the same idea cuts a square into 4 smaller squares, and then the tree is called a quadtree. 

A diagram compares the same map as a plain grid of 4,096 cells and as a tree of 799 squares. Because the big squares hold open air and unknown space, the tree says exactly the same thing with a fifth of the pieces. In 3D the saving is much larger, because a big cube holds 8 times as many voxels at each level, not 4. This is why OctoMap is an octree. It has a second benefit as well, because a program can ask the map at a coarser level, such as "is anything in this 8 centimetre cube?", with one look.

Step five introduces the truncated signed distance function. An occupancy map is good for asking if a space is free, but it is poor at showing the exact shape of a surface, because each voxel is only "in" or "out". So a signed distance function stores something more precise. For each voxel it stores the distance from the voxel's centre to the nearest surface. The sign says which side the voxel is on: positive in front of the surface, in free space, and negative behind it, inside the object. This means the surface itself is where the value crosses zero. 

A truncated signed distance function, or TSDF, only keeps the exact value close to the surface. Values further than a chosen truncation distance are cut off. Many libraries go further and only visit the voxels inside this thin band. Because the camera knows nothing about voxels far behind a surface, those voxels are not updated at all. Keeping only a thin layer round each surface saves work, and it stops one side of a thin object from wiping out the other. 

Each new depth picture updates the TSDF by a weighted average. For each voxel near the measured surface, the program works out the signed distance from this reading, then mixes it into the stored value. For example, take one camera ray, with 1 centimetre voxels and a truncation distance of 3 centimetres. A surface stands 50.0 centimetres from the camera, and three depth readings measure it at 50.4, 49.7, and 50.2 centimetres. 

A diagram shows these three readings giving three TSDF lines along a ray. Take the voxel whose centre is at 49.5 centimetres. Because the first reading says the surface is at 50.4, this voxel is 0.9 centimetres in front of it. The other two readings give 0.2 and 0.7, so the average of the three is 0.6. The next voxel, at 50.5 centimetres, gets negative 0.1, negative 0.8, and negative 0.3, which average to negative 0.4. This means the value crosses zero between these two voxels, and a straight line between them crosses zero at 50.10 centimetres. That is exactly the average of the three readings, and within 1 millimetre of the true surface. Because the voxels at 53.5 and 54.5 centimetres are more than 3 centimetres behind every reading, they are not updated. This is how a TSDF turns many noisy depth pictures into one smooth surface, finer than its own voxels. A program then pulls a triangle mesh out of the zero crossing with a method called marching cubes.

Step six is the distance field that planners read. A planner does not need to know what an obstacle looks like. Instead it needs to know how far each point of the arm is from the nearest obstacle. So a Euclidean signed distance field, or ESDF, stores exactly that for every voxel: the straight-line distance from the voxel to the nearest occupied voxel, which is negative inside obstacles. Libraries build it from the TSDF or the occupancy map by spreading a wave out from the surfaces, one layer of voxels at a time. 

A diagram shows the ESDF of the two-view map, with lines at 5, 10, and 15 centimetres from the nearest obstacle. A planner models the arm as a set of balls. This means a ball is clear of every obstacle when the ESDF at its centre is bigger than its radius, and that is one look-up per ball, however many obstacles there are. The diagram checks two balls that stand for parts of the gripper. A ball with a radius of 4 centimetres has an ESDF at its centre of 12.4 centimetres, so it is clear by 8.4 centimetres. But another ball with a radius of 5 centimetres has an ESDF at its centre of only 4.1 centimetres. This means it overlaps the box by 0.9 centimetres, and the planner rejects that pose. An ESDF also says which way is away from the obstacle, which is the direction in which the distance grows fastest. So optimisation planners use that direction to push a path away from obstacles.

The second function reads a voxel's state, returning unknown if it was never updated, occupied if its log-odds is greater than zero, and free otherwise. The third function updates the TSDF by calculating the signed distance for voxels near the surface and keeping a running average. Finally, a function checks if the arm is clear by looking up the ESDF at the centre of each ball making up the arm, and returning false if the distance is less than or equal to the ball's radius.

The next section explains where this is used on a robot arm. A volumetric map sits between the depth camera and the parts of the program that must not hit anything. There are several common uses. First, avoiding obstacles nobody put in the model. A planning program knows the shape of the arm and the fixed table, but not a toolbox someone left on the table. MoveIt, the most used planning framework for arms, builds an OctoMap from the depth camera for exactly this, treating every occupied voxel as an obstacle. Second, remembering what the camera cannot see now. When a wrist camera turns away, the map keeps the table's voxels so the planner still avoids them. Third, removing the arm from its own map. The arm is often in the camera's view, so MoveIt removes every point that lies on the arm's known shape, plus a small margin, before it updates the map. Fourth, planning fast with a distance field. GPU planners can read an ESDF, and optimisation planners use its distance and direction at every waypoint. Fifth, scanning an object before grasping it. A TSDF fuses pictures from several sides of a part into one closed mesh for a grasp planner. Sixth, choosing where to look next. Unknown voxels show where the robot has not looked, helping a program pick the next camera pose. Finally, checking whether a space is empty. Before placing a part in a bin, the arm asks the map whether the voxels where the part will go are free, because an unknown voxel means it needs to look first. 

However, a volumetric map is not a measuring tool. An occupancy map with 1 or 2 centimetre voxels is the model of what the arm must not hit, not the model of the part it is about to grasp. The part itself is measured on the point cloud.

The next part covers where it is useful, and where it is not. A volumetric map works best when the camera's pose is known well, the depth is good, and the scene changes slowly compared with the camera's frame rate. But it can fail in a few common ways. 

If the camera's pose is wrong, walls and objects appear twice or smeared, and free space eats into real obstacles. The solution is to recalibrate, use the joint angles from the exact moment of the picture, or slow the arm while mapping. If the voxels are too big, the planner refuses gaps the gripper could pass through, and thin rods vanish or grow fat. The fix is a smaller voxel near the work area, or a separate precise model for the part being grasped. If the voxels are too small, the map update is slow and memory grows. Here, you should use a larger voxel, a GPU library, or update the map at a lower rate. Glass, shiny metal, or black foam cause missing hits, so a real obstacle reads as free or unknown. You can treat unknown space as blocked near the arm, or add the object as a known shape. Objects that move quickly, like a person's hand, leave ghost obstacles. This is handled by clamping the log-odds, clearing the map before each plan, or using a separate safety sensor for people. If the arm's own body is not fully removed, a cloud of false voxels appears round the gripper. This requires a larger removal margin or checking the arm model and camera pose. Finally, if unknown space is treated as free, the planner might send the arm into space no camera has seen. To fix this, mark unknown voxels near the path as obstacles, or look there first.

The next section lists libraries that provide these maps. Four libraries do most of this work for robot arms. First is OctoMap, written in C++ with ROS wrappers. It builds a log-odds occupancy octree with clamping. It is the most used occupancy map for arms because MoveIt uses it. Second is Open3D, used from Python and C++. It builds a TSDF and a mesh from its zero crossing. It is the most used way to fuse depth pictures into a mesh in Python, but it does not build an ESDF for planning. Third is nvblox, which runs in C++ on an NVIDIA graphics card, as well as Python and ROS 2. It builds a TSDF, an ESDF, and a mesh, updated on the graphics card many times a second. It is used in NVIDIA's robot stack. Fourth is voxblox, written in C++ for ROS 1. It builds a TSDF, then an ESDF from it. It is mostly used in research and flying robots. 

In plain words, if you use MoveIt, you already use OctoMap, and it is the one to learn first. If you want a clean 3D model of an object from a handful of depth pictures, use Open3D's TSDF. If you have an NVIDIA card and a planner that reads a distance field, use nvblox. In MoveIt, the map is set up in a configuration file that names the sensor plugin, the topic to read, the maximum range, the margin used to remove the arm's own body, and the voxel size.

The next part asks why you need a volumetric map, and what it costs. A volumetric map gives one steady answer, built from many noisy pictures, to three questions: is this space free, is it occupied, or has the robot not looked there yet? It remembers space the camera no longer sees, and with a distance field, it tells a planner how far every point is from the nearest obstacle in one look-up. 

The obvious alternative is to skip the map and use the latest point cloud directly as the obstacle. That is simpler and has no delay. But a single cloud has holes, stray points, and no memory. It cannot tell "free" from "not seen", which is the difference between safe and unsafe. So choose the raw cloud when the scene is fully in view and nothing is hidden. But choose a map when the camera moves, when parts of the scene are hidden, or when the robot must be sure a space is empty. 

The costs of a volumetric map are that it needs a good calibration and exact timing. You must choose the voxel size, trading off detail, speed, and memory. Updating a 3D map at 1 centimetre takes real computing time, which is why some libraries move that work to the graphics card. Moving objects leave ghosts unless the map is clamped or cleared, and the map is coarse by design.

The next section looks at the learned alternative. No learned model replaces the map as the record of free, occupied, and unseen space, but three kinds of model help it. A shape completion model guesses the hidden back of an object from one view. Scene reconstruction builds a 3D scene from colour photos, seeing glass and shiny surfaces that leave holes in a depth map, though it takes time and the scene must stay still. A learned collision checker gives a fast distance to obstacles like an ESDF, but is least reliable near the edge of an obstacle. So for collision avoidance the map still wins, because it never guesses. It keeps unseen space as unknown, and that honesty keeps the arm safe. In practice, the map and the models are often used together.

Open3D does steps one, two, three, and five for you. It cuts the box into voxels, walks along the ray for every depth pixel, combines the new reading, and runs the marching cubes step to turn distances into triangles. What you still have to write is the map that a planner reads. Open3D does not build the occupancy octree or the Euclidean signed distance field. In a ROS 2 arm project, you do not build those in Python either. MoveIt's occupancy map monitor builds the OctoMap for you from a point cloud topic, configured in a YAML file. So the Python code is the right tool for scanning an object into a mesh, but the wrong tool for giving a planner something to avoid. You also have to supply the exact camera pose for every picture at the moment it was taken. 

Finally, you have to decide the voxel size, the truncation distance, and the depth range. The voxel size sets the detail and memory, usually 2 to 5 millimetres for an object an arm will grip. The truncation distance has to be a few voxels wide, because too small a value makes a noisy reading fall outside the band, and too large a value rounds off the corners. The depth range ignores readings that are too far away, because a depth camera's error grows with distance. You also decide the origin and length of the box the map covers in your own world frame. Put them wrong, and every call succeeds while the mesh comes back empty.
