Multi-view geometry: depth from two pictures. This page explains how a program finds depth from two pictures of the same scene, without a depth sensor. One picture only tells you the direction of each point, but two pictures, taken from two known places, tell you where the point is. The page covers five ideas, and the first two of them work together. Triangulation finds a point from two lines of sight, and epipolar lines say where to look for a match in the second picture. Rectified stereo turns the shift between two side-by-side pictures into depth, while parallax finds a height from how far a point shifts when the camera slides. And the ground-plane constraint gets depth from a single camera when the point is known to be on the table.

This assumes you are familiar with the pinhole camera model and rigid transforms. Other pages already use some of these ideas. The section on baseline, not count, shows why two views far apart beat many views close together, and the section on two photos from one moving camera measures an object's width and height by moving the camera. This page explains the geometry underneath both. Every number here comes from a real run of the diagram script with a specific camera, which has a resolution of 320 by 240 pixels, focal lengths f x and f y of 277.1, a center at x 160 and y 120, and is positioned 0.40 metres above the table.

The core idea in one sentence is this. A pixel tells you the direction of a point; two pixels of the same point, seen from two known places, tell you where the two directions cross, and that crossing is the point. 

So here is an everyday example. Hold a finger up in front of your face, then close your left eye and then your right. The finger jumps sideways against the wall behind it, and a near finger jumps a lot while a far one jumps a little. Your brain uses that jump to judge distance, and two cameras, or one camera that moves, can do the same, because they can measure the jump in pixels.

Everything on this page depends on knowing where the two viewpoints are relative to each other. On a robot arm that knowledge is often free, because the arm itself moved the camera, and its joints report exactly how far.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step, starting with triangulation, which is where two rays cross. Because two directions cross at the point, this section starts with the arithmetic of that crossing. Each pixel defines a ray, which is a straight line that starts at the camera and runs out into the scene. The pinhole camera model gives its direction as u minus c x divided by f x, v minus c y divided by f y, and 1, in the camera's frame. The camera's pose turns that direction into the room's frame.

Triangulation means finding the point where two rays cross. With perfect numbers they cross exactly, but with real, noisy pixels two rays in 3D usually miss each other by a little. So the program looks for the point that is closest to both rays. The standard way writes two straight-line equations for each view and solves them together, which is the same kind of step as the direct linear first guess in finding pose from points.

Here is a worked example, in which a wrist camera looks straight down from 0.40 metres and sees a spot on top of a red box at pixel 212.5, 86.5. The arm then moves the camera 80 millimetres to the side and turns it 13.2 degrees so that it still faces the box, and in the second picture the spot is at pixel 211.9, 86.0.

The diagram shows these two wrist-camera poses, the two rays, and where they cross. On the left, the two rays cross on top of the box, and triangulating the two exact pixels gives 64.4, 41.1, 60.0 millimetres, which is the true spot. On the right is a close-up, where each pixel has been moved by 0.5 pixels, in opposite directions. The rays now cross at 64.0, 40.5, 65.1 millimetres, which is 5 millimetres higher. Almost all of the error is along the line of sight, because two rays that meet at a narrow angle slide along each other easily. Dashed lines show each ray moved by 1.5 pixels, and the diamond between them is the region where the answer could land.

The distance between the two viewpoints is the baseline, and a wider baseline makes the rays meet at a wider angle, which makes the diamond shorter. A table shows the results of repeating the example two thousand times with 0.5 pixels of random noise on each pixel, for four different baselines. As the baseline increases from 18 millimetres to 200 millimetres, the median error drops from 11.3 millimetres down to just 1.3 millimetres. The worst tenth of runs also improves dramatically, falling from 27 millimetres or more at the shortest baseline, to just 2.7 millimetres or more at the longest. The run agrees with the formula from the page on baseline, not count, where the error equals the depth squared, times the pixel noise, divided by the focal length times the baseline. With a depth of 0.34 metres and a noise of 0.5 pixels, doubling the baseline halves the error.

The next step is epipolar lines, which tell you where to look for the match. Triangulation needs to know which pixel in picture 2 shows the same point as a pixel in picture 1. Finding that pixel is called matching, and it is the hard part, but geometry makes it much easier.

The pixel in picture 1 is a ray, and the point is somewhere along that ray, although we do not know where. So now look at that whole ray from camera 2. A straight line in the room looks like a straight line in a picture, so the match must lie somewhere on one line in picture 2. That line is called the epipolar line, and it shrinks the search for the match from the whole picture to one line.

For a second worked example, camera 2 is now 80 millimetres to the side, 30 millimetres forward, and turned 13.2 degrees. There is also a second red box on the table that looks the same as the first one.

The diagram shows how a pixel in view 1 becomes a line in view 2. The spot is at 212.5, 86.5 in view 1, and its epipolar line in view 2 is the purple line. The true match, at 211.9, 110.8, lies on that line, zero pixels away, while the look-alike box is 56.0 pixels from it and therefore cannot be the match, however similar it looks. This exact test is used to match objects between two views on the tracking and association page.

However, the test fails when two candidates both lie on or near the line. Then geometry alone cannot choose, and the program needs appearance or a third view.

The epipolar line comes from one 3 by 3 matrix called the essential matrix, E, which holds how camera 2 is turned and shifted relative to camera 1. If you write a pixel with the lens numbers taken out, as x equals u minus c x divided by f x, v minus c y divided by f y, and 1. Then for any point seen in both pictures, x 2 transposed, times E, times x 1 equals zero. Here, E equals the cross product of the shift t, times the rotation R.

R is the turn from camera 1 to camera 2, and t is the shift. The equation says one thing in plain words: the two rays and the line between the two cameras all lie in one flat plane. They must, because they form a triangle with the point at its tip. So E times x 1 is the epipolar line in picture 2, in lens-free units.

In our example, t is negative 77.9, 30.0, 18.3 millimetres in camera 2's frame, and the equation gives negative 3.5 times ten to the power of negative 18 for the true match, which is zero apart from rounding. When the pixels still carry the lens numbers, the same matrix is written with them folded in. This is called the fundamental matrix, F, and is equal to the inverse transpose of K, times E, times the inverse of K, where K is the 3 by 3 matrix of lens numbers.

The essential matrix can also run backwards. Given five or more matched points, and no knowledge of how the camera moved, a program can find E, and from it R and the direction of t. This is how a camera works out its own movement from pictures alone, and it always uses RANSAC, because some matches are wrong. However, it cannot find the length of t, because two pictures of a small scene taken close together look exactly like two pictures of a large scene taken far apart. So the scale must come from somewhere else: the arm's own movement, a known object size, or a depth reading. On a robot arm, the arm's joints almost always know the movement, so the program uses the known R and t directly.

The next concept is rectified stereo, which turns disparity into depth. A stereo camera is two cameras side by side, fixed to one bar, and the distance between them is its baseline. When the two cameras point the same way and their rows line up, the pair is called rectified. Real stereo cameras are never built perfectly, so their pictures are corrected in software to make them rectified, and that correction uses a stereo calibration.

In a rectified pair, every epipolar line is simply a row of pixels, so a point in row 86 of the left picture is in row 86 of the right picture. The only difference is how far along the row it appears, and that difference is the disparity, which is u left minus u right, measured in pixels. Depth then follows from one division: depth equals the focal length f x times the baseline, divided by the disparity.

The worked example uses a 50 millimetre baseline, which is typical of a small stereo depth camera. The diagram shows the same row in both pictures, and how depth is calculated from the disparity. The spot on the box top is at a u coordinate of 212.5 in the left picture and 171.7 in the right one, in the same row. So the disparity is 40.75 pixels, and 277.1 times 0.050 divided by 40.75 equals 0.340 metres, which is the right depth.

However, a chart shows the catch. Depth falls as one over the disparity, so a far point has a small disparity, and a one-pixel mistake there costs a lot. A table gives the numbers, showing how the error grows as depth increases. At a close depth of 0.20 metres, the disparity is 69.3 pixels, and a one-pixel mistake only changes the depth by 2.8 millimetres. But at a far depth of 2.00 metres, the disparity is just 6.9 pixels, and a one-pixel mistake causes a massive 252.3 millimetre error. Good stereo matchers reach about a quarter of a pixel of accuracy.

The error grows with the square of the depth, so stereo is precise close up and poor far away. For a table-top arm, where most things are under 0.6 metres away, it works well.

To get depth for every pixel, a stereo matcher searches each row of the right picture for the best match of each left pixel. That search needs texture, because a blank white table has nothing to match, and neither does a shiny surface that reflects different things into each camera. So many stereo depth cameras add a projector that throws a dot pattern onto the scene to give plain surfaces some texture. The page on sensors compares these devices.

Next is parallax, which finds height from a sliding camera. A wrist camera can make its own stereo pair. The arm slides the camera sideways, and the program compares the two pictures. How far a point moves in the picture is its parallax, which is the same thing as disparity, except that the baseline comes from the arm rather than from a fixed bar.

This is especially useful for heights. A camera looking straight down at a table sees every table point shift by the same amount, which is f x times the baseline divided by H, where H is the camera's height. A point above the table is closer to the camera, so it shifts more, and that extra shift tells you how far above the table it is.

In the worked example, the camera is 0.40 metres above the table and slides 40 millimetres. The diagram shows that a mark on the table shifts 27.71 pixels, while the spot on the box top shifts 32.60 pixels. So its depth is 277.1 times 0.040 divided by 32.60, which equals 0.340 metres, and its height is 0.40 minus 0.340, which is 60.0 millimetres, the true height of the 6 centimetre cube.

A mistake of half a pixel in the shift gives 54.7 millimetres or 65.1 millimetres instead of 60.0, which is a 5 millimetre error. This is about the same size as in the triangulation example above, because it is the same geometry. A longer slide makes it smaller.

The table's shift is a useful check, because every table point should shift by 27.71 pixels. If they do not, the slide was not what the arm reported, or the camera was not looking straight down. The page on two photos from one moving camera works through the same idea for an object's width.

The final method is the ground-plane constraint, using one camera and a known table. Sometimes one picture is enough. If a point is known to lie on the table, its depth is wherever its ray meets the table. So the table acts as the second view, because it is a second surface that the point must lie on, and a ray and a plane cross at exactly one point. The pinhole camera model page works the method through step by step.

However, the constraint is only as good as the claim that the point is on the table. Here is what happens when the claim is wrong. The straight-down camera sees the spot on top of the 6 centimetre cube at pixel 212.5, 86.5. If the program assumes the spot is on the table, it places it at 75.8, 48.4, 0 millimetres, while the truth is 64.4, 41.1, 60 millimetres. So the position on the table is 13.5 millimetres wrong, as well as the height. With the tilted camera from the pinhole page, which looks 60 degrees down, the same 60 millimetre mistake puts a point 44.1 millimetres too far away. This is because a slanted ray travels a long way sideways for each millimetre of height.

So the ground-plane constraint suits flat things that really lie on the table: sheets, coins, the bottom edge of an object where it touches the table. For the top of an object, use one of the two-view methods above, or subtract the object's known height before intersecting the ray with a plane at that height.

The section ends with pseudocode for the three most common jobs, which I will describe in plain steps. 

First, to triangulate one point from two views with known poses, the program calculates the ray for each pixel using the camera poses and lens numbers. It then finds the point closest to both rays using linear least squares. It also checks the shortest distance between the two rays; if the gap is more than a few millimetres, it rejects the match because the pixels were not the same point. 

Second, to match along the epipolar line, the program calculates the essential matrix from the two known camera poses, and folds in the lens numbers to get the fundamental matrix. It multiplies this by the first pixel to get the line in the second picture. It then finds candidate features within 2 pixels of that line, and picks the one that looks most like the feature in the first picture. 

Third, to find height from a sideways slide of a downward camera, the program measures the shift in pixels for the same point. Depth is the focal length times the slide length, divided by the shift. Height is then the camera height minus that depth. As a check, table points must shift by the focal length times the slide length, divided by the camera height.

The next part of the page explains where these methods are used on a robot arm. Two views appear on a robot arm more often than it seems. First, most depth cameras on arms are stereo pairs with a dot projector. The rectification and the disparity search run inside the camera, and the depth picture that comes out is the focal length times the baseline divided by disparity for every pixel. Second, for measuring with a wrist camera, the arm moves the camera between two poses and triangulates corners, holes or edges. The baseline comes from the joints, so it is known exactly. Third, for heights of objects a depth camera cannot see, like glass and shiny metal, their outlines still shift with parallax. Fourth, when matching the same object in two cameras, a cell with two fixed cameras uses epipolar lines to decide which detection in one picture is the same object as a detection in the other. Fifth, for reading items on a flat table, a single colour camera above a conveyor or a table uses the ground-plane constraint to place flat parts, with no depth sensor. Finally, for building a 3D model of the scene, a camera moves through many poses and triangulates many points. The page on scene reconstruction covers the learned methods that do this.

The page then covers where it works, and where it does not. The uses just mentioned all depend on knowing the two camera poses and matching the right pixels. Two-view geometry is exact, so its failures come from the inputs. A table lists the common problems and what to do instead. 

If the baseline is short for the distance, you will see depth that jumps by centimetres between frames; the solution is to move the camera further between views, or get closer. If there is a wrong match, you will see a triangulated point far from anything, or a large gap between the rays; instead, match along the epipolar line, reject large gaps, and use RANSAC. On plain or shiny surfaces, you will see holes or noise in the stereo depth picture; to fix this, add a projected dot pattern or use a learned matcher. If the camera poses are wrong, table points will not shift by the expected amount, or there will be a consistent tilt in the depth; you should recalibrate the hand-eye transform or read the arm's pose at the exact moment of the picture. If the object moved between the two pictures, heights and depths will make no sense; take both pictures quickly, or use a stereo camera which takes both at once. If a point assumed on the table is not on it, positions are pushed away from the camera by several centimetres; use the ground-plane constraint only for things that lie flat. Finally, if you try to get scale from pictures alone, you will get a correct shape at the wrong size; take the scale from the arm's movement or a known object. 

Two views also cannot see what neither camera sees, so a point hidden behind something in one view cannot be triangulated. And depth from stereo is poor far away, because the error grows with the square of the distance.

The next section lists libraries that provide these tools. OpenCV provides the two-view building blocks in its calib3d module for C++ and Python, including functions to triangulate points, find the essential and fundamental matrices, recover pose, and compute epipolar lines. It also has stereo functions to calibrate and rectify a pair, compute disparity, and turn disparity into points. ROS 2 provides disparity and point cloud nodes in its stereo image processing package, which turns a stereo pair into a point cloud topic. PoseLib for C++ and Python estimates relative pose by finding the essential matrix and camera movement with RANSAC. COLMAP is a command-line and C++ tool that builds a 3D model from many pictures by triangulating matched features. Finally, depth camera software, like the Intel RealSense SDK, runs the stereo matching inside the camera itself.

The page then explains why you would use two views, and what it costs. It answers four questions: what it is, what it does for you, why it rather than the obvious alternative, and what it costs.

It is the geometry of two rays crossing. So it gives you depth and 3D points from ordinary colour pictures, and it tells you where to look for a match.

The obvious alternative is a single depth camera and nothing else, which gives a depth for every pixel with no matching step in your program. That is the right choice for most table-top arms. However, depth cameras fail on glass, shiny metal and black surfaces, and their fixed internal baseline sets their accuracy. Two-view geometry with a wrist camera instead works on any surface whose outline you can see, and lets you choose the baseline. For example, a 200 millimetre slide is about eleven times more precise than an 18 millimetre built-in stereo baseline. The ground-plane constraint needs no second view at all, but it only works for things that lie flat.

The cost is this. You need to know the two camera poses accurately, which means a good calibration and pictures taken while the arm is still. You need a reliable match for every point. You need a second picture, which takes time when the arm has to move. And you need a wide enough baseline, which the working space may not allow.

The next section covers the learned alternative. The page on depth from pictures covers two learned models for this job. A learned stereo model, such as RAFT-Stereo or FoundationStereo, does the matching with a network, then turns each shift into a depth with the same rule as this page, so its answer is still in real metres. This is suggested when you pick your own cameras, or work in bright light or at longer range, though like a written matcher it still struggles on a plain white surface. A monocular depth model uses a single camera to guess depth from one picture with no second view and no matching. But its errors are often several centimetres at one metre, many versions give no fixed scale, and it is best kept for rough jobs such as telling the foreground from the background. The geometry on this page still wins when you need a measured depth for a grasp, want no graphics processor, or can choose a wide baseline by moving the wrist camera.
