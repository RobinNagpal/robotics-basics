Scene reconstruction: NeRF and Gaussian splatting. This page is about building a whole 3D scene from many ordinary photos. The two best-known methods for it are called NeRF and Gaussian splatting. The page answers four questions. What do these methods build, and how do they turn flat photos into 3D? Why must they be fitted again for every new scene? And when is this worth doing on a robot arm that already has a depth camera?

It helps to know what a point cloud is, and that training a model means adjusting it a little at a time until its answers match the examples. It also helps to understand how lines of sight from known camera places cross at a point, because NeRF and Gaussian splatting use that same idea with many photos.

The first section explains what it is. Scene reconstruction builds one 3D description of a scene from many photos taken from different places, so that you can draw the scene from any point of view. For example, think of walking round a statue with a phone and taking a photo every few steps. Each photo on its own is flat, but together the photos hold enough to work out the whole shape of the statue. A spot on the statue's nose shows up in many photos, each time from a different direction, and where those directions cross is where the nose is. A NeRF or a Gaussian splat stores the result in a form that can draw a new photo from any place, even a place where no photo was taken. It can also draw a depth picture from that place, which says how far away each surface is.

The next part covers what goes in and what comes out. Because the scene is built from photos rather than measured directly, what goes in is two things. First, many photos of the same scene, taken from different places. Tens of photos is common, and they can come from an ordinary colour camera, so no depth camera is needed. Second, the system needs to know where the camera was for each photo, and which way it pointed. This is the camera's pose, which is three numbers for its position and three for its direction. 

A diagram here shows a robot arm carrying its wrist camera round a mug. It takes ten photos from different known places, and its joint readings say exactly where the camera was for each one. 

What comes out of the process is one stored scene. This scene will give you a new colour photo from any camera pose you choose. It will also give you a depth picture from any camera pose, which turns into a point cloud in the usual way. Finally, it can give you a surface, if you run one more program to pull a surface out of it.

Moving on to how it works inside, there are three steps. Step one is to know where each photo was taken. Everything depends on knowing the camera pose for each photo, and there are two ways to get it. A robot arm has the easier of the two ways. If the camera sits on the wrist, then the arm's joint readings say where the wrist was, and so where the camera was, with no extra work. This also means the scene comes out at its real size, in metres. Without an arm, a program such as COLMAP works out the poses from the photos alone. It finds the same small spots in many photos and works back to where each camera must have been. This takes time, and the result has no real size until you measure something in the scene.

Step two depends on the method. With NeRF, it uses a network that answers questions about any spot. NeRF stands for neural radiance field, where radiance means the light and colour that comes from a spot. So a NeRF is one neural network that answers a question about any spot in the scene. You give it the X, Y, and Z coordinates of a spot, and the direction you are looking from. It gives back two things: how solid that spot is, and what colour it looks from that direction. How solid a spot is comes back as a number, where zero means empty air and a large number means the spot is inside something that blocks light. The direction matters for the colour, because a shiny surface looks different from different sides. 

To draw one pixel of a photo, NeRF follows the line of sight from the camera through that pixel and out into the scene. A diagram illustrates this, showing one pixel's line of sight passing through empty air, hitting the near wall of a mug, and continuing to a far wall. The network is asked about each point along that line. The first solid point gives the pixel its colour, and the points hidden behind it count for almost nothing. 

So, the steps for one pixel are as follows. First, pick points along the line, from near the camera to far away. Second, ask the network about each point, meaning how solid it is and what colour it is. Third, walk along the line from the camera. Points in empty air add nothing, and the first solid point adds most of the colour. Points behind it add very little, because the solid point blocks the view. Finally, add all those contributions together, and the total is the colour of the pixel. That same walk also gives the depth of the pixel, which is the distance to where the line first becomes solid.

Step two with Gaussian splatting works differently, using many soft blobs. Instead of a network, Gaussian splatting stores the scene as a long list of small soft blobs, and it uses no network at all to draw. The word Gaussian is the name of the smooth bell shape that each blob's edge fades with. The word splatting is the name for drawing each blob flat onto the picture, one after another. A diagram shows a mug drawn normally, next to the same mug stored as many overlapping soft blobs. Each blob stores a place, a size, a direction, a colour, and how see-through it is. Thousands of them drawn over each other make the picture. 

To draw a photo, the program sorts the blobs from near to far and paints them onto the picture, so that near blobs cover far ones. This is much faster than asking a network about many points along every line of sight, which means a splat can be drawn many times a second. Gaussian splatting is therefore not a neural network at all. It belongs here because it is fitted in the same way, from the same inputs, and because robot teams use it for the same jobs as NeRF.

Step three is to fit the model to the photos. Whichever of the two you use, both methods start with a scene that is wrong. A NeRF starts with random numbers inside its network, while a splat starts with blobs in rough places. Then the program repeats a sequence of steps many thousands of times. First, it picks one of the real photos and its camera pose. Second, it draws the scene from that same pose. Third, it compares the drawing with the real photo, pixel by pixel. Finally, it adjusts the network, or the blobs, a little so the drawing gets closer to the photo. When the drawings match all the photos, the scene is finished, because it must now be right from every direction that had a photo. Since the photos came from all round, the only way to match them all is to have the shape in the right place.

The next section explains how it is trained. The word trained means something different here. Most models are trained once, on a large collection of examples, and then used on new inputs. Instead, a NeRF or a splat is fitted to one scene and knows only that scene. So if the arm moves a cup, the old fit is out of date and a new one is needed. The data is just the photos and the poses, and nobody labels anything, because the photos are the right answers and the drawings are the guesses. The first NeRF took hours or days of computing on a graphics card to fit one scene, but later methods cut that sharply. Instant-NGP and Gaussian splatting can fit a small scene in minutes on a good graphics card. There is also a newer kind of model that is trained once, in the ordinary way, from many thousands of scenes. Then, given a few photos of a new scene, it gives the 3D points straight away with no fitting at all. DUSt3R, mentioned in the next section, is one of these.

The page then lists some well-known models that robot teams use. NeRF, from 2020, was the first neural radiance field, showing that one network fitted to photos can draw new views of a scene with fine detail. Instant-NGP, from NVIDIA in 2022, stores most of what the network knows in a fast lookup table, making fitting a NeRF far faster. 3D Gaussian Splatting, from the Inria research institute in 2023, introduced the soft blobs described earlier, and can draw new views in real time on a good graphics card. Its code has a research-only licence. Nerfstudio is an open-source toolkit with an Apache 2.0 licence that fits both NeRFs and splats with the same commands. Dex-NeRF, from 2021, used a NeRF to find and grasp transparent objects with a robot arm, where a depth camera gives no usable points. Finally, DUSt3R, from 2024, is trained once on many scenes, so that from two or more photos of a new scene it gives 3D points for every pixel, even when the camera poses are unknown.

Next is a worked example about grasping a drinking glass. The clearest case for all this is an object a depth camera cannot see. Here, an arm has to pick up a clear drinking glass from a table with a depth camera on its wrist. The depth camera's light passes straight through the glass, so the point cloud shows the table where the glass should be. That means a point cloud model has nothing to work with. 

Scene reconstruction solves it in five steps. First, the arm moves its wrist camera along an arc round the glass and takes a colour photo at each stop. Second, at each stop the software saves the joint readings, and from them it works out the camera pose for that photo. Third, a NeRF is fitted to the photos and poses. The edges of the glass bend and reflect light in a way that changes from photo to photo, so to match all the photos the NeRF has to put something solid where the glass is. Fourth, the software asks the NeRF for a depth picture from a camera pose straight above the glass, and now the glass has depth readings. Finally, that depth picture becomes a point cloud, and a grasp model chooses where to put the fingers. This is the idea behind Dex-NeRF. The whole job takes much longer than one depth shot, because the arm has to move and the NeRF has to be fitted. So it is worth it only when the depth camera cannot see the object.

The next section covers what goes wrong. The glass example worked because the glass stayed where it was, but five things go wrong when conditions are less kind. First, the scene must stay still. Fitting assumes every photo shows the same scene, so if something moves between photos the fit gets confused and draws blurry or doubled objects. Even a person walking past in the background can spoil it. Second, having too few views. With only a few photos, the method can match them all with the wrong shape. Small bits of fog or stray blobs then appear floating in the air. They look fine from the photo positions and wrong from anywhere else, so more photos from more directions is the main fix. Third, bad camera poses. If the poses are off, the lines of sight from different photos do not meet in the right places, and the result is blurry. On an arm this comes from poor calibration between the camera and the wrist. Fourth, a splat is not a surface. The blobs are placed to make the pictures look right, so their centres are not points on the real surface. Plain Gaussian splatting is often the least accurate method for measuring. So if you need a surface to grasp, use a variant made for surfaces, or ask for a depth picture and check it. Finally, it is slow. Even fast methods take seconds to minutes to fit, which is far too slow for an arm that has to react to things moving. Scene reconstruction therefore suits jobs where the arm can stop, look carefully, and then act.

This leads to why you would use this rather than a depth camera, and what it costs. Scene reconstruction is a way to build one 3D scene from many photos with known camera poses. It does give the arm a scene it can view from any direction, including depth where a depth camera fails. The obvious alternative is a depth camera, with no fitting at all, because it gives a point cloud in one shot, many times a second. If you need more sides, you take depth shots from several places and merge the point clouds. 

So why fit a scene instead? First, a depth camera fails on see-through, shiny, and very dark surfaces, while scene reconstruction works from colour photos and uses the fact that these surfaces look different from different sides. Second, it fills the gaps between views, because merged point clouds have holes and overlaps while a fitted scene is one smooth whole. Third, it works with a plain colour camera, which is cheaper and smaller than a depth camera on a small arm. And fourth, it can draw realistic new photos, so teams use it to make extra training pictures for other models from views the camera never took. 

What it costs you comes in four parts. It costs time, because the arm has to move round the scene and the fit has to run, which is seconds at best and often minutes. It requires a graphics card, since fitting on an ordinary processor is very slow. The scene must not change while you fit it, and the fit is thrown away when it does. And finally, licences, because much of the Gaussian splatting code is for research only, so you must check before building a product on it. So, use a depth camera for everyday picking, and use scene reconstruction when the depth camera cannot see the object, or when you need a detailed, complete model of a scene that will stay still.

The page then mentions the written alternative. Instead of fitting a scene, other methods build 3D from many views using traditional programming. Multi-view geometry finds points seen from two or more known camera places, and measures the height of glass from how far its outline shifts. Volumetric maps combine many depth pictures into one map of small cubes, each marked free, occupied, or not yet seen. Iterative closest point lines up point clouds taken from several places to remove small errors in the camera poses. These need no fitting and no graphics card, and they give measured shapes. Instead, scene reconstruction wins when the arm needs new pictures from views it never took, or when a clear or shiny object gives neither depth readings nor a sharp outline.

The final section is about using it in Python. The page has explained that a reconstruction is fitted to the photos of one scene rather than trained once and reused, and that this fitting is the run. That shapes how you use it, because what you run is not a Python call on one input but a fitting job over a folder of photos. This section shows the real shape of that work. 

Nerfstudio is the recommended toolkit, and it is driven from the command line. There is no short documented Python call that fits a scene, so the process is actually three commands. The first command works out where each photo was taken. The second command fits the scene. The third command writes the result out as a point cloud. 

Your Python code then starts where that export lands, and it is ordinary Open3D work from there. The code example shows loading the exported point cloud file. It finds the table as a flat plane, keeps everything that is not the table, and groups the remaining points into separate objects. It then loops through these objects, printing the number of points and the center position for each one. 

What the toolkit gives you out of the box is a great deal. The first command runs COLMAP to recover the camera poses, the second does the whole fitting, and the third gives you a file in a format Open3D reads. You write none of the rendering and none of the optimisation, and switching between a NeRF and a splat is just a matter of changing one word in the command. 

What you still have to write yourself is the photo capture and everything after the export. Something has to move the arm to tens of viewpoints and record a picture at each one, and that is your program. Then the exported cloud is a cloud like any other, so finding the object in it and turning that into a grasp is the same work as usual. One detail matters more than it looks. When COLMAP recovers the poses by itself, it has no way of knowing the real size of anything, so the exported cloud is in the scene's own units rather than in metres. You fix that either by giving nerfstudio the poses from the arm's joint readings, which are already in metres, or by measuring one known distance in the cloud and scaling everything by what you find. 

What you have to decide first is whether the minutes of fitting fit into your task at all, because the same fitting has to run again for every new scene. So this is a sensible choice for a shelf that is scanned once, and a poor one for objects arriving on a belt. You also decide how many photos to take and how much they overlap, since COLMAP fails on blurry or barely overlapping pictures and then nothing downstream works. Finally, you decide on the licence, because nerfstudio itself is Apache 2.0, while the original 3D Gaussian Splatting code is for research only.
