RANSAC: fitting when some points are wrong.

This page explains random sample consensus, or RANSAC, which fits a line, a plane, a circle, or a cylinder to a set of points. It is meant for the case where many of those points belong to something else. It answers four questions, and the first of them is how RANSAC finds the right shape when a third or half of the points are wrong. The other three are how many tries it needs, where a robot arm uses it, and what makes it fail.

It is for a reader who has read the page on least-squares fitting. That page explains parameters, residuals, and the root mean square residual, and it shows that one bad point can move a least-squares line a long way. This page starts from that problem, and every number on it comes from a real run of the diagram script.

RANSAC is the standard way a table-top robot finds the table in a depth camera's point cloud. Although Martin Fischler and Robert Bolles published it as long ago as 1981, it is still the first thing most perception pipelines run on a new point cloud.

The first section asks what this page answers. Real point clouds are messy, and a depth camera looking at a table shows why. It sees the table, but it also sees the objects standing on it, the edge of the table, and the floor beyond. Then there are the false readings, which are scattered points that appear where the light hit a shiny surface. So if you want the table's plane, only some of those points belong to it.

Least squares uses every point, so the other points drag the answer away. You could remove them first, but to know which points to remove you would need to know where the table is, and that is the question you started with.

RANSAC breaks this circle, because it does not need to know in advance which points are good. Instead, it finds the shape and the points that belong to it at the same time. The points that belong to the shape are called inliers, and the rest are called outliers.

The next part gives the idea in one sentence. The previous section said that RANSAC finds the shape and its good points at the same time. However, it did not say how, and the answer fits into a single sentence. You guess the shape from a few randomly chosen points, count how many other points agree with the guess, repeat many times, and keep the guess that most points agree with.

Here is an everyday example, in which a group of people each point at where they think a lost ball landed. Most of them saw it, so they point near the same spot, while a few are guessing and point all over the field. If you take the average of all the pointing directions, the guessers drag it away from the true spot. Instead, you pick one person at random and ask everyone else whether they agree with that person, give or take a metre. If most hands go up, then you have found the spot. But if only two hands go up, you picked a guesser, so you pick someone else and ask again.

The key point is that a guess made only from good points will be agreed with by all the other good points. However, a guess that includes even one bad point will be agreed with by almost nobody. So you do not need to know which points are good. You only need to keep trying until one guess is made entirely from good points.

The next section explains how it works, step by step, starting with the loop. It turns that single sentence into a loop you could program. RANSAC needs three things from you. First, the kind of shape. Second, the smallest number of points that defines one such shape. This is two for a line, three for a plane, and three for a circle on a table. Third, a distance limit. This limit says how close a point must be to the shape to count as agreeing with it, so it should be a little larger than the sensor's noise.

Once you have given it those three things, it runs a loop. First, it picks the smallest number of points at random. Second, it makes the one shape that passes exactly through them. Third, it measures every point's distance from that shape, and counts the points closer than the distance limit, because those points are this try's inliers. Fourth, if this try has more inliers than the best try so far, it remembers it. Fifth, it repeats from the first step a fixed number of times. Finally, it takes the inliers of the best try, and fits the shape to all of them with least squares, so that this last step uses every good point and not just the two or three that were picked.

To show this, the page gives a worked example of a table edge with stray points. The example has 55 points in a side view of a table edge, measured in millimetres. Forty of them lie along the edge, whose true line is y equals 0.25 x plus 20, with 1.5 millimetres of noise. The other fifteen are stray readings scattered above the edge. So 27 percent of the points are outliers.

The first diagram shows three of the 100 tries RANSAC made, with a distance limit of 4 millimetres. Try 1 picked a stray point and an edge point. Its line therefore crosses the edge at an angle, so only 7 points lie within 4 millimetres of it. Try 3 picked two edge points close together at the right end, and their noise tilts the line a little. So it fits the right part of the edge and misses the left part, which leaves only 20 points agreeing. Try 9 picked two edge points 65 millimetres apart, so its line follows the whole edge and all 40 edge points agree with it. No try did better than 40, which is why try 9 is the one RANSAC kept.

The final step fits a least-squares line to those 40 inliers. A second diagram compares the result with a least-squares line through all 55 points. The least-squares line through everything is pulled up by the outliers. Its equation is y equals 0.188 x plus 34.0. Its offset is therefore 14 millimetres too high, and its slope is a quarter too shallow. The RANSAC line follows the edge, with the outliers marked and ignored. Its equation is y equals 0.260 x plus 19.1, which is close to the true y equals 0.25 x plus 20. RANSAC also labelled every one of the 55 points correctly, with the 40 edge points as inliers and the 15 stray points as outliers.

The page then describes the steps as a program. It starts with an empty set of best inliers. It repeats a loop for a set number of tries. Inside the loop, it chooses the sample size of points at random, without repeats, and makes the shape that passes exactly through them. If the shape could not be made, for example if the points are on one line, it continues with the next try. Otherwise, it finds every point whose distance to the shape is less than the distance limit. If this set of inliers is larger than the best inliers found so far, it updates the best inliers. After the loop finishes, it does a least-squares fit of the shape to the best inliers to get the final shape, and finds all points within the distance limit of this final shape to return as the final inliers.

Two common improvements to this loop are worth knowing. The first of them stops early. Once a new best try is found, it recomputes how many tries are needed from the share of inliers just found. The next part gives that formula, and you stop as soon as that many tries have been done. The second improvement, called MSAC, scores each try by how close the agreeing points are, not just how many there are, which breaks ties between guesses more sensibly.

The loop repeats a fixed number of times, so the next thing to work out is how many tries are enough. A try succeeds when every point in its sample is an inlier. So if a share w of the points are inliers and a sample has s points, one try succeeds with a chance of w multiplied by itself s times, written as w to the power of s. To be sure, with a chance p such as 99 percent, that at least one try succeeds, you need a specific number of tries. The number of tries equals the logarithm of one minus p, divided by the logarithm of one minus w to the power of s.

In the worked example, w is 40 out of 55, which is 0.727, and s is 2. So one try succeeds with a chance of 0.53. For 99 percent certainty the formula gives 6.1, which means 7 tries are enough, and yet the example used 100. That is common in practice, because a line or plane try is cheap, so people use a generous number.

A diagram plots this formula for a line, a plane, and a shape that needs 6 points, showing the tries needed for 99 percent success against the share of outliers on a logarithmic scale. The number of tries grows slowly at first and then very fast. For a plane with half the points as outliers, 35 tries are enough. But for a plane with 70 percent outliers, 168 tries are needed. A shape that needs 6 points, with half the points as outliers, needs 292 tries. This is why RANSAC works best for shapes defined by very few points. It is also why it slows down sharply when the wanted shape is only a small part of the scene.

The next part discusses planes and cylinders in a point cloud. A plane needs a sample of only 3 points, so it is one of the cheap shapes in the chart. This is also the shape a table-top robot wants most often. The plane through those 3 points has a normal, which is the direction at right angles to the plane. Because the three points make a triangle, that normal is equal to the cross product of two edges of the triangle. The cross product is a standard operation that gives the direction at right angles to two other directions. A point's distance from the plane is then the size of its offset from one of the three points, measured along the normal.

A diagram shows RANSAC running on a simulated depth camera view of a table with a box and a cup on it, plus 25 stray readings. It used 200 tries and a distance limit of 5 millimetres. The full point cloud has 1669 points. RANSAC separates the table plane's 1120 points from the 549 leftover points of the box, the cup, and the stray readings. The fitted plane's normal is minus 0.030, 0.020, 0.999. The table itself was made tilted with that exact normal, so the fit matches to three decimal places. The top of the box is also a plane, but it has only 90 points against the table's 1100 or so, so RANSAC keeps the table. The leftover points go to clustering, which splits them into one group per object.

A cylinder, such as a can, a bottle, or a pipe, is harder, because two or three positions alone do not fix its axis. The usual method therefore works out each point's normal first, meaning the direction the surface faces at that point, from a small plane fit to its nearest neighbours. Then a try picks 2 points together with their normals. Because both normals on a cylinder point straight away from the axis, the axis must run at right angles to both. That gives the axis direction, and from the axis direction the position and the radius follow. A point agrees if its distance from the axis is close to the radius and its normal points away from the axis. The Point Cloud Library does this with its cylinder model.

The final part of this section is about choosing the distance limit. Of the three things RANSAC needs from you, the distance limit is the one that matters most. A good start is two to three times the sensor's noise. For example, on a table half a metre away, a depth camera has about 1.5 millimetres of noise, so a limit of 4 to 5 millimetres is common.

If the limit is too small, real surface points count as outliers. So the inlier count of the true shape drops, and a wrong shape can win. If the limit is too large, points just above the surface count as inliers. On a table, that means the bottom few millimetres of every object are deleted along with the table. That matters most for flat objects such as a coin or a phone lying down, because those can vanish completely.

The fourth section covers where RANSAC is used on a robot arm. It is mostly a perception tool, but it also appears in calibration and sensing. First, removing the table. The classic table-top recipe finds the biggest plane, deletes it, and clusters what is left into objects. Second, finding walls and bin sides. A robot picking from a bin runs RANSAC several times to find the biggest plane like the bin floor, remove its points, find the next like a wall, and so on, until what is left is the parts. Third, fitting cylinders. A robot that picks cans, bottles, or pipes fits a cylinder to each cluster to get its axis and radius, and so the direction to approach it from. Fourth, finding a circle in a noisy rim. A glass's rim seen by a camera often has a few pixels from a reflection or from a neighbouring glass, so a circle RANSAC finds the rim first, and then least squares refines it. Fifth, matching features between pictures. When a camera on the arm moves, a program matches small patches between the old and the new picture. Some of those matches are wrong, so RANSAC finds the camera movement that most matches agree with. The same idea finds an object's pose from matched points. Sixth, calibration. When some checkerboard corners or marker detections in a calibration set are wrong, a RANSAC step can drop those poses before the final least-squares fit. Finally, checking a known table. When the table is fixed to the arm and measured once, RANSAC is not needed on every picture, but it can still run now and then to check that the camera has not been knocked.

The fifth section explains where it is useful, and where it is not. All of the uses above work for the same reason. The shape you want must be the biggest single shape in the data, and it must be defined by a few points. You must also be able to say how far from the shape a good point may be, because that distance is what decides which points agree. Once those conditions hold, it copes with half or more of the points being outliers, which almost nothing else does.

But once one of them fails, RANSAC fails with it. The page provides a table of what goes wrong, the signs you would see, and what to use instead. If the wanted shape is not the biggest, the answer might be too high or tilted. You should check the plane's normal and height against what you expect, restrict the search region, or use a known table plane. If there are two shapes of similar size, the answer flips between them from one picture to the next. You should find one, remove its points, find the other, and decide between them by a rule. If there are too few tries for the share of outliers, you get a different, wrong answer on some runs, so you should raise the number of tries using the formula. If the distance limit is too small, many real surface points are marked as outliers and the answer jumps around, so raise the limit to two or three times the noise. If the distance limit is too large, flat objects vanish with the table, so lower the limit. If the shape needs many points to define, it takes very many tries and is slow, so use a method that starts from a good guess, like iterative closest point, or a learned model. If you need the exact same answer every run, tiny differences make tests flaky, so fix the random seed or refit with least squares on the inliers. Finally, if no shape dominates, like in a cluttered pile of parts, you get low inlier counts for every try. In that case, use a point cloud model that labels each point, or use clustering first.

The sixth section lists libraries that provide it. You do not have to write the loop yourself, because RANSAC is built into every major point cloud and vision library. Open3D provides it for Python and C plus plus, returning the plane and the inlier indices, which is the usual table-removal call. The Point Cloud Library provides it for C plus plus, covering planes, lines, circles, spheres, and cylinders, as well as MSAC and other variants. OpenCV provides it for matched points between pictures, and newer versions offer improved methods. Scikit-learn wraps any regression model, which is good for lines and planes in NumPy arrays. Scikit-image provides general RANSAC with ready-made line, circle, and ellipse models. For a table plane in Python, the Open3D call is a single line. You call it with a distance threshold of 0.005, which is 5 millimetres written in metres, and 3 points per try.

The seventh section asks why RANSAC, and what it costs. RANSAC is a fitting method that tries many shapes, each one through a few random points, and keeps the shape that most points agree with. It gives the arm the right shape even when many of the points belong to other things, and it says which of the points belong to that shape.

The obvious alternative is least squares on all the points, perhaps after throwing away the points far from a first fit and fitting again. That works when only a few points are bad. But the first fit is already pulled towards the bad points, so it may throw away good points and keep bad ones. RANSAC never averages bad points in, so it works when a third or half of the points are bad.

A second alternative is a robust loss, which is a least-squares variant that counts large errors less. It is smooth and repeatable, and good when outliers are a small share. But it starts from a guess, so with many outliers it can settle on the wrong shape, while RANSAC needs no starting guess at all.

RANSAC costs you five things. First, it uses random choices, so two runs can give slightly different answers unless the seed is fixed. Second, it needs a distance limit, which means you must know the sensor's noise before you can set it. Third, it is slower than one least-squares fit because it makes many tries, and it gets very slow for shapes that need many points. Fourth, it finds only the biggest shape, which may not be the one you meant. Finally, it needs a least-squares refit at the end to get the best accuracy.

The eighth section discusses the learned alternative. There is an alternative that does not fit a shape at all. A segmentation model gives every point a name, such as mug, box, or table. So it replaces RANSAC when no single shape dominates the scene, such as a cluttered pile of parts. For shapes that need many points to define, a pose model finds the object's pose directly. But a point cloud model needs labelled 3D data, which is much scarcer than labelled photos. It also needs a graphics card for large clouds, and it knows only the kinds of object it was trained on. RANSAC is still the better choice for the table, the walls of a bin, and other simple shapes. This is because it needs no training, runs on an ordinary processor, and says exactly which points belong to the shape.
