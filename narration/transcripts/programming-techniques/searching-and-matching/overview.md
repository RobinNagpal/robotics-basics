Searching and matching. This chapter covers the techniques that find the closest thing, and decide which thing is which. Between them, they answer three questions that come up again and again in robot-arm software. First, which point, object or pose is nearest to this one? Second, where exactly is a known part, given a scan of it? And third, which of the objects seen now is which of the objects seen before? A fourth question comes up with camera pictures, which is, which small spots in one picture show the same thing as spots in another? 

This is for a listener who knows what a point cloud, a frame and a camera picture are, but who has not met these algorithms before. This page is the map of the chapter, and each technique then has its own page with a worked example, pseudocode and a list of libraries.

The first section explains what these techniques are for. Those four questions look different from each other, but this family of techniques does one job behind all of them. That job is finding the closest thing, and deciding which thing is which. 

A robot arm meets this job all the time, because a depth camera gives a cloud of tens of thousands of points. So, for each of those points, the software needs the few points next to it, to work out which way the surface faces. In the same way, a gripper tip moves over a table, and the software needs the object nearest to it. Or, a camera sees three mugs, for example, and the software needs to know which mug is the one it was already carrying.

The page has a diagram showing these three questions on one table, seen from above. The first panel finds the object nearest to the gripper. The second moves a stored outline of a bracket onto a scan of the real bracket. Finally, the third panel pairs each mug seen now with a mug seen one frame ago. All three panels share one idea, because in each of them you measure how far apart two things are with a number called a distance or a cost. Then you look for the smallest distance, or for the set of pairs whose distances add up to the smallest total.

The next part of the page covers the four techniques, divided into two groups. Since every one of those jobs comes down to a distance, the chapter has four technique pages. 

The first group holds the three most used techniques. Nearly every robot arm with a camera runs these, often many times a second, and the other techniques in this book depend on them too. First is nearest-neighbour search. This finds, for a query point, the closest point in a set, or the k closest, or all points within a radius. It uses a tree of boxes, called a k-d tree, so that it does not have to measure the distance to every point. Second is iterative closest point, or ICP. This moves a model of a part onto a scan of the part. It pairs each model point with its nearest scan point, moves the model to fit those pairs, and then repeats those two steps. Third is assignment and matching. This pairs up two lists, such as the mugs seen one frame ago and the mugs seen now, so that each thing gets at most one partner and the total cost is as small as possible. The Hungarian algorithm does this exactly, while a greedy method does it approximately. ICP can also get a first guess from 3D shape features, for when the part could be turned any way at all.

The second group holds a technique that is also used, but only on some robots. These are robots that must find objects with printing or texture on them from a camera picture. This technique is image features and matching. It finds corners in two pictures, describes the patch round each one as a list of numbers, pairs corners whose lists are alike, and keeps the pairs that agree on one movement of the object. It finds a known flat object, such as a label, and gives the pairs that a 3D pose calculation needs.

The page includes a table comparing the four techniques by what goes in, what comes out, and where they usually fail. For example, nearest-neighbour search takes a set of points and a query point, and outputs the closest points. It fails if nothing is near enough, or if the distance measurement does not match what similar means. Iterative closest point takes a model, a scan, and a first guess, and outputs the rotation and shift to line them up. It fails if the first guess is too far off, or if the part is symmetric. Assignment takes two lists and a cost for every pair, and outputs a one-to-one pairing, failing if the costs are wrong or if objects are closer together than the measurement error. Finally, image features and matching takes a stored picture and a camera picture, and outputs pairs of matching spots and their pose. This fails if the object has no texture or has a repeated pattern.

These four techniques build on each other. ICP runs a nearest-neighbour search in every round. Assignment often uses nearest-neighbour search to fill in its table of costs, or to throw away pairs that are clearly too far apart before it starts. Image feature matching pairs spots with a nearest-neighbour search among their descriptions, and its answer is often the first guess that ICP then makes exact.

The next section explains why the obvious method is not enough. Each of those four techniques exists to avoid a simpler method, because for every one of the questions there is an obvious method that is always right, which is to try every possibility. For the nearest point, that means measuring the distance to every point, and for the best pairing it means trying every possible way of pairing the objects up. 

Since a table with ten objects needs only ten distance checks, the obvious method is often the right choice when the numbers are small. The trouble is how fast the work grows as the numbers get bigger. A diagram shows this growth with two curves. The left panel matches every point of one scan to its nearest point in a second scan of the same size. Checking every pair needs nine million distance checks for three thousand points. However, using a k-d tree needs only forty-four thousand, four hundred and twenty-five checks, which is about fifteen per point. The right panel pairs up objects. Trying every pairing of twelve objects means trying four hundred and seventy-nine million, one thousand, six hundred pairings. Instead, the Hungarian algorithm needs roughly twelve times twelve times twelve, or one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight steps.

A depth camera with a 640 by 480 picture gives up to three hundred and seven thousand, two hundred points in each picture, and it gives thirty pictures a second. So checking every pair is not possible on a live camera, and the k-d tree is what makes point cloud work run at all.

The next part of the page covers how this chapter connects to the others. These techniques are used throughout the book, because searching and matching is the second of the seven families of techniques in it. 

First, geometry and cameras turns pixels and depth into 3D points in the arm's frame, and those points are what this chapter searches. ICP's answer is a rigid transform, which is the same rotation and shift that the geometry chapter explains. Its pose from points technique turns the pairs found by image feature matching into a 3D pose. Second, fitting and estimation uses nearest neighbours to find the points on which to fit a plane or a normal. It also supplies the Kalman filter, which predicts where a tracked object should be now, so that assignment can compare detections against the prediction rather than the old position. Its RANSAC algorithm throws away the wrong pairs in image feature matching and in ICP's global first guess. Third, image and point cloud processing uses radius search to grow clusters of points into objects. Finally, decisions and task logic uses the same greedy idea as greedy matching, and solves larger assignment problems with optimisation solvers.

Learned models do some of the same jobs. For example, a learned tracker can follow objects from frame to frame, and a learned pose model can find where a known part is without ICP. Even then, the programmed techniques stay in the pipeline, because a learned tracker still usually pairs its boxes with the Hungarian algorithm. A learned pose is also often finished off with a few rounds of ICP to make it more exact.
