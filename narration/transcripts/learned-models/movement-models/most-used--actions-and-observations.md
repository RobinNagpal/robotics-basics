Actions and observations. The three pages before this one described policies that copy a person, and all three of them read an observation and write an action. So this page is about those numbers themselves, and it opens both of them up. It shows what each number means, how it is written down, and why the choice matters. 

It answers six questions, and each one is a choice you have to make before you record anything. Should the action be joint angles or a position for the gripper? Should it be a place to go to, or a change from where the arm is now? How do you write down a turn of the gripper? Why must every number be rescaled before training starts? How fast should the policy run on the robot? And when can data recorded on one robot be used to train a policy for another?

It is for a reader who has read about behaviour cloning and who knows what a policy, an observation and an action are. These choices look like small details, but in practice they decide whether a policy trains at all, and whether someone else's recordings are any use to you. Before this page, it helps to have read about rigid transforms, which explains frames, rotation matrices and quaternions, because the sections on turning use all three.

The first section gives the main idea in one sentence. The introduction said that these choices decide whether a policy trains at all, and the reason is that a policy only ever sees lists of numbers. What each number means, and what size it is, decides what the policy can learn. For example, imagine you give a friend directions to your house. You could say to go to 12 Station Road, which is an address, and it works from anywhere. Or you could say to turn left, then walk 200 metres, which is a change from where they stand, so it only works if they start in the right place. You could also give the directions in miles to someone who thinks in kilometres. All three describe the same house, but some of them will get your friend lost. A policy has the same problem, because the same movement of the arm can be written down in several ways. The network has to learn from whichever way you chose. So some of those ways are easy for a network to learn, and some of them are not.

The next part explains what an observation is made of. Because a policy only sees lists of numbers, this section says which numbers those are on the way in. The observation is everything the policy is given at one moment, and for a robot arm it usually has three parts. 

First, camera pictures. Often one camera looks at the table from a fixed place, and one camera sits on the arm's wrist. The pictures are usually made smaller before they go in. Many policies use 224 by 224 pixels, while ACT uses 480 by 640. 

Second, the arm's own state. This is the angle of each joint and how open the gripper is, read from the arm's own sensors. People call this proprioception, which means the body's sense of its own position. Some policies also get the gripper's position in space, worked out from the joint angles. 

Third, there is sometimes more. This can be a sentence saying what task to do, as in vision-language-action models. It can be the last one or two moments, so that the policy can tell which way things are moving. And it can be force readings, for tasks with a lot of pressing.

Each picture goes through a seeing network first, which turns it into a list of numbers. So the rest of the policy then sees one long list of numbers, in which the joint angles, the gripper width and the picture numbers all sit side by side. This is why the rest of this page matters. Nothing in the list says that a number is in radians or in metres, because the network only ever sees values.

The action is what the policy gives back, and it has three parts: where the arm should move, how it should be turned, and how open the gripper should be. The next few sections go through those three parts one at a time.

The first part of the action is where the arm should move, and there are two common ways to say it. 

The first is joint angles, where the action is one target angle for each joint, so for a six-joint arm that is six numbers. ACT on ALOHA works this way, and so do most policies trained with cheap LeRobot arms. 

The second is the gripper pose, also called the end-effector pose. The end-effector is the tool at the end of the arm, which here is the gripper, and its pose is where it is, as three numbers for x, y and z, together with how it is turned. The action then says where the gripper should be, and ordinary code works out the joint angles that put it there. That code is called inverse kinematics, and is explained fully on the page about numerical inverse kinematics.

A diagram here shows why the choice between them matters. Two different arms with different link lengths put their gripper in exactly the same place, pointing straight down at a block. The script that drew the diagram worked out each arm's joint angles. The gripper pose is the same for both arms, which is 40 centimetres out, 10 centimetres up, pointing down. But the joint angles are not the same, because the first arm needs 59 degrees, minus 72 degrees and minus 77 degrees, while the second arm needs 71 degrees, minus 104 degrees and minus 56 degrees.

So the two ways of writing the action have different strengths. Joint angles need no extra code. They say exactly what each motor should do, so the arm can never be asked for a pose it cannot reach. They are the natural choice when the recording comes from a leader arm, which already records joint angles. But they only mean something on one kind of arm. Gripper pose, on the other hand, means the same thing on any arm, because putting the gripper 2 centimetres above the block makes sense for every robot. It is also closer to the task, since the task is about where the gripper goes. But it needs inverse kinematics underneath, and that can fail near the edge of the arm's reach.

The second choice is whether the action names a place to go to, or a change from here. An absolute action names a place, such as moving the gripper to x equals 42 centimetres, y equals 10 centimetres. The numbers are measured from a fixed point, which is usually the base of the robot. A relative action, also called a delta action, names a change from where the arm is now, such as moving the gripper 1 centimetre to the left. The word delta is the name of the Greek letter, which people use to mean a change in something.

A picture shows the same kind of recording made on two robots. On both of them, a person reached from a resting spot to a mug somewhere on the table. The only difference is that the second robot is bolted to the table in a different place. Each dot in the picture is one recorded moment. When the targets are absolute, measured from each robot's own base, the two robots' clouds of dots sit in different places. The middle of the first robot's cloud is at about 42 and 10 centimetres, and the middle of the second robot's is at about 56 and minus 16 centimetres. But when the same movements are written as the relative change from one moment to the next, both clouds are in the same place. For both robots, the average change is about 5 millimetres along x and about minus 7 to minus 8 millimetres along y.

This is the main reason people like relative actions. A network trained on the absolute numbers has to learn two separate things, one for each robot, whereas a network trained on the relative numbers learns one thing. So the recordings from the two robots help each other.

But relative actions have two costs of their own. First, small errors add up. Each change is added to where the arm is now, so if each step is a little short, then the arm ends up well short after a hundred steps. An absolute target does not drift in this way. Second, the change depends on the step length. One centimetre per step means 50 centimetres a second at 50 steps a second, and 10 centimetres a second at 10 steps a second. 

Because of this, many recent models use a middle way, in which the action chunk is written relative to the pose the gripper had at the start of the chunk, and not relative to the step before. Every target in the chunk then refers to the same starting point, so small errors do not add up inside the chunk.

The next choice is how a turn is written down. The action has to say how the gripper should be turned. A turn in 3D has three degrees of freedom, but there are several ways to write it as numbers. You can use three angles, often called roll, pitch and yaw, or Euler angles, which are easy for people to read. You can use a quaternion, which is four numbers that store the axis of the turn and how far it turns. You can use a rotation matrix, which is nine numbers. Or you can use the six-number form, which keeps only the first two columns of the rotation matrix, because the third column can always be worked out from the other two. This form was proposed for neural networks in 2019.

The problem is that some of these forms jump. A chart shows what happens to the numbers when the gripper turns smoothly from 150 degrees to 210 degrees about the vertical, with a small tilt. At 179 degrees the yaw number is 179, but at 181 degrees it is minus 179. So the gripper moved by 2 degrees, while the number moved by 358 degrees. The quaternion jumps as well, because many libraries keep its last number positive, so its z number flips from 0.996 to minus 0.996 at the same place. However, the six numbers change smoothly, by only about 0.03 each.

A jump like this is bad for a network, because a network gives outputs that change smoothly with its inputs. Near 180 degrees, the recorded answers are sometimes 179 and sometimes minus 179 for nearly the same picture. The network is trained to be close to both, so it learns their average, which is zero. That is a turn of zero degrees instead of 180 degrees, which leaves the gripper facing the wrong way. This is the same averaging problem that behaviour cloning suffers from, caused here only by how the number is written.

For this reason many policies output the six-number form, while people usually store rotations as quaternions and show them to people as three angles. Small changes of rotation from one step to the next are less of a problem, because a change of a few degrees stays far from the jump, so three angles are often used for relative actions.

The last number in the action is the gripper number, which says how open it should be. There are two common forms for that number. It can be a width, which is a number from fully closed to fully open, such as 0 to 8 centimetres, letting the policy close gently on something soft. Or it can be open or closed, which is one number that is 0 or 1, and the gripper's own controller then closes until it feels resistance. Many datasets use this binary form. But datasets disagree about which way round it goes. In some of them 1 means open, while in others 1 means closed. If you mix them without checking, the policy learns to open when it should close for half of its data. You must always check the gripper convention of every dataset you use.

The next section is about rescaling every number before training. This is worth a section of its own, because the numbers in one observation have very different sizes. A joint angle in radians is often between minus 3 and 3, while a wrist speed can be 5 or more. A gripper width in metres is between 0 and 0.08, and a change in position per step might be 0.002 metres. 

A diagram shows four such numbers from a made-up set of recordings. On their own scales, the gripper width and the change in x are so small that they almost vanish. But after normalising, every number has had its average taken off and has been divided by its spread. The spread here is the standard deviation, which is a measure of how far the values usually are from their average. After this, every number has an average of 0 and a spread of 1.

Normalising matters for two reasons, and both of them are about training. The first reason has to do with the loss. Training adds up the error in every number, so suppose the policy is wrong by one spread in each of the four numbers. Without normalising, the wrist speed makes up 94.6 percent of the loss, while the gripper width makes up 0.016 percent and the change in x makes up 0.0002 percent. So training works hard on the wrist speed and almost ignores the gripper, which is often the number that decides whether the task succeeds. The second reason has to do with the inside of the network. The layers of a network work best when their inputs are around minus 1 to 1, because very large or very small inputs make training slow or unstable.

There are two common ways to normalise a number. You can use the mean and spread, taking off the average and dividing by the standard deviation. Or you can stretch the values from smallest to largest, so that the smallest becomes minus 1 and the largest becomes 1. Diffusion and flow policies usually do this for actions, because their cleaning-up process assumes the answer lies in a fixed range.

The statistics come from the training data, and they must be saved with the policy, because the same numbers must be used when the policy runs on the robot. The policy's output is then turned back into real units with the same numbers, in reverse. The LeRobot library does all of this for you, keeping a file of statistics with the average, standard deviation, smallest and largest value of every number. Each policy's settings then say which of the two ways to use for pictures, for the arm's state and for actions.

The next section covers how often the policy decides, and chunks. The control rate is how many times a second the arm gets a new target, measured in hertz. ALOHA records at 50 hertz, the DROID dataset records at 15 hertz, and the Bridge dataset records at 5 hertz. The control rate is part of what an action means, and this matters most for relative actions. Say a recording at 50 hertz has the gripper move 2 millimetres each step, which is 10 centimetres a second. If a policy trained on it runs at 10 hertz and sends the same 2 millimetres each step, then the arm moves at 2 centimetres a second. It moves the right way at a fifth of the speed, and it may never reach the mug before the time runs out.

An action chunk is a list of the next few actions, predicted together. A chunk's length only means something together with the control rate. A chunk of 100 steps at 50 hertz covers 2 seconds, while a chunk of 16 steps at 10 hertz covers 1.6 seconds, and a chunk of 16 steps at 50 hertz covers only a third of a second. So when you use a policy or a dataset, write down three things together: the control rate, the chunk length, and how many steps of each chunk the arm plays before the policy is asked again.

To show how this all fits together, the page gives a worked example of one recorded moment. The arm is holding the gripper 40 centimetres out and 10 centimetres up, pointing straight down. At the next moment of the recording, the gripper has moved 1 centimetre towards the robot and turned 2 degrees. 

This single movement can be written in four ways. As absolute joint angles, it might be 61.19 degrees, minus 75.77 degrees, and minus 73.42 degrees. As relative joint angles, it is a change of plus 2.61 degrees, minus 4.23 degrees, and plus 3.63 degrees. As an absolute gripper pose, it is x equals 39 centimetres, y equals 10 centimetres, turned minus 88 degrees. And as a relative gripper pose, it is a change in x of minus 1 centimetre, a change in y of 0 centimetres, and a turn of plus 2 degrees. 

Notice that the relative gripper pose is the only one a person could read and understand at once. Also, a small, simple move of the gripper can need all three joints to change, by different amounts.

Now consider how that same moment's turn is written down. In 3D, a turn of 30 degrees about the vertical axis, written in the six-number form, is exactly 0.866, 0.5, 0, minus 0.5, 0.866, 0. But a network never outputs six numbers that fit together exactly. If it outputs numbers that are slightly off, the program tidies this into a real rotation in three steps. First, it makes the first three numbers length 1. Second, it removes from the second three numbers any part that points along the first, and then makes the rest length 1. Third, it works out the third column from the first two with a cross product, which gives the direction at right angles to both. The result is a turn of about 34.5 degrees about the vertical, which is close to what the network meant. But the same repair cannot be done for three angles that jumped by 358 degrees.

Last of all comes the rescaling. In the made-up recordings, the gripper width has an average of 0.0387 metres and a spread of 0.0326 metres. So an open gripper at 0.07 metres has the average subtracted and is divided by the spread, becoming 0.96. A change in x of 0.010 metres, with an average of 0.0020 metres and a spread of 0.0040 metres, becomes 2.0. So after rescaling both numbers are of an ordinary size, and the loss treats them fairly.

All these choices decide whether you can use data from another robot. Robot recordings are slow and costly to collect, so people want to train one policy on recordings from many different robots. This is called cross-embodiment training, where an embodiment is one kind of robot body. Whether it works depends almost entirely on the choices above.

The first large attempt was Open X-Embodiment in 2023, pooling recordings from 22 kinds of robot. The policies trained on it wrote every robot's action as the same seven numbers, but the numbers were not put into the same frame or the same scale for every robot. So the same seven numbers could mean different movements on different robots, and the policy had to guess which robot it was on from the pictures.

Octo, from 2024, normalised each dataset's actions with that dataset's own statistics, so that a large arm and a small arm gave numbers of the same size. It can also be fine-tuned for a robot with a different kind of action, such as joint angles, by giving it a new output part.

NVIDIA's GR00T N1.7, released in 2026, went further, because its actions are written relative to the gripper's current pose. A move of 3 centimetres to the left means the same thing on every body, while a target position does not. This choice let it learn from 20,000 hours of video of human hands, because a human hand moving 3 centimetres to the left is written the same way as a gripper moving 3 centimetres to the left.

Physical Intelligence's pi zero point seven takes another route. Each training recording carries a label that says whether its actions are joint angles or gripper poses. The model learns what the label means, so you can choose the kind of action when you run it.

The pattern is the same in all four models. Data from another robot helps only when its numbers mean the same thing as yours, or when the model is told what they mean. So for any dataset, you must ask if the actions are joint angles or gripper poses, absolute or relative, which rotation form they use, which way round the gripper number is, what the control rate is, and how the numbers were normalised.

When things go wrong, the fault usually follows from one of these choices. 
If the arm moves at the wrong speed, going the right way but too slowly or too fast, the usual cause is relative actions played at a different control rate from the recording. 
If the gripper spins suddenly by nearly a full turn, the usual cause is three angles or a quaternion with a jump in it near 180 degrees. 
If the gripper opens when it should close, the usual cause is two datasets with opposite gripper conventions. 
If the arm ignores the gripper or moves only one joint, the usual cause is numbers that were not normalised, so the loss was dominated by one large number, or the statistics saved with the policy do not match the ones used in training. 
If the arm drifts further and further from where it should be, this is because relative actions add up small errors. 
If the arm stops at the edge of its reach or jerks, it is because gripper-pose actions need inverse kinematics, which can fail near the edge of the reach. 
And if mixed data makes the policy worse, it happens because the datasets' numbers mean different things.

Several libraries and models handle these choices for you. LeRobot stores recordings in one standard format, lists numbers by name, keeps their statistics, and handles normalising. Octo normalises each dataset separately. Open X-Embodiment stores many robots' recordings, but you must read each dataset's description before mixing them. GR00T writes its actions relative to the gripper's current pose. SciPy has a rotation class that converts between forms, and the Universal Manipulation Interface records demonstrations with a handheld gripper, writing actions relative to the gripper's current pose so the recordings can train policies for different arms.

Weighing all these choices together, choosing them well makes a policy train faster and more reliably, and decides whether other people's recordings can help you. The obvious alternative is to store whatever numbers your robot happens to produce, which is usually absolute joint angles in the robot's own units at its own rate. This is the right choice for a single robot trained only on its own data, because it is simple and nothing needs converting. But the other choices start to pay off when you want to mix data from more than one robot, or from human video, or to reuse a model someone else trained. Then relative gripper-pose actions, the six-number rotation form, and careful normalising are what make the numbers mean the same thing everywhere. 

The costs are that gripper-pose actions need inverse kinematics, relative actions drift and tie the data to one control rate, converting a dataset requires reading its conventions carefully to avoid silent errors, and the statistics must travel with the policy so the outputs are the right size.

There is no written alternative for this page, because it describes the numbers that every policy reads and writes, rather than a model that does a job. Written code makes the same choices, using rigid transforms and numerical inverse kinematics. The difference is only that a programmer can handle a jumping number with a special case, but a neural network cannot, because it just learns the average of the two sides.

The final section shows how to read these choices out of a dataset using Python, which is how you find out what somebody else's recordings actually contain before you train on them. Using the LeRobot library, you can download just the metadata of a dataset, which takes only a few seconds even for a massive dataset. You can print the frames per second, the robot type, and the action names. The action names tell you whether the dataset uses joint angles, with names like shoulder pan position, or a gripper pose, with names like x, y, z and a rotation. You can print the shape to see how many numbers there are per action, which catches mismatches when mixing robots. And you can print the statistics, which hold the minimum, maximum, mean and standard deviation of every signal, exactly what is needed for rescaling.

You can also load action chunks by giving the dataset a list of time offsets in seconds. The dataset returns that many frames stacked together instead of one. You divide by the frames per second to turn a count of frames into seconds. You rarely write this by hand for a packaged policy, but writing it yourself is how you load a chunk for a network of your own.

LeRobot gives you a file format that carries its own meaning, so a dataset is self-describing and the statistics travel with it. What you have to decide is what your own recordings will contain, and that decision is made once and is very hard to change. If you record joint angles, your data is tied to your arm's geometry. If you record gripper poses, you need a working inverse kinematics solver and you have chosen a turn convention. If you record changes rather than absolute targets, small errors add up over a chunk. None of these can be converted afterwards without the full calibration and kinematics of the robot that recorded them. So the honest advice is to read these fields on any dataset you are offered, and to expect that a dataset recorded for another robot needs work you can measure in weeks rather than an afternoon.
