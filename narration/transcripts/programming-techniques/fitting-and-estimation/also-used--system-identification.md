System identification: measuring the numbers a model needs. 

This page explains system identification, which means finding the numbers inside a physical model of the arm from measurements of the arm itself. Examples include a joint's friction, the mass of the part in the gripper, and the stiffness of a springy finger. The page answers five questions about numbers like these. How do you move the arm so that the data can tell the numbers apart? How do you fit them? How do you follow a number that drifts over time? How sure can you be of each number? And which end of that range should the arm act on? 

It is for a reader who has read the page on least-squares fitting, because that page explains parameters, residuals, and the least-squares solve, and this page uses all three. The part on drifting numbers also uses one idea from the Kalman filter page. Every number on this page comes from a real run of a diagram script.

The first section explains what this page answers. Each of those five questions matters because a controller, a planner, or a simulator uses a model of the arm. A model here means a set of equations, such as saying the torque a joint needs is its friction plus the weight it lifts. Those equations have numbers in them, like a friction value, a mass, a distance, or a stiffness. The equations themselves come from physics and are usually right, but the numbers in them are usually not known well. 

A datasheet gives a typical value, not the exact value for your arm, because friction changes from one gearbox to the next and falls as the gearbox warms up. The mass of the part in the gripper changes with every pick, and a rubber finger pad gets softer with wear. 

So, system identification measures these numbers on the real arm rather than trusting the datasheet. You move the arm in a planned way, record what happens, and fit the numbers so that the model's equations match the recording. The result is a model that describes your arm today, plus an error bar that says how far each number might be off.

The next part gives the idea in one sentence. Move the arm so that each unknown number has a visible effect, record the result, fit the numbers by least squares, and state how sure you are of each one. 

That one sentence covers the whole method, and an everyday example shows why each part of it is needed. Suppose you want to know how much a suitcase weighs and how much the wheels drag, but you have only a luggage scale that measures how hard you pull. If you pull it at one steady walking speed, you learn only the total pull. This means you cannot say how much of it is drag that grows with speed, and how much is a fixed amount of rubbing. That is why you pull it slowly, then quickly, then slowly the other way, so the two effects show up differently. Because they show up differently, you can separate them. Choosing those different speeds is the probe move, and working out the two numbers from the pulls is the fit.

The page then explains how it works, step by step. Since the probe and the fit are easier to follow on a real example, the worked example uses one joint of a small arm. That joint needs torque to overcome friction, where torque is the turning force a motor gives, measured in newton-metres. Most arm motors report their torque, or a current that is proportional to it.

The first step is to write the model so the numbers sit on their own. A common model of joint friction has two parts, and each part has its own number. First, viscous friction grows in step with speed, like stirring honey. Its number is called b, measured in newton-metres per radian per second. Second, Coulomb friction is a fixed amount of rubbing that always opposes the motion, whatever the speed. Its number is called c, measured in newton-metres. It is named after Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, who studied rubbing surfaces. 

Written out, the torque at a steady speed w, in radians per second, is b times w, plus c times the sign of w. Here, the sign of w is plus one when the joint turns one way and minus one when it turns the other way. 

The important point is that the unknowns b and c are each multiplied by something you know. So, each reading gives one row of a linear equation. A known row made of w and the sign of w, multiplied by the unknown pair b and c, gives the measured torque. Least squares solves exactly this kind of equation, and many physical models can be written this way, even when the physics is bent. For example, a payload's weight enters an arm's torque as the mass times a known function of the joint angles. This is explained more fully on the page about arm dynamics, which shows the full equations for a whole arm, where the rows that multiply the unknowns are often called the regressor.

Once the model is written that way, the next choice is the design of the probe move, which is the motion you run to collect the data. People call the quality of a probe move its excitation, meaning how strongly it makes each unknown number show up in the data. 

The simulated joint has true values of b equals 0.25 and c equals 0.40, and its torque readings scatter by 0.05 newton-metres, which is the sensor noise. A diagram on the page compares two probe moves of forty readings each. It shows a rich probe that holds eight speeds in both directions, a poor probe that holds speeds near 1 radian per second in one direction, and the fitted friction values from 300 repeats of each. 

The rich probe holds eight speeds, from minus 2 to plus 2 radians per second, taking five readings at each. The poor probe, however, holds speeds between 0.9 and 1.1 radians per second, all in one direction. When each probe was repeated 300 times with fresh noise, each repeat gave one fitted pair of b and c. 

The rich probe's answers sit in a small cloud around the true values, and its fitted b ranged from 0.204 to 0.294 over the 300 repeats. However, the poor probe's answers lie along a long thin line. Its fitted b ranged from minus 0.025 to 0.589, and one answer even had negative friction, which is not possible. 

The reason is simple, because at a speed of about 1 radian per second, the torque is about b plus c. As a result, the poor probe measures that sum very well, but it cannot tell how the sum splits between b and c. Every point along the line gives nearly the same sum. The rich probe, however, includes slow and fast speeds, so a change in b and a change in c change the torques in different ways. 

A number called the condition number measures this, and it compares how well the best-measured and the worst-measured combination of the unknowns are pinned down. A value near 1 is ideal, so the rich probe's rows, with a condition number of 4.9, are much better than the poor probe's 28.3. If the poor probe held exactly one speed, the condition number would be infinite and the fit would have no single answer. 

So, the rules for a good probe follow from all of this. First, make each unknown change the readings in its own way. For friction, use slow and fast speeds, in both directions. Second, cover the range the arm will use, because a number fitted from slow moves may be wrong for fast ones. Third, keep the probe safe by staying inside the joint limits, the speed limits, and the workspace, and start gently. Finally, check the condition number of the rows before running the probe on the arm. It needs only the planned speeds, not the readings.

The next step is to fit by least squares. Once the probe has been run, the rows and the readings are in hand, so the fit is one least-squares solve, as the least-squares page explains. One real run of the rich probe gave readings such as minus 0.940 newton-metres at minus 2 radians per second, 0.489 newton-metres at 0.5 radians per second, and 0.880 newton-metres at 2 radians per second. 

The fit gave b equals 0.2285 newton-metres per radian per second, against a true value of 0.25, and c equals 0.4205 newton-metres, against a true value of 0.40. The residuals, which are the gaps between each reading and the fitted curve, have a spread of 0.043 newton-metres. This is close to the sensor's true noise of 0.05 newton-metres. That is a sign that the model has the right shape, because nothing is left over except noise. 

A diagram shows the forty torque readings at eight speeds, the fitted friction curve with a jump at zero speed, and a narrow band showing how sure the fit is. The slope of each half of the curve is b, and the jump where the speed crosses zero is two times c, because the rubbing changes direction. The pale band around the curve shows two spreads of the fit's uncertainty, and the next part explains where that band comes from.

That band comes from an error bar on each fitted number. An error bar is the spread of a fitted number, meaning how far it would move if you ran the same probe again with fresh noise. That is why it comes almost free with a least-squares fit, in three steps. 

First, estimate the sensor's noise from the residuals. Square them, add them up, and divide by the number of readings minus the number of unknowns. Here that is 40 minus 2, which is 38. The square root is the 0.043 newton-metres mentioned earlier. Second, multiply that squared noise by the inverse of the table of rows transposed and multiplied by itself. The result is the covariance of the fitted numbers. This is a small table that gives each number's squared spread and how the numbers' errors are linked. Third, the square root of each number on the diagonal of the covariance is that number's error bar. 

For the run above, this gives b equals 0.229 plus or minus 0.012, and c equals 0.421 plus or minus 0.017. Both true values lie within two error bars of the fit, which is what an error bar of one spread should do most of the time. 

You can check the formula against the repeats in the probe picture. There, the 300 fitted values of b had a spread of 0.0148, and the formula predicts 0.0141. For the poor probe, the repeats gave 0.1100 and the formula 0.1118. So the formula is trustworthy, and it also tells you in advance that the poor probe is eight times less sure. 

The covariance also shows a link between the two numbers, because the errors of b and c have a correlation of minus 0.91. In other words, when b comes out too high, c usually comes out too low, and this matters when you use both numbers together. For example, the torque the joint needs at 1.5 radians per second is 1.5 times b plus c, which equals 0.763 newton-metres. Its error bar, worked out with the link, is only 0.0075 newton-metres. However, if you ignore the link and treat the two errors as separate, you get 0.025 newton-metres, which is more than three times too cautious. This is because the fit knows the total torque at the speeds it measured far better than it knows either number alone.

The next part explains carrying error bars through a formula, and a Monte Carlo check. The number you need is often not the fitted number itself, but something worked out from it. Carrying an error bar through a formula is called error propagation. 

For example, the arm holds a part still with its forearm level, and the elbow motor reports an extra torque of 1.20 newton-metres, with a spread of 0.04 newton-metres. The part's centre sits 0.150 metres from the elbow axis, with a spread of 0.006 metres, because the grasp is never in exactly the same place. The part's mass is the torque divided by gravity times the distance. That is 1.20 divided by 9.81 times 0.150, which equals 0.815 kilograms. 

For a formula made only of multiplying and dividing, there is a simple rule: the relative spreads, meaning the spread divided by the value, add in squares. For the torque, 0.04 divided by 1.20 is 0.033. For the distance, 0.006 divided by 0.150 is 0.040. For the mass, you take the square root of 0.033 squared plus 0.040 squared, which gives 0.052. As a result, the mass is 0.815 kilograms with a spread of 5.2 percent, which is 0.043 kilograms. Notice that the distance, not the torque, is the larger source of doubt, so to improve the answer you should measure the grasp position better rather than the torque. 

The rule is exact only for a straight-line formula, and a division is not straight. A Monte Carlo check therefore tests it by brute force. You draw many random torques and distances, each from its own bell curve, work out the mass for every pair, and look at the spread of the results. The name comes from the casino in Monaco, because the method runs on random numbers. 

A diagram shows a histogram of 20,000 Monte Carlo masses, the bell curve the formula predicts on top of it, the best value of 0.815 kilograms, and the cautious end of 0.900 kilograms. The 20,000 samples have an average of 0.817 kilograms and a spread of 0.0427 kilograms, against 0.815 kilograms and 0.0425 kilograms from the rule. The bell curve from the rule sits on top of the histogram, so the rule is good enough here. If the two had disagreed, for example because a spread was large compared with its value, you would trust the Monte Carlo result.

The next step is to act on the cautious end. Whether the error bar comes from the formula or from Monte Carlo, a fitted number with an error bar is a range, not a point. The arm still has to make one decision, so the safe habit is to act on the end of the range that fails gently if you are wrong. That end of the range is called the cautious end. 

For the payload, a heavier part is the dangerous case, because it needs more torque to stop, takes longer to brake, and puts more load on the gripper. So the planner should use the upper end, which with two spreads is 0.815 plus two times 0.043, giving 0.900 kilograms. In the Monte Carlo run, only 3.1 percent of the samples lay above 0.900 kilograms. 

Which end is cautious depends on the decision, not on the number. First, for a payload mass used in a speed limit or a lift check, use the upper end. Second, for friction used in feed-forward control, use the lower end of c. Feed-forward means adding the torque the model says friction will take, so the controller does not have to wait for an error to build up. Too much added torque makes the joint push itself when it should stop, or shake around the target. Finally, for the stiffness of a springy finger used to limit squeezing force, the force is the stiffness times the squeeze distance. To stay under a force limit, work out the distance from the upper end of the stiffness. 

When the cautious end is too costly, for example when the upper-end payload is over the arm's limit but the best value is not, the answer is to measure better. That is why you should run a longer probe or measure the grasp position, rather than hoping.

The page then covers numbers that drift, using recursive least squares with forgetting. The cautious end handles a number you have measured once, but some numbers change while the arm works. For example, a gearbox's friction falls as it warms up over the first twenty minutes. Running the probe again and again stops the arm's real work. Instead, the arm can refine the numbers from its normal moves, one reading at a time. 

Recursive least squares does this, because it keeps the current fitted numbers and a covariance table P, like a Kalman filter. Each new reading nudges the numbers towards it, by an amount that depends on P. With no forgetting, the answer after all the readings is the same as a batch least-squares fit over all of them. The Kalman filter page explains the same predict-and-correct idea; recursive least squares is that filter for numbers that are meant to stay constant. 

However, a number that stays constant is the wrong assumption for friction that drifts, because plain recursive least squares weighs a reading from twenty minutes ago as much as the latest one. A forgetting factor, written as the Greek letter lambda, fixes this, and it is a number just below 1. At each step, all older readings count lambda times as much as before, so the filter then remembers roughly the last 1 divided by 1 minus lambda readings. For example, a factor of 0.98 remembers about 50 readings. 

A diagram shows recursive least squares estimates of viscous friction over 600 readings for forgetting factors of 1, 0.98, and 0.90, against a true value that falls from 0.30 to 0.20. The filter runs on 600 readings, one every 2 seconds, taken during normal moves at speeds between 0.3 and 2 radians per second in both directions. The true b is 0.30 for the first 150 readings, falls steadily to 0.20 by reading 450, and then stays there. 

The three settings behave very differently. First, a factor of 1 never forgets. It is smooth, but it trails far behind. At reading 600 it says 0.258, while the true value is 0.200. Over the last 150 readings it is 0.063 too high on average. Second, a factor of 0.98 follows the fall with a short lag. At reading 600 it says 0.207. Over the last 150 readings it is 0.017 too high on average, with a shake of 0.010. Finally, a factor of 0.90 remembers only about 10 readings. It follows fastest, but it shakes, showing a spread of 0.022 over the last 150 readings, which is more than twice that of 0.98. 

This is the same trade as the process noise q in a Kalman filter, because forgetting fast follows changes but passes on more noise. So choose lambda from how quickly the real number can change compared with how often readings arrive.

The steps above fit into a few short functions, which the page shows as pseudocode. The code defines three functions. The first function does a batch fit with error bars. It takes the rows and readings, solves for the numbers using matrix multiplication and transposition, calculates the residuals and the noise squared, and then finds the covariance and error bars before returning them. The second function performs one step of recursive least squares. It takes the current numbers, the covariance table P, a new row and reading, and the forgetting factor lambda. It predicts the reading, calculates a gain, updates the numbers based on the prediction error, and updates the covariance table P, dividing by lambda to apply the forgetting factor. The third function calculates the Monte Carlo spread. It takes a formula, inputs with spreads, and a count. It loops for that count, drawing random inputs from bell curves, applying the formula, and returning the average and spread of the results. To start recursive least squares, you set the numbers to a rough guess and make P large, such as 100 times the identity table, which says you know almost nothing yet.

The fourth section explains where this is used on a robot arm. Once the steps are clear, it is easier to see that system identification appears wherever a model's numbers decide how the arm moves or what it believes. 

First, it is used for joint friction for feed-forward. Friction numbers let a PID controller add the torque friction will take, so a slow move does not stick and then jump. Second, it is used for payload identification after a grasp. The arm holds the part still in two or three poses and fits its mass and the position of its centre from the joint torques. A mass that is far from the expected one means the wrong part, or two parts, or a slipping grasp. Third, it finds the full arm's masses for a model-based controller. Each link's mass, centre, and inertia enter the equations of arm dynamics linearly, so a rich probe that moves all joints together can fit them all at once. 

Fourth, it is used in collision detection. A monitor compares the torque the model expects with the torque the motors report, and stops the arm when the gap is too large. A poorly identified model needs a wide margin, which makes the monitor slow to notice a real collision. Fifth, it measures the stiffness of a springy finger or a force-controlled contact. Pressing a finger or a tool on a known surface at a few depths and fitting force against depth gives the stiffness that impedance and force control needs. 

Sixth, it measures contact and friction with the table. A robot that pushes objects can fit the table's friction from push after push, and track it with recursive least squares. Seventh, it makes a simulator match the real arm. Fitting a simulator's masses, friction, and motor delays to recordings from the real arm narrows the gap between simulation and reality. Finally, it is used for camera placement. Finding where a camera sits on the arm is also identification. It uses a geometric model with unknown numbers, fitted from a probe of many arm poses, and the same rule applies: vary the poses so every unknown shows up.

The next section covers where it is useful, and where it is not. In all of those places, system identification works best when the physics is known and only the numbers are not, when the numbers can be written so they multiply known quantities, and when the arm can be moved safely through a probe. 

However, a table lists the ways it goes wrong, giving the cause, the sign you would see, and what people use instead. If the probe does not excite every number, you will see huge error bars, numbers that change a lot between runs, or impossible values like negative friction. Instead, you should use a richer probe and check the condition number before running it. If the model is missing an effect, like sticking at very low speed, the residuals will show a pattern rather than random scatter, and their spread will be larger than the sensor's noise. You should add the missing term, fit only the speed range the model describes, or add a learned correction. 

If speed and torque readings are out of step in time, the fit will change when the probe changes speed quickly, and you will see hysteresis loops in a torque-speed plot. You must line the readings up in time first. If you use forgetting while the arm stands still, the covariance P will grow every step, and the numbers will jump on the first move after a pause. You should update only while the arm moves enough, or put a ceiling on P. 

If the forgetting factor is too small, the numbers will shake from reading to reading. You should raise lambda, or run a slower filter on the numbers. If there are rare large readings, like a bump or a missed sample, one reading will move the fit a long way. You should throw away readings with a large residual. If the number depends on something you did not vary, like temperature or load, the model will fit the probe well but the real task badly. You should probe under the task's conditions, or track the number with recursive least squares. Finally, if the physics is too complex to write down, like cloth or a tangled cable, no small set of numbers will fit. In that case, you need a learned model.

The next part lists libraries that provide it. Since those failures are easier to avoid with tested code, it helps that for a model written as rows times unknowns, the fit is one call to a least-squares routine. Several libraries cover the fit, the full-arm rows, the error bars, and the drift. 

In Python, NumPy provides functions for the fit, the covariance, and the condition number. SciPy offers curve fitting that returns the covariance and handles models that are not just rows times unknowns. The uncertainties library carries error bars through a formula automatically using the straight-line rule, and the padasip library provides recursive least squares with a forgetting factor. For C++ and Python, Pinocchio builds the rows for a whole arm's masses and inertias from its description file. MuJoCo has a system identification toolbox in Python that fits a simulator's parameters to recorded trajectories. MATLAB offers a whole System Identification Toolbox, which is a long-standing commercial tool strong on probe design and model checking. Recursive least squares itself is about ten lines, and many projects write it themselves, as in the pseudocode earlier.

The seventh section explains why system identification is used, and what it costs. System identification measures the numbers in a physical model of the arm by running a planned probe, fitting the numbers by least squares, and stating an error bar for each. It gives the arm a model that matches this arm today, and a range that says how far to trust it. 

The obvious alternative is to use the datasheet or the design files, which costs nothing and needs no arm time. However, a datasheet gives a typical value, and friction in particular varies from unit to unit and with temperature by tens of percent. A payload's mass and grasp position are not in any datasheet at all, and a datasheet value comes with no error bar, so the arm cannot tell whether it is safe to act on. 

A second alternative, used for simulators, is domain randomisation. This means training across many random guesses of the numbers so that the real arm falls somewhere inside. It avoids measuring, but wide random ranges make the result more cautious than it needs to be. As a result, identifying the numbers first lets you randomise only over the real error bars. 

The costs are these: you need a model with the right terms, and the fit cannot find an effect the model leaves out. The probe takes time on the real arm, and it must be designed to be safe as well as rich. The numbers go stale as the arm wears, warms, or picks up a new tool, so you must re-run the probe or track the numbers with recursive least squares. And a tracker with forgetting must be guarded, because it can drift or jump when the arm stands still.

The next section covers the learned alternative. Those costs point to an alternative, because learned arm models use networks that learn how the arm's body behaves from its own recordings. A network can learn the whole model and capture effects no textbook equation has. However, it needs far more data, its internal numbers have no physical meaning you can check, and it can behave oddly in movements it has not seen. 

The usual choice is residual learning. This means you keep the identified physics model and train a small network only on what it gets wrong, such as friction that changes with speed and temperature, or a cable that pulls differently in each pose. System identification is the right first step, and often enough on its own, so you add the learned correction only when the residuals still show a pattern after the missing terms are in the model. When the leftover error depends on only a few numbers, such as a joint's speed and temperature, Gaussian processes can learn that correction from tens to hundreds of samples and say how sure they are, and linear regression can fit it with the same least squares this page uses. Learned dynamics models use the same idea for objects the arm pushes.
