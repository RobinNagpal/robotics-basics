Pose from points: Perspective-n-Point. 

This page explains how a program finds an object's full pose from one colour picture. The object's pose is where it is and which way it is turned, meaning three numbers for its position and three for its rotation, making six in all. Robotics people call this a six degrees of freedom pose. The method on this page is called Perspective-n-Point, or PnP. It answers four questions: what does PnP need, how does it find the pose, what happens when some of its inputs are wrong, and why is a flat printed marker hard to read when it faces the camera squarely. 

This follows the pages on the pinhole camera model, which turns a point into a pixel, rigid transforms, which describe a pose as a rotation and a shift, and calibration, which already uses PnP once to find the board in each picture. Every number here comes from a real run with a camera of 320 by 240 pixels, a focal length of 277.1 in both directions, and an optical centre at pixel 160, 120.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. If you know where some points are on an object, and you can see which pixels they land on, there is only one pose of the object that puts all of them on those pixels. 

For an everyday example, imagine you are lost in a town, but you can see three landmarks you know, like a church tower, a bridge, and a tall chimney. You know where each one is on your map, and you can see in which direction each one lies from where you stand. Only one spot on the map gives those three directions at once, so that spot is where you are. Sailors call this taking a fix. 

PnP is the same idea. The map is the object's own shape, written as points in millimetres. The directions are the pixels, because each pixel is a direction from the camera, as the pinhole camera model page shows. The answer is the object's pose relative to the camera. Turned around, it is also the camera's pose relative to the object, which is exactly what calibration needs.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step, starting with what goes in and what comes out. PnP needs three things before it can start. First, it needs the object's points, in the object's own frame. For example, the corners of a 6 centimetre cube measured from the middle of its base. These come from a drawing, a computer-aided design model, or a printed pattern. Second, it needs the pixel where each of those points appears. Something else finds these, like a corner detector or a neural network, because PnP does not look at the picture at all. Finally, it needs the camera's lens numbers and its distortion, which calibration measures. 

Each point must be paired with its own pixel, and that pairing is called a correspondence. PnP trusts the pairing completely. What comes out is a rotation and a shift, which together are the transform from the object to the camera. Rigid transforms then carry it into the arm's base frame.

The page then returns to reprojection error. Suppose you guess a pose. With that guess, you can place each object point in the camera's frame and then project it to a pixel. The distance between where a point reprojects and where it was really seen is its reprojection error in pixels. PnP is a search for the pose with the smallest reprojection error. It combines the errors of all the points into one number, the root mean square. It squares each distance, takes the mean, and takes the square root. When the pose is right, this number is about as large as the noise in the pixels, usually a few tenths of a pixel.

Because the projection formula divides by depth, the error is not a simple straight-line function of the pose. So PnP works in two stages. First, a short formula gives an approximate pose directly. The simplest one is the direct linear transform, or DLT. It treats the twelve numbers of the transform matrix as unknowns, writes two straight-line equations for each point, and solves them all at once. It needs six or more points that are not all on one plane. Better formulas exist, such as EPnP, which needs only four points and is very fast, and P3P, which uses exactly three. 

In the second stage, the program refines the guess. It nudges all six pose numbers at once in the direction that shrinks the error fastest, and repeats until the error stops falling. The usual method is Levenberg–Marquardt, which is fast because there are only six unknowns. The first guess matters, because refinement only walks downhill from where it starts, and can settle in the wrong valley if the start is poor.

The page shows a worked run using a red 6 centimetre cube standing on a table, turned 20 degrees. The camera is looking down at an angle, and can see six corners of the cube. The diagram shows what happens when the refinement is given a deliberately poor start, with the cube in the middle of the table and not turned. On the left, an orange cube shows where the first guess puts the corners, and red lines show the reprojection errors, which start at about 50 pixels. On the right, after the refinement, every orange dot sits perfectly on its target. A table tracks the error falling step by step. It starts at 50.46 pixels, drops to 5.25 in the first step, then to 0.43, and settles at 0.30 pixels by the third step. The final pose was 3.3 millimetres and 0.51 degrees from the truth. Since one pixel covers 1.7 millimetres at that distance, 3.3 millimetres is about what 0.3 pixels of noise on six points allows. For comparison, the DLT first guess on the same six points gave an error of 2.8 pixels, and was off by 5.1 millimetres and 4.6 degrees. So the DLT is a good place to start, but not a place to stop.

Next, the page explains how many points are needed. A pose has six unknown numbers, and each point gives two measurements, its horizontal and vertical pixel coordinates. So three points give six measurements, which is just enough, but they can fit up to four different poses. A fourth point is needed to choose between them. In practice, three points give up to four answers, which is used inside RANSAC. Four points give one answer if they are well spread, like the four corners of a square marker. Six or more points make the answer steady because the noise averages out. Spread matters as much as count, because points that are close together give a poor pose. The object should also fill a fair part of the picture.

The section ends with pseudocode for the whole process. It takes the object points, the pixels, and the camera calibration. First, it undistorts the pixels. Then it gets a first guess for the rotation and shift using a method like DLT, EPnP, or P3P. Next, it loops up to 30 times, projecting the points, finding the errors, and using Levenberg-Marquardt to find the step that shrinks the squared errors fastest. It applies that step, and stops early if the error stops falling. Finally, it checks the root mean square error. If it is greater than about 2 pixels, it reports failure. Otherwise, it returns the pose and the error.

The third section covers PnP inside RANSAC, for when some matches are wrong. The previous section assumed every pairing of a point with a pixel is correct. In real pictures, a feature matcher might pair a pattern on one side of a box with a similar pattern on the other side. One wrong pairing can pull the whole pose away, because refinement tries to satisfy every pairing at once. 

RANSAC, which stands for random sample consensus, is the standard cure. The page on RANSAC explains it for lines and planes, and for PnP it works like this. First, pick a few pairings at random, such as 3 or 4. Second, find the pose that those few imply. Third, reproject every object point with that pose, and count the pairings whose reprojection error is under a limit, such as 3 pixels. These are the inliers that agree. Fourth, repeat this many times and keep the pose with the most inliers. Finally, refine that pose using only its inliers. 

The page shows a test on a printed box with 40 matched features, where 12 of them, or 30 percent, were replaced with random wrong places. The diagram shows the true box in grey. On the left, refinement on all 40 pairings produced an orange box that is about 50 millimetres and 47 degrees away from the truth, making it useless. On the right, RANSAC found exactly the 28 right pairings. It caught all 12 wrong ones, and the final pose was just 0.4 millimetres and 0.1 degrees from the truth. 

The page explains how many tries are enough. With 70 percent right pairings, drawing 6 points gives a clean draw about 12 percent of the time. To be 99 percent sure of at least one clean draw, you need about 37 tries. Drawing 4 points succeeds more often, needing only about 17 tries, which is why real libraries use four-point or three-point solvers inside RANSAC.

The fourth section is about a flat target, and why a face-on view is unstable. Many robot cells find objects with a fiducial marker, which is a printed black-and-white square like an ArUco or AprilTag marker. The four corners of the square are exactly a PnP problem with four points on one plane. This is enough in theory, but in practice, when the marker is nearly face-on to the camera, two different poses fit the corners almost equally well. One is tilted a little towards the camera, and the other is tilted the same amount away. 

A diagram shows a 40 millimetre marker tilted 12 degrees one way or the other. Seen from the side, they face clearly different ways, 24 degrees apart. But the four corners they put in the picture differ by at most 1.27 pixels. At 5 degrees of tilt, the gap is only half a pixel, so a corner detector with normal noise cannot reliably tell the two apart. This happens because of perspective. When tilted, the near edge looks a little longer than the far edge, and that small difference is the only clue to which way it tilts. 

A chart and table show how wrong the facing direction comes out at different tilts. Face-on, the facing direction is typically 7.4 degrees wrong, and the solver often chooses the completely mirrored pose. At 30 degrees of tilt, the error drops to 1.3 degrees, and the solver never chooses the wrong mirror pose. The distance to the marker, however, is always accurate to about 2 millimetres. So a small, face-on marker gives a good position and a poor rotation. 

To fix this in practice, you can tilt the camera or the marker so it is seen at 20 to 45 degrees. You can make the marker bigger in the picture, or use several markers spread apart. You can use the rotation you already know, for example if the marker is flat on a table. Finally, you can filter over time with a Kalman filter or a median filter to smooth out a rotation that flips from frame to frame.

The fifth section lists where PnP is used on a robot arm. It runs wherever a program knows an object's shape and can find points on it in a colour picture. First, reading a fiducial marker on a fixture or tray gives its pose in one picture. Second, calibration uses PnP to find the board's pose in every picture. Third, keypoint models use a neural network to find named points on an object, and PnP turns them into a pose. Fourth, matching against a stored picture of a product allows PnP to run inside RANSAC on the matched features. Fifth, a wrist camera can check its own pose by looking at a fixed marker on the table, to see if the hand-eye calibration has drifted. Finally, placing a part in a fixture works well when the part has drilled holes or printed corners that give PnP several well-spread points.

The sixth section covers where it works and where it does not. PnP is exact geometry, so it fails only when its inputs are wrong or too weak. A table lists common failures. If some pairings are wrong, you see a large error or a jumping pose, and you should run PnP inside RANSAC. If a small flat marker is seen face-on, the rotation wobbles or flips, so you should tilt the view or use a bigger marker. If the points bunch together in the picture, the pose changes a lot with small noise, so you need points spread over the whole object. If lens numbers are wrong or distortion is ignored, the pose is consistently off, so you must calibrate and undistort the pixels first. If the object's model does not match the real object, you get a steady error well above pixel noise, so you must measure the real object. If you use a poor first guess with few points, refinement can settle on a wrong pose, so you should use a proper solver like EPnP. Finally, if the object is symmetric, two or more poses fit equally well, so you must use a feature that breaks the symmetry or accept the ambiguity. 

PnP cannot find points itself, and gives no answer for an object whose shape you do not know. For those, a depth camera and a matching method like iterative closest point are the usual choice.

The seventh section lists libraries that provide PnP. OpenCV has it in the calib3d module for C++ and Python, with functions like solvePnP and solvePnPRansac. You can pass flags to choose solvers like EPnP, P3P, or a specific solver for square markers. The OpenCV aruco module finds markers and boards. The AprilTag library in C and Python estimates tag poses and keeps the one with the smaller error. PoseLib provides fast minimal solvers with RANSAC built in. There are also ROS 2 marker packages that publish each marker's pose directly.

The eighth section explains why to use PnP and what it costs. PnP gives you a full six degrees of freedom pose from one colour picture in well under a millisecond, with an error number that tells you how well it fits. The obvious alternative is a depth camera, which needs no printed marker and no known feature points. However, depth cameras struggle with shiny, black, and see-through surfaces, and have millimetres of noise. PnP needs only a colour camera, works on objects depth cameras cannot see, and is precise when points are well spread. The cost is that you need the object's shape as points, a reliable way to find them in the picture, and a calibrated camera. You also need RANSAC for wrong pairings, and you must plan the viewing angle for flat targets.

The ninth section looks at the learned alternative. The page on keypoints and object pose describes two learned ways to get the same six numbers. A keypoint model finds named points on the object in a messy picture, and PnP still turns those points into the pose. A render-and-compare model draws the object's CAD model at a guessed pose and corrects the guess until the drawing matches the photo. Learned models win in clutter and on objects with no marker. But PnP alone still wins when you can put a marker on the object, because it needs no training and no graphics processor, and its reprojection error tells you how well the pose fits.

The final section shows how to use it in Python. It uses the OpenCV call solvePnPRansac, because PnP is easy to feed with the wrong numbers. The code takes the camera matrix and distortion coefficients. It defines the object points in millimetres, and the image points in pixels, making sure they are in the exact same order. It calls solvePnPRansac, passing the points, the camera numbers, a flag for the iterative solver, a reprojection error threshold of 2 pixels to reject wrong matches, and a confidence of 0.999. The function returns a rotation vector, a translation vector, and the inliers. The code then converts the rotation vector into a 3 by 3 matrix and builds a 4 by 4 transform matrix. 

The library handles the first guess, the least-squares refinement, and the RANSAC loop. You just have to choose the right solver flag, like the square solver for a marker, or P3P for exactly three points. You still have to write the pairing and the checking yourself. PnP never looks at the picture, so a detector or matcher has to produce the image points. You also have to count the inliers and reproject the points to check the error yourself, because the function returning true does not mean the answer is right. On a flat target, you have to decide which of the two possible poses to keep. Finally, you must remember that if your object points are in millimetres, the translation comes back in millimetres too, which can be a surprise on an arm that works in metres. You must set the reprojection error threshold based on your detector's accuracy, and you must decide whether to trust the rotation at all, since a small flat marker seen nearly face-on gives a position you can rely on and a rotation you cannot.
