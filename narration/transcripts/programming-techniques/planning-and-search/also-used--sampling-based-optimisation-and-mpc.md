Sampling-based optimisation and model predictive control. 

This page explains how to choose the best plan when you can score a plan but cannot take a gradient of the score. It covers three methods: random shooting, the cross-entropy method, and CMA-ES. It then covers model predictive control, which runs one of these methods again and again while the arm moves. The page answers four questions, and the first two are about the methods themselves. How does each method work, and how do they compare on the same problem? Then come the practical ones: where does a robot arm use them, and when should you use something else instead?

It is for a reader who has read the chapter overview and the page on trajectory optimisation. That page improves a path by following the gradient, which is the direction in which the cost goes down fastest. This page is about what to do instead when there is no gradient to follow. Every number and picture on this page comes from a real run of a planning and search script.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. Sampling-based optimisation makes up many candidate plans, scores each one by simulating it, and uses the best-scoring ones to decide where to look next.

An everyday example shows the idea before any robot arm is involved. You are adjusting the shower, but you cannot see a formula for the water temperature, and you can only turn the tap and feel the result. So you try a few positions, notice which ones felt best, and then try a few more positions close to those. After a few rounds of this the water is right. You never worked out which way the temperature changes with the tap, because you only ever compared results. Sampling-based optimisation does the same thing, with a computer model in place of your hand under the water.

The next part sets up the example, which is pushing a block past a mug. The shower needed only the tap turned, so here is a robot task with far more to choose. The example on this page is a pushing task, seen from above. A block three centimetres across starts at zero, zero, and the target is at twenty, four. A mug stands at ten, two, right on the straight line between them. The mug is three and a half centimetres in radius, so the middle of the block must stay more than five centimetres from the middle of the mug. A dashed circle in the pictures shows that limit.

The arm does not grip the block, because it only pushes it. One action is one push, in which the pusher moves by some distance to the side and some distance forwards, at most five centimetres in total. A plan is a list of eight pushes, so one plan is made of sixteen numbers in all.

To score a plan, the program needs a push model, which is a rule that says where the block ends up after a push. The model on this page is deliberately simple, and it has two parts. First, the block slides zero point eight times as far as the pusher moves, because it slips. Second, it also drifts to the left by zero point one five times the push length, because the pusher meets it a little off centre.

The score of a plan is the distance from the block's final position to the target, plus one hundred if the block touches the mug on the way, so a lower score is better.

This score has no useful gradient, because the "plus one hundred" jumps from zero to one hundred the moment the block touches the mug. A gradient tells you what a tiny change does, and here a tiny change either does nothing to that part of the score or makes it jump. The same is true of many scores on a robot, such as whether the grasp held, whether the camera saw the part, or any score that comes out of a physics simulator. This is explained more fully on the Book 3 page about pushing and sliding, which explains why real pushing is hard to write as a formula at all.

The third section explains how it works. With the task and the score now fixed, the three methods differ only in how they choose the next batch of plans to try. They come in order below, from the simplest to the one that learns the most from each round, and then model predictive control wraps a loop around whichever one you pick.

The first method is random shooting, which means trying many plans and keeping the best. Its name comes from firing many random shots and keeping the one that lands nearest the target. First, it makes up many random plans. Here each push is picked at random, up to five centimetres in any direction. Next, it runs each plan through the push model, and works out its score. Finally, it keeps the plan with the lowest score.

A diagram on the page shows one real run with four hundred random push plans spread out around the block. Most of them wander around the start, because random pushes cancel each other out, and eighty-three of them, shown in red, touch the mug. The best plan, shown in green, ends twelve point one centimetres short of the target.

Random shooting is poor here because the plan has sixteen numbers. For a good plan most of those sixteen must be right at the same time, and random guessing almost never manages that. However, with only two or three numbers to choose, random shooting works well.

The next method is the cross-entropy method, or CEM, which narrows the search. It repeats random shooting, but each round it moves the search towards the best plans of the round before. The name comes from statistics, and you do not need that background to use the method.

It describes where to search with two lists of numbers. The mean is the middle of the search, with one value for each of the sixteen numbers of a plan. The spread then says how far from the mean to look, again one value for each number.

First, it starts with a mean of zero pushes and a spread of three centimetres. Second, it makes fifty plans by adding random amounts to the mean, scaled by the spread. Third, it scores all fifty, and keeps the best five. These are called the elites. Fourth, it sets the new mean to the average of the five elites, and sets the new spread to how much the five elites differ from each other. Finally, it goes back to the second step, and stops after a set number of rounds.

A diagram shows three rounds of this narrowing. In round one the plans spread everywhere. By round four they bend below the mug, and by round eight they form a tight bundle ending at the target.

A table gives the real numbers from that run, showing how the method focuses on the good plans over eight rounds. The spread shrinks every round, starting at three centimetres and ending at zero point four four centimetres. As it shrinks, the best plan's distance to the target drops from fifteen point one centimetres in the first round down to exactly one centimetre in the eighth round. This shrinking spread is how CEM focuses on the good plans. It is also its weakness, because if the spread shrinks before the mean has reached a good place, the search stops moving. On this run CEM got to one centimetre, but on other runs it stopped further away.

The third method is CMA-ES, which also learns the shape of the search. It stands for covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy, and it is the answer to that early narrowing. It keeps a mean and a spread, like CEM, but it adds two things on top.

First, it learns which numbers should change together. In a push plan, pushes three and four often need to turn left together, and CEM gives each number its own spread, so it cannot say that. CMA-ES instead keeps a table, called the covariance matrix, that says how each pair of numbers should move together. The search cloud can then stretch along a slanted direction instead of only along the axes.

Second, it keeps a separate step size. If the last few rounds all moved the mean the same way, the step size grows, so the search moves faster. If the moves were back and forth, the step size shrinks instead. This stops the search from freezing too early, which is CEM's weakness.

The maths behind these updates is much longer than the idea, so in practice you use it from a library. The script uses the standard settings, which for a plan of sixteen numbers means twelve plans per round.

The next part compares the three on the same budget. Now that all three methods have been described, the fair way to compare them is to give each one the same number of plan scores. Here each method scored four hundred plans, and each ran twenty times with different random seeds. A seed is the number that starts a random number generator, so each seed gives a different run.

A chart and a table show the median best score against the number of plans scored. After four hundred plans, random shooting ends at a median of nine point two centimetres from the target. The cross-entropy method ends at five point three centimetres, and CMA-ES ends at zero point two centimetres.

On this problem CMA-ES is clearly the best, while CEM beats random shooting but often narrows too early, and this matches common practice. CEM is popular because it is ten lines of code, and because it works well on short plans with a good starting guess, which is exactly the situation inside model predictive control. CMA-ES is instead the usual choice for a longer one-off search, such as tuning a set of gains.

The page then explains model predictive control, which means to plan, do one step, and look again. All three methods above score their plans in a model, so a plan is only ever as good as that model. The real block is never exactly like the model. To show this, the script uses a real block that slides only zero point six five times as far as the pusher and drifts zero point three zero times the push to the left. The planner does not know this, because it still uses the model's zero point eight and zero point one five.

Model predictive control, or MPC, deals with this by re-planning all the time, in four steps. First, look at where the block really is now. Second, plan a short sequence of pushes from there with the model. Here the plan is five pushes, found by CEM. Third, do only the first push of the plan, and throw the rest away. Finally, go back to the first step.

The short plan is called the horizon, and each new plan starts from the last plan, shifted by one push, so it already starts close to a good answer. This is called a warm start, and it is why CEM is enough inside MPC.

A diagram compares a one-off plan against MPC. On the left, a plan is made once with CMA-ES and carried out blind. In the model the plan ends zero point zero seven centimetres from the target. However, the real block slides less and drifts more, so it ends five point four centimetres from the target. Nothing corrected that error, because nothing ever looked at the real block.

On the right is MPC instead. It makes five-push plans before each real push. The model was wrong every time, but each error was small and was corrected at the next look, so the real block reaches the target, staying within zero point seven centimetres for the last three pushes.

MPC for the block uses a slightly different score from the one-off plan. It adds up the distance to the target after every push, not only the last one, so the block gets there soon and then stays. It also adds a cost for coming within one point five centimetres of the mug's limit, and that margin is there because the model is not exact. Without it, a plan that passes the mug by a millimetre in the model can touch it in reality.

This is the same MPC that is described on the Book 3 page about controlling the move: solve a short optimisation from where you are now, do the first command, throw the rest away, and solve again. Book 3 also lists the solvers that use gradients, while this page is the version for scores without a gradient.

The page then gives the pseudocode that puts the two together, with CEM first and then the MPC loop that calls it. The CEM function loops through the rounds, makes random plans based on the mean and spread, scores them by simulating them, finds the elites, and updates the mean and spread. The MPC function loops until the task is done. It measures the real world, calls CEM to get a plan, does only the first push on the real arm, and then updates the mean by shifting the plan along and adding a zero push at the end.

Random shooting is simply the CEM function with one round and a very wide spread. CMA-ES instead replaces the two update lines with its own updates for the mean, the covariance matrix and the step size.

The next part is about using a learned model inside MPC. The MPC above scored its plans with a push model written by hand. Sometimes nobody can write such a model, and then the model can instead be learned from records of the arm pushing the block. The planner itself does not change at all when this happens. CEM, the horizon of five pushes, the warm start, the score and doing only the first push all stay the same. Instead, only the prediction step inside the scoring function is replaced. The page on learned dynamics models in Book 6 explains how such a model is built and trained.

A single learned model carries a danger that a written one does not, because the planner searches for the plan with the best predicted score. Where the model has seen no records its predictions are only guesses, and the planner is drawn to any guess that looks good. So the usual choice is an ensemble, which is several copies of the network, each trained from different random starting numbers on its own resampled copy of the records. Where the records are dense the copies agree, and where there are none they disagree. The planner therefore uses the copies' average as the prediction, and adds a cost for how far apart they end up. This keeps the plans where the model knows what happens. The code changes to roll the plan forward through each copy of the ensemble, find the average path, and add a weight times the spread to the score.

The example runs use a real block that differs from the one above in two ways. First, every push has zero point one five centimetres of random scatter. Second, a push longer than three centimetres starts to turn the block, so it drifts a further zero point three centimetres for every centimetre beyond three centimetres. The arm records two hundred random pushes, each at most three centimetres long, and five small networks learn from them.

A diagram shows the forward slide and sideways drift against the push length. Inside the band of recorded pushes up to three centimetres, the five learned copies sit on the real block's line, and they match the real block better than the written model does. Beyond three centimetres, where there are no records, they spread apart, reaching zero point three zero centimetres apart at five centimetres. None of them knows about the extra drift, so that spread is the only warning the planner ever gets.

A table and a diagram show the results of sixty real runs for each version of MPC. The learned model on its own was no better than the written one. It was accurate where it had records, but the planner still chose long pushes, where it was only guessing, and twenty-six of the sixty runs touched the mug. With the cost for disagreement added, the planner chose fewer long pushes, and the runs that touched the mug fell to thirteen of sixty. However, sixty runs is only just enough to show this, because the ninety-five percent confidence intervals barely overlap. The page on evaluation and failure in Book 6 explains these intervals.

The weight on the spread is itself a setting to choose, and too much of it makes the planner timid. With a weight of two instead of zero point five, only four of sixty runs touched the mug, but only four ended within one centimetre, and the median run ended three point nine centimetres short. In other words, the planner refused the long pushes it needed. PETS, described in Book 6, is the standard published version of this method, and the mbrl-lib library provides it.

Section four covers where this is used on a robot arm. The pushing example is one case of a wider pattern, because sampling-based optimisation is used wherever a program can simulate a plan but cannot differentiate the result. Here are the concrete places it turns up on an arm.

First, pushing and sliding objects. An arm may push a mug out of the way before a grasp, or slide a box against a wall. The contact is hard to model with a clean formula, so a sampled plan through a rough model, corrected by MPC, is common.

Second, MPC with a learned model. Learned dynamics models plan with CEM and MPC through a neural network. The network has a gradient, but it is often unreliable, and CEM only needs the network's predictions.

Third, tuning controller gains. The gains of a PID controller can be scored by running a test move and measuring the overshoot and the settling time, so CMA-ES can tune a handful of gains this way.

Fourth, choosing a grasp. Grasp quality models use CEM to refine grasp candidates towards the ones the model scores highest.

Fifth, paths with costs that have no gradient. STOMP is sampling-based optimisation applied to a whole path, and MPPI is the same weighted-average idea used inside MPC.

Finally, fitting a model to measurements. When a simulator has unknown settings, such as friction, CMA-ES can choose the settings that make the simulator match recorded data, which is one approach to system identification.

Section five explains where it is useful, and where it is not. All of those uses rely on the same small requirement, because these methods need only one thing, which is a way to score a plan. That makes them easy to apply, but it also means they know nothing about the problem beyond the scores they see.

A table lists common problems and what people do instead. If there are too many numbers in a plan, the scores stop improving, so people use a shorter horizon, fewer numbers per push, or a gradient method. If CEM narrows too early, every run stops at a different, mediocre plan, so people use more plans per round, a minimum spread, or CMA-ES. If scoring is slow, people run many plans at once on a graphics card, or use a cheaper model. If the model is wrong, the plan looks perfect in simulation but fails on the arm, which is why people use MPC to re-plan from the real state, and add a margin around obstacles. If MPC is too slow for the control rate, people use fewer plans, warm starts, or use MPC only for the slow outer loop. If a score has a smooth, known gradient, sampling takes far more evaluations than needed, so people use trajectory optimisation or a solver.

Finally, if there is a hard safety limit, sometimes a plan breaks the limit. This is the row that matters most, because a sampled plan satisfies a rule only as far as its score punishes breaking it, and only in the model. So anything that must never happen needs its own check outside the optimiser, just as Book 3 says MPC cannot replace a safety stop.

Section six lists libraries that provide it. Because the methods themselves are small, CEM is short enough that most people write it themselves. CMA-ES is longer, so a library is the safer choice there. A table lists well-known ones. For Python, there is pycma, which is the reference CMA-ES by its author. There is Optuna, which puts CMA-ES inside a tuning framework that is handy for gains. There is Nevergrad, a collection of gradient-free optimisers. There is pytorch_mppi for sampling MPC on a graphics card, and mbrl-lib for CEM and MPC for learned dynamics models. For C++, there is MuJoCo MPC, which includes a Predictive Sampling planner that Book 3 recommends for building intuition. For a first try, the page advises writing CEM in NumPy around your own model and score. Then move to pycma when the plan has more than a handful of numbers and CEM stops improving.

Section seven explains why you would choose sampling, and what it costs. With the methods, the uses and the libraries covered, this section answers the four questions for sampling-based optimisation: what it is, what it does for you, why use it rather than the obvious alternative, and what it costs.

It is a family of methods that choose a plan by scoring many candidate plans in a model and moving the search towards the best ones. MPC then wraps it in a loop that re-plans from the real state after every step. Together they let an arm act well with only a rough model and a score, even when the score jumps, as it does when a block touches a mug.

The obvious alternative is a gradient method, such as the trajectory optimiser of this chapter. When a smooth gradient exists, a gradient method needs far fewer evaluations, and it scales to hundreds of numbers. However, it cannot use a score that jumps, a simulator it cannot look inside, or a learned model whose gradient is unreliable, while sampling can use all three. So choose sampling when the score has no useful gradient and the plan has tens of numbers rather than thousands. Choose a gradient method instead when the cost is smooth and you can write it down.

The second alternative is to plan once and then carry the plan out blind. That is cheaper, but the MPC picture showed what happens, because a plan that was perfect in the model missed by five point four centimetres on the real block. MPC costs a new search at every step, and in return it corrects that error for free.

The costs come in five parts. The first is the sheer number of evaluations, because one decision takes hundreds or thousands of model runs. The second is that the result is random, so two runs give different plans. The third is that there is no guarantee of the best plan, only of a good one. The fourth is a dependence on the model, which MPC reduces but does not remove. The fifth is the settings you must choose, which are the number of plans, the number of elites, the horizon and the spread. On this page's problem, the choice of method alone changed the result from nine point two centimetres to zero point two centimetres.

Section eight covers the learned alternative. Using a learned model inside MPC keeps the search and learns only the prediction, but the other learned alternative replaces the search itself. A policy that learns without a model, such as a reinforcement learning policy, turns the state straight into the next action in one pass, with no rollouts at each step. So it wins when each decision must be fast and the task stays fixed. However, Book 6 says it needs far more attempts to learn, and it learns only one task, while MPC with a model can push the block to a different mark tomorrow just by changing the score. So choose MPC when the goal changes, or when the search fits in the time between steps.

The final section is about using it in Python. It gives the shortest honest version of the cross-entropy method and CMA-ES on the pushing task, showing exactly where the line between your code and a library falls.

The first block of code is the cross-entropy method, written out in NumPy. It is short on purpose, because this is a method most people write rather than install. It defines a score function that simulates eight pushes, limits each push to five centimetres, calculates the slip and drift, checks if the block hits the mug, and returns the distance to the target plus the penalty. Then it runs a loop for eight rounds of fifty plans, keeping the best five each time to update the mean and spread.

The second block replaces the search loop with pycma, which is CMA-ES written by the person who invented it.

Running the first block prints a best plan fifteen point one centimetres from the target after round one, and two point two centimetres after round eight, with the spread shrinking from three centimetres to zero point four three centimetres. This is the narrowing described earlier. Running the second block on the same score with a budget of four hundred simulations reaches zero point two three centimetres. That gap is the argument for CMA-ES: on the same budget it learns the shape of the search as well as its position, so it keeps improving after the cross-entropy method's spread has collapsed.

The library does the sampling and the updates, which together are several hundred lines that are easy to get wrong. What you still have to write is the score function, and on this page that is not a detail but the entire content. A sampling optimiser knows nothing except how to ask what a plan costs, so the model of how the block slides, the keep-out distance from the mug, and the penalty for touching it are all yours. That is also why the method is worth knowing. The score function contains an "if" statement that jumps by one hundred, and such a jump has no useful gradient, so no gradient-based optimiser can use this function at all. For model predictive control you also write the outer loop, which measures the world, plans, does only the first push, shifts the mean along by one step and plans again.

What you have to decide or measure is the model inside the score, the budget, and the starting spread. The slip and drift are measurements of your own pusher on your own surface, and a wrong model gives a confident plan that fails on the robot. The budget is set by how long you can afford between control cycles rather than by what converges best. The starting spread of three centimetres has to cover the useful plans without wasting the first rounds on absurd ones. Finally, the penalty of one hundred for touching the mug is a choice. It has to be large enough that no plan buys distance by clipping the mug, which you check by looking at whether the best plan ever touches it.
