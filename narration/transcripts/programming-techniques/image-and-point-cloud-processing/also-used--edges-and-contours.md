Edges and contours. This page explains how a program finds the outline of an object in a picture, and what it can learn from that outline once it has traced it. Because a program does that work in a fixed order, the page follows the same order through five questions. First, what is an edge, and how is it computed from the raw brightness numbers? Second, how does the Canny edge detector turn a noisy picture into thin, clean lines? Third, how does a program walk round the outline of a blob and store it as a list of points? Fourth, how does it then turn that list into a polygon with a few corners? And fifth, how does it count those corners, or fit a known shape, so that it can say "this is a triangle" or "this is a circle"? Because real outlines are often broken, a last section adds a sixth question: how does a program find a straight line or a circle when the outline comes in pieces?

It is for a reader who knows what a pixel and a grey picture are, and who has already read about thresholding and colour masks. That page explains how to turn a picture into a mask, which is a picture where each pixel is either object or not object. This page often starts from such a mask rather than from the raw picture. So the mask has to be tidy before its outline is worth tracing, and this is explained on the page about morphology and the distance transform.

On a robot arm, edges and outlines answer questions like "where exactly is the border of this part?", "which way is this block turned?" and "is this block a hexagon or a circle?". They answer them cheaply, because they cost a few milliseconds and need no training data at all.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. An edge is a place in a picture where the brightness changes sharply, and a contour is the closed line you get by following the edge all the way round one object.

For example, put a white plate on a dark wooden table and look at it. Because one side of the rim is dark and the other side is bright, your eye finds the plate's rim at once. If you traced that rim with a pencil on a photo, the line you drew would be its contour. If someone then asked whether the plate was round or square, you would look at the traced line and count its corners. This means a program does the same three things, in the same order, and the rest of this page is how it does each one.

The next part of the page explains how it works. The three things your eye did become five steps in a program. While the first two find edges in a grey picture, the last three start from a mask, trace its outline and describe its shape. Because it depends on what a program already has, a real program often uses only some of the five. For example, a program that already has a clean mask from a colour threshold skips steps one and two and begins at step three.

Step one computes the gradient of a picture. The gradient of a picture at a pixel is an arrow that points in the direction in which the brightness rises fastest. Because its length says how fast the brightness rises, the arrow has length zero in a flat area and is long on an edge.

Since a program cannot measure a slope at a single pixel, it has to compare that pixel with its neighbours. The most common way of doing so is the Sobel operator, which looks at the three by three patch of pixels round the pixel it is working on. It multiplies that patch by two small tables of weights, one for the change from left to right and one for the change from top to bottom. So between them the two tables cover both directions.

For the change from left to right, the left column of weights is minus one, minus two, minus one. The middle column is all zeros. The right column is plus one, plus two, plus one. To get the change from left to right, multiply each weight by the pixel under it and add the nine results together. Then do the same with the second table of weights to get the change from top to bottom. That second table is rotated, so its top row is negative, its middle row is zero, and its bottom row is positive. The length of the arrow is the square root of the sum of the squares of those two changes, and its direction is the angle of the point formed by those two changes. Because the pixels right next to the centre are the best evidence of what happens at the centre, the middle row and column carry a weight of two.

The page shows a diagram of an eight by eight picture where a bright square with brightness two hundred sits on a dark table with brightness twenty. The left panel shows the brightness numbers, and the right panel shows the gradient that the Sobel operator computes at every pixel.

Take a pixel that is dark while its right-hand neighbour is bright. Its three by three patch has rows reading twenty, twenty, two hundred. For the change from left to right, each row gives minus twenty plus zero plus two hundred, which is one hundred and eighty, and the middle row counts twice. So the total change from left to right is seven hundred and twenty. Because the top row and the bottom row are the same, they cancel, so the change from top to bottom comes out as zero. This means the arrow has length seven hundred and twenty and points straight to the right, towards the bright square.

A pixel inside the square gets no arrow at all, because its whole patch reads two hundred and both sums come out as zero. A pixel on the square's top-left corner gets a change of five hundred and forty in both directions. This means its arrow has length seven hundred and sixty-three point seven and points diagonally down and to the right, into the square. All of these numbers come from running the Sobel operator in NumPy on this exact picture.

Two things in the right panel matter for the step that follows. First, the arrows sit on both sides of the real border. This means a raw gradient gives an edge two pixels thick rather than one. Second, the arrows always point across the edge and never along it. That is why the Canny detector, which is the next step, can use both of those facts.

Step two is the Canny edge detector. Because the gradient from step one is both thick and noisy, something has to thin it down. The Canny edge detector, published by John Canny in nineteen eighty-six, is the standard way to turn a gradient into thin edge lines one pixel wide, and it does that in four steps.

First, blur the picture a little with a Gaussian blur. A Gaussian blur replaces each pixel by a weighted average of the pixels round it, with the nearest pixels weighted most. This removes single-pixel noise, which would otherwise give strong gradients everywhere.

Second, compute the gradient at every pixel with the Sobel operator.

Third, thin the edges. Look at each pixel's two neighbours across the edge, in the direction of its gradient arrow. Keep the pixel only if its gradient is at least as long as both of theirs. Otherwise set it to zero. This step is called non-maximum suppression, and it leaves only the ridge of each thick band.

Finally, keep strong edges, and weak edges that touch them. Use two thresholds, a high one and a low one. A pixel above the high threshold is a strong edge and is kept. A pixel between the two is a weak edge. It is kept only if it is connected, through other kept pixels, to a strong edge. Everything below the low threshold is dropped. This step is called hysteresis thresholding.

The two thresholds in the fourth step are the reason Canny works so well. For example, a real edge often has a strong part and a faint part, where a shadow falls across it. So a single high threshold would break the edge at the faint part, while a single low threshold would keep every patch of noise as well. The two thresholds together keep the whole of a real edge and still drop the noise that stands alone.

A diagram shows the four steps on a sixty-four by ninety-six picture that has a lot of noise in it. Because there is so much noise, the two thresholds really matter here. A dim triangle with brightness one hundred and thirty and a bright turned square with brightness two hundred sit on a table of brightness sixty.

Each panel is the real output of that step, computed in NumPy. The Gaussian blur has a spread of one pixel, the low threshold is eighty and the high threshold is two hundred.

The numbers tell the story of what each step takes away. A single threshold of eighty on the gradient keeps one thousand, one hundred and eighty-five pixels, which form thick bands round both shapes as well as many specks of noise. After thinning, five hundred and fifteen pixels above eighty remain, of which one hundred and sixty-three are strong and the other three hundred and fifty-two are weak. Hysteresis then keeps seventy-six of the weak pixels, because they touch a strong chain, and it drops the other two hundred and seventy-six. So the final result has two hundred and thirty-nine edge pixels in all. In the last panel the kept weak pixels are blue, and they lie almost all on the dim triangle, whose edge is too faint in places to be strong. Because the dropped weak pixels are scattered noise that touches no strong chain, they are drawn in light grey.

The code for the Canny detector comes to only a few lines, because each of its steps is a simple pass over the pixels. It smooths the image, computes the Sobel gradient and direction for each pixel, and then thins the edges by checking neighbours along the gradient direction. Finally, it finds all strong pixels above the high threshold, and uses a queue to grow that edge into any neighbouring weak pixels that are above the low threshold.

Step three is tracing a contour. Canny gives back loose edge pixels rather than whole objects. So a program that wants to describe an object still needs its outline as one list of points in order, and that ordered list is the contour.

Because a mask's outline is always closed while an edge line can have gaps in it, contours are usually traced from a mask rather than from Canny edges. The usual method is border following, also called Moore-neighbour tracing. It works like a person walking round a pond while keeping one hand on the fence.

First, scan the mask row by row from the top. The first object pixel you meet is on the outline. Call it the start. Second, stand on the current pixel. Look at its eight neighbours one by one, turning clockwise, beginning just after the pixel you came from. Third, the first object pixel you meet is the next outline pixel. Step onto it. Finally, repeat until you come back to the start pixel, arriving the same way as the first time.

The result is a list of pixel positions in walking order. For example, a diagram shows a mask of a hexagonal block, its traced outline, and the polygon that simplifies it. The middle panel shows the one hundred and thirty outline points the trace found, and the right panel shows the six corners that the next step keeps from them.

A contour gives several useful numbers straight away, before anything is simplified. Its area is the number of pixels inside it, which here is one thousand, six hundred and twenty-four. Its perimeter is the length of the line through the outline points, which here is one hundred and fifty-four pixels. Its centroid is the average position of the pixels inside. That is why it is a good point to aim the gripper at when the object is compact.

A mask can hold several objects, and an object can have a hole in it, such as a washer. This is why real contour functions handle both cases: they return one contour per outline, and they also record which contour lies inside which. Then, when you only want the outer outline of each object, you ask the function for the outer contours only.

Step four is simplifying the contour to a polygon. Because the traced outline follows every pixel step, it holds far more points than the shape itself needs. For example, a hexagon's outline has one hundred and thirty points, while a person would describe the same shape with six corners. The Ramer-Douglas-Peucker algorithm removes the points that are not needed. It keeps a point only if leaving that point out would move the line by more than a chosen tolerance.

For an open line, which has a first point and a last point to work from, the method goes like this. First, draw a straight line from the first point to the last point. Second, find the point that lies farthest from that line. Third, if that distance is less than the tolerance, the straight line is good enough, so keep only the two end points. Otherwise, keep the farthest point, and repeat the whole method on the part before it and the part after it.

A contour is closed, however, so it has no first point and no last point to start from. The usual fix is to cut the loop in two at two points that lie far apart. Then simplify each half on its own, and join the two halves back together. Because the outline point farthest from the centroid is almost always a real corner, the code behind the pictures on this page starts there.

Since the tolerance is the one setting that matters here, it is worth choosing with care. For example, a common choice is two percent of the contour's perimeter, so that the setting grows with the object's size in the picture. For the hexagon that works out as three point zero eight pixels, and it gives six corners.

The page shows a table of how the number of corners changes with the tolerance, for the hexagon and for a circle of about the same size. The hexagon gives six corners over a wide range of tolerances, from one percent to five percent. This means that a wide flat range is the sign of a real corner count. The circle has no real corners, so its count keeps falling as the tolerance grows. At half a percent it has sixteen corners, but at five percent it has four, meaning it would even pass for a square. This means that counting corners on its own cannot tell a circle from a polygon, so the next step adds a second measure.

Step five is counting corners and fitting shapes. Once a program has the polygon from step four, naming the shape is simple for flat parts with straight sides. Three corners means a triangle, and six corners means a hexagon. But four corners means either a square or a rectangle, and the side lengths and angles tell you which.

Round shapes have no corners, so they need a different test. The usual one is roundness, also called circularity. It is calculated as four times pi times the area, divided by the square of the perimeter. It is one for a perfect circle and smaller for every other shape. For example, a perfect square gives zero point seven nine and a perfect equilateral triangle gives zero point six zero.

A diagram compares four shapes of the same size: a triangle, a square, a hexagon, and a circle. It shows their corner count at a two percent tolerance and their measured roundness. The measured roundness values are zero point five six for the triangle, zero point six nine for the square, zero point eight three for the hexagon, and zero point nine zero for the circle. These are all lower than the perfect values given above. They come out low because a traced outline steps from pixel to pixel, and so it is a little longer than the true smooth outline. The order is still right, however, and the circle stands clearly apart from the other three. This means a rule such as "roundness above zero point eight seven means round, otherwise count the corners" sorts all four shapes correctly here. But on your own camera you would measure the values on real parts before choosing that number.

For a round object you usually want its centre and its radius rather than a corner count. A least-squares circle fit finds the circle that passes closest to all the outline points at once. That fit is explained more fully on the page about least-squares fitting. Run on the circle's one hundred and twenty-eight outline points, it gives a centre of thirty point zero, thirty point zero, and a radius of twenty-two point five six pixels. The mask itself was drawn with its centre at thirty, thirty and a radius of twenty-three pixels. So the fit is half a pixel small, because the traced points are the centres of the outer ring of pixels, and those centres lie half a pixel inside the true edge. This is a real effect rather than a mistake, so it is worth knowing about when you turn pixels into millimetres. The diagram shows this fitted circle as a dashed line.

Other fits work in the same way, and each one answers a different question about the part. For example, the smallest turned rectangle round a contour gives the part's length, width and angle, while a fitted ellipse gives the tilt of a round rim. A program can also compute the convex hull, which is the outline you get by stretching a rubber band round the contour. Then comparing the hull's area with the contour's area finds dents and notches.

The next section explains finding lines and circles by voting, using the Hough transform. Steps three to five all start from a clean mask with one closed outline. But real edges are often not like that at all. A cable lies across the edge of the table, a shadow hides part of a box, or a gripper finger covers a quarter of a cup's rim. In each of those cases Canny gives back several short pieces of edge, plus specks of texture. So in those cases no single contour is the whole object.

The Hough transform finds straight lines and circles in exactly those broken edges. Its idea fits into one sentence: every edge pixel votes for every shape that could pass through it, and the shape with the most votes wins.

For example, ask a room of people, each standing somewhere on a large floor, to name every straight path across the room that passes through their own spot. Most paths get one or two names, but if ten people happen to stand in a row, the path along that row is named ten times. This means you find the row by counting names, without ever looking for it directly. It does not matter if there are gaps in the row, or if other people stand around at random.

For lines, every point votes for every line through it. A straight line in a picture can be described by just two numbers. In other words, a vote for a line is really a vote for a pair of numbers. The first number is the line's angle, and the second is its distance from the picture's top-left corner, measured straight across to the line. With those two numbers, a pixel's distance is its x coordinate times the cosine of the angle, plus its y coordinate times the sine of the angle.

The Hough transform keeps a vote table, also called an accumulator. It has one row for each possible distance and one column for each possible angle, and every cell starts at zero. Then, for each edge pixel, the program tries every angle, works out the distance of the line through that pixel at that angle, and adds one vote to the matching cell. This means each pixel draws a wavy curve of votes across the table. Because pixels that lie on one line all vote for the same cell, their curves cross at that cell.

A diagram shows lines through one point, and the curves of four points in the vote table. Three pixels lie on one line, so their curves cross at one spot in the table: an angle of sixty-four degrees and a distance of forty pixels. A fourth pixel is not on that line, so its curve misses that cell altogether.

The code for this method is short. It creates a table of zeros, then loops over each edge pixel. For each pixel, it loops through angles from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees, calculates the distance, and adds a vote to that cell. Finally, it repeatedly finds the cell with the most votes. If that cell has enough votes, it records the line and sets that cell and the cells around it to zero, so one line is reported once. Only angles from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees are needed, because a line at one hundred and ninety degrees is the same line as one at ten degrees, with the sign of the distance flipped.

The page gives a worked example of a broken table edge. A diagram shows one hundred and sixty-seven edge pixels. One hundred of them lie on the front edge of a table, which has two gaps where a cable and a shadow cross it. Twenty-seven lie on one side of a box, which has one gap. But the other forty are specks of table texture, and they belong to no line at all.

The vote table has over fifty-four thousand cells, and each bright band in it is one pixel's curve. The table edge gets eighty-eight votes, at an angle of seventy-six degrees and a distance of sixty-eight pixels. The other twelve of its one hundred pixels round into the next row, at a distance of sixty-seven. This is why the program clears the cells round a peak once it has taken that peak, because otherwise it would report the same line twice. The box side gets twenty-eight votes, at an angle of zero degrees and a distance of eighty-eight. Because specks do not line up with each other, no speck gets more than a few votes. The two lines found run straight across the gaps. Because a gap only means a few missing votes, the line is found all the same.

The Hough transform can also find circles, such as the rim of a cup seen from above. A camera looking straight down at a cup sees its rim as a circle. But a circle needs three numbers rather than two: the x and y of its centre, and its radius. In other words, the Hough transform for circles is the same voting idea with a different shape of vote.

First fix the radius, say twenty pixels, so that only the centre is left to find. Then every point at a distance of twenty from an edge pixel could be the centre. So each edge pixel votes for every cell on a circle of radius twenty round itself. Because the real centre lies at twenty pixels from every rim pixel, all of those circles of votes pass through it.

A diagram shows one hundred and thirty-four edge pixels, of which one hundred and nine lie on the rim of a cup. A gripper finger hides a quarter of the rim, and the other twenty-five pixels are specks. Each rim pixel votes on a circle round itself, and the votes pile up at the centre.

In the vote table, the highest cell is the true centre, with sixty-nine votes, while the highest cell anywhere else has only twelve. Because each rim pixel sits at a whole-pixel position, the peak does not get all one hundred and nine votes. Its true distance from the centre is a little more or a little less than twenty, so its circle of votes can pass through a neighbouring cell instead. The peak is still clear, however, even with a quarter of the rim missing.

When the radius is not known, the program tries a range of radii and keeps the one whose best cell has the most votes. For example, trying radii from ten to thirty pixels shows that radius twenty gets sixty-nine votes, while radius nineteen gets thirty-four and radius twenty-one gets thirty-two. Every other radius gets twenty-six or fewer, so the radius is found as well as the centre. This means that neither the centre nor the radius has to be known beforehand.

However, trying every radius costs a lot of time, because the vote table becomes three-dimensional. So OpenCV's HoughCircles function avoids that cost with a shortcut, using the direction of each pixel's gradient from step one. On a circle the gradient points straight towards or away from the centre. This means each pixel only has to vote along one short line instead of round a whole circle.

The page lists where the Hough transform is used, and where it fails. It earns its place on a robot arm because each of these jobs has a line or a circle hidden inside broken edges. First, finding the rim of a cup, bowl or bottle seen from above. It gives the centre and radius even when a finger or a handle hides part of the rim. Second, finding round parts on a tray, like washers or coins, even if they overlap a little. Third, finding straight edges for alignment, like the edge of a table or a box, which tells the arm how the object is turned. Finally, finding holes and pegs, where a camera on the wrist looks down at a hole before an insertion.

But it fails in four ways as well. First, too many edges. On a patterned surface, random pixels line up by chance and give false peaks. You can fix this by raising the vote threshold, blurring more before Canny, or working from a colour or depth mask instead. Second, a slanted view. A circle seen at a slant is an ellipse, so the circle vote smears out. You should look from straight above, or fit an ellipse to the contour instead. Third, coarse cells or a wrong radius range. Cells that are too big merge two nearby lines, while cells that are too small split one line's votes over several cells. A radius range that leaves out the real radius finds nothing at all. Finally, clean outlines. When the outline is whole and there is little noise, a least-squares fit to the contour is faster and more exact. When some points are wrong but the object is one shape, RANSAC is the other common choice. Hough is best when several lines or circles hide in one set of broken edges, because every shape gets its own peak. You never have to write the voting loop yourself, as libraries like OpenCV and scikit-image provide functions for it.

The next section shows where these methods sit in the software of a real robot arm. Edges and contours turn up at many points. They are used for sorting blocks by shape, by finding the contour, counting corners, and measuring roundness. They find which way a part is turned, because the smallest turned rectangle round a contour gives the angle of a long part, so the wrist can turn to match. They measure a part before grasping it, converting the contour's width in pixels to millimetres to check if it fits between the gripper fingers. They check a flat part against a drawing, so holes or missing corners show up as differences. They find printed square markers used for calibration. They find the rim of a cup or bowl by fitting an ellipse to the edge. And they check the result of a place operation, where a small gap between a placed part's contour and a tray pocket's contour means the part sits correctly.

The uses just mentioned all share the same conditions. Edges and contours work best when the object is flat or seen straight from above, has a clear border against a plain background, and does not touch anything else. This means they get worse quickly as soon as any of those conditions fails.

The page lists common failures. If there is low contrast between the object and the table, Canny finds only parts of the outline. The fix is better lighting, a contrasting table colour, or a depth threshold. On textured objects or a patterned table, you get hundreds of edges inside the object. People use a colour or depth mask first, then trace contours on the mask. Shadows can make the object look bigger or the wrong shape, which is fixed by diffuse lighting or a depth camera. If objects touch each other, they come back as one contour with too many corners. This is solved by the distance transform and watershed methods, or depth clustering. If the camera is at a slant, squares look irregular and circles look like ellipses. You should look from straight above or correct the view with the camera's known pose. For a small or far object, corners get rounded off, so you should move the camera closer. Noise in the mask can add an extra corner or tiny contour, which is fixed by an opening step on the mask and setting a minimum contour area. Finally, for see-through or shiny objects, the edge found is a reflection, not the object's border. For these, people use a segmentation model trained on such objects.

Because every step on this page is available in well-known libraries, nothing has to be written from scratch. OpenCV provides all these steps for C++, Python, and Java. It has Sobel and Scharr for gradients, Canny for the edge detector, and findContours for border following. It provides functions for polygon simplification, perimeter, area, and fitting shapes like turned rectangles, ellipses, and convex hulls. It also provides the Hough transform for lines and circles. In Python, scikit-image provides similar functions for gradients, Canny, contours, polygon simplification, and the Hough transform, as well as region properties for labelled blobs. SciPy provides the Sobel gradient on any NumPy array, and the Point Cloud Library provides boundary estimation for 3D point clouds. In OpenCV's Python interface, the names start with cv2 dot.

The next section asks whether you should call these methods at all, and what they cost. Edges and contours are a fixed set of rules. The gradient finds where brightness changes, Canny thins that into lines, and border following turns a mask into an ordered outline. Then polygon simplification and shape fitting describe that outline in a few numbers.

What they do for you is give exact geometry. A contour tells you the border of an object to within about a pixel, and from that border you get the area, the centre, the angle, the width and the shape class. Every one of those numbers is easy to check by hand. So when one of them is wrong, you can see why by drawing the contour on the picture.

The costs come in three parts. First, you must control the scene, because every method depends on a clear border between the object and its background. Second, you must choose thresholds, which are the two Canny thresholds, the mask threshold, the polygon tolerance and the roundness cut-off. Because each one depends on the lighting and the camera, each one needs checking on real pictures. Third, a contour is flat, so it says nothing about height, and it says nothing about the parts of the object the camera cannot see.

The obvious alternative to these methods is a trained segmentation model or object detector. A segmentation model or an object detector finds the object's outline or box, and a keypoint and pose model finds which way that object is turned. A model copes with the texture, clutter and shadows that break contours. But it needs labelled training pictures and a computer that can run it, and it can fail in ways that are hard to explain. So choose contours instead when the scene is controlled, which means a plain table, good light, parts that stand apart, and shapes that differ in clear geometric ways. Because a model's output mask still has to be turned into a centre, an angle and a size, programs often run contour steps on it even when a model is used.

The final section shows how to use these methods in Python. It explains a short program that runs the Canny detector on a grey picture to get an edge picture, and traces contours on a mask to get closed outlines it can measure. Both come from OpenCV.

The code reads an image, converts it to greyscale, and runs Canny with a low limit of fifty and a high limit of one hundred and fifty. It then reads a mask and runs findContours to get the outermost outlines. For each outline, it calculates the perimeter, simplifies it to a polygon using a tolerance of two percent of the perimeter, and fits a minimum area rectangle to get the width, height, and angle. Finally, it runs the Hough transform on the edge picture to find broken lines.

On a made-up picture of two green blocks on a grey table, Canny marks two hundred and eighty-seven edge pixels, and findContours on the colour mask returns two outlines. The larger has an area of four thousand, eight hundred and ninety-four pixels and a perimeter of two hundred and seventy-seven point seven, simplifies to four corners, and fits a seventy by seventy pixel rectangle. The smaller fits sixty by fifty pixels. The Hough transform on the same edge picture finds three line segments, the first running from coordinates one hundred and fifty, one hundred and sixty to one hundred and fifty, ninety, which is one side of the larger block.

OpenCV does every step, but you still have to write the interpretation. A contour with four corners might be a square block or a square hole or the shadow of both, and only your program knows which. You also have to write the step that picks the contour you care about, because findContours returns every outline in the picture including the frame of the table. And you have to read the fitted rectangle's angle carefully, because the same rectangle can be reported as seventy by seventy at minus ninety degrees or at zero degrees, so a gripper angle computed from it needs wrapping into the range the gripper can actually reach.

Finally, you have to decide or measure the thresholds. The fifty and one hundred and fifty are gradient strengths in your own pictures, so you set them by trying values on real pictures from your own camera and lighting. The rule of thumb is that the high limit should be two or three times the low one. The tolerance of two percent of the perimeter decides how much detail survives, and making it a fraction of the perimeter rather than a fixed number of pixels is deliberate, because it then behaves the same on a near object and a far one. For the Hough transform, you decide the threshold, which is how many edge pixels must agree before a line is reported, and the maximum line gap, which is how large a break in a line you are willing to bridge. Both depend on how broken you expect your outlines to be, which is a property of your scene and not of the library.
