Force and slip models. This page answers one question: how can a robot tell that an object in its fingers is starting to slide out, early enough to do something about it? To answer that, it explains the force signals a model reads, how a slip model works and is trained, the models people have published, and why this job is harder than it looks. 

It is written for a reader who has already read the page on touch sensing models, because this page uses the moving dots from its section on that topic. You should also know from the page on how a model learns what it means to train a model on examples. Before this page, it helps to have read the page on sensor streams, which explains windows of readings, smoothing, rates of change and thresholds that do not flicker. A slip model reads the same kind of window.

The first section covers what a slip model is. A slip model reads the force signals from a gripper over a short stretch of time and says whether the held object is starting to slide. 

Here is an everyday example of the same skill in a person. You carry a full glass of water, and your hand is a little wet. You feel a tiny slide on your fingertips before the glass moves far, so without thinking you squeeze a bit harder, and the glass stays put. In other words, you did not wait to see it fall. 

So a slip model tries to give a robot that same early warning. The word slip means that the object moves relative to the fingers, and the useful moment to catch it is the very start, which is called incipient slip. Incipient means just beginning, and at that moment squeezing a little harder still works. However, a second later the object is already on the floor. 

This page also covers the wider family of force models, which do a related job. These read force signals and say something else about the contact, such as how the object is sitting in the fingers, or whether a part has clicked into place. However, slip is the most common job, so the page uses it as the main example.

The next part explains what goes in and what comes out of the model. Now that slip has a name, here is what the model actually reads. The input is a short window of recent sensor readings, where a window means the last few tenths of a second of readings, taken together. Those readings can come from several different sensors. First, a tactile sensor on the finger pad can provide a short run of tactile pictures, or the positions of the printed dots in each picture. Second, a force sensor on the finger pad can give the push straight in and the push sideways at each moment. Third, a force-torque sensor at the wrist can give six numbers at each moment, which are a push along three directions and a twist about three directions. Finally, a small vibration sensor, called an accelerometer, can be placed near the finger. 

Then the output is usually one of two things. The first is a yes-or-no answer, slipping or not slipping, and the second is a number between zero and one that says how likely a slip is. In the second case, the program then compares that number with a limit it chooses. Some force models give other outputs, such as "the grasp will hold" or "the part is seated", but they all work in the same way.

The next section shows what slip looks like in the signal. A slip leaves marks in the signal, and a slip model learns to recognise them, so it is worth seeing those marks first. 

The page includes a diagram showing one grip as a force trace, where the fingers close, lift, hold, and then slip. It shows the force on one finger pad during one grip. The force jumps when the fingers close, and it wobbles briefly. Then it wobbles again when the lift starts, and after that it holds steady. When the slip starts, a fast, small shaking appears, and then the force drops as the mug slides out. 

However, two things make slip hard to spot by eye, and by a simple rule. The first is that the jolts from closing and lifting look a lot like the shaking from slip, because both are quick changes. So a rule that says a quick change means slip fires on every lift. The second is that the drop in force comes late, because by the time the force has clearly dropped, the object is already sliding out. 

But a tactile sensor shows slip in a second way, and this one comes earlier. A second diagram shows dots on a gel pad at three moments: while the object is held, while it is starting to slip at the rim, and while it is slipping. While the object is held, the dots in the contact lean a little, all together. When slip starts, the dots at the rim of the contact slide first, while the dots in the centre still stick, and the rim goes first because that is where the pressure is lowest. Once the object slips fully, every dot in the contact moves the same way. That middle stage is the moment a slip model tries to catch, because the centre still holds and there is still time to squeeze harder.

The next part explains how it works inside, starting with how it reads one window at a time. A slip model does not read the whole grip at once. Instead, it reads one short window at a time, and it moves the window forward as new readings arrive. A diagram shows a short window of the force signal going into a small network that says slip or no slip. It shows two windows on the same signal. The green one covers a steady stretch, so the network says no slip, while the red one covers the fast shaking, so the network says slip, and the same network runs on every window. 

The steps are these. First, the program keeps the last few tenths of a second of readings in memory. Second, it passes that window to the model. Third, the model gives back slip or no slip, or a number for how likely a slip is. Finally, the program acts on the answer, and the window moves on. The window length is a choice with a cost attached to it. A short window gives an answer sooner, but it holds less evidence, so the model makes more mistakes. Instead, a long window is more reliable, but its answer comes later.

The section then covers what kind of network is used. The window is the same idea for every sensor, but different signals suit different networks. For tactile pictures, the model often uses a convolutional neural network to read each picture, as explained in the touch sensing page. A second part then looks at how the pictures change over the window. A common choice for that second part is a long short-term memory network. This is a network that reads a sequence one step at a time and keeps a short memory of what it has read so far. 

For a force signal, which is only a few numbers at each moment, a simpler network is often enough, because it can read the window as one list of numbers. Some models first turn the window into a set of frequencies, which shows the fast shaking of slip more clearly. 

Finally, some models also look at a camera picture of the object in the gripper. The camera can see the object move a lot, while the tactile sensor can feel it move a little, so together they catch more slips than either one alone.

The next section describes how the model is trained, and where its examples come from. A slip model learns from windows of readings that each have the right answer, slip or no slip, and getting those answers is the hard part. So the usual way is to cause slips on purpose, many times over, and record them. A robot grips an object gently and lifts it, or pulls it sideways, or tilts it, so that some of those grips hold and some slip. 

For each window, someone then needs to know whether a slip was happening, and people find that out in three ways. First, they watch the object with a camera and a tracking marker. If the object moves relative to the fingers, that window is marked slip. Second, they use the tactile dots. If the dots in the centre of the contact move, the object has slipped. Third, they mark it by hand from a video. This is slow and it is done for small sets only. 

Published slip data sets are small, and they are much smaller than the data sets for camera models, because every example costs a real grip on a real robot. That is one reason slip models often fail on objects unlike the ones they were trained on.

The next part lists well-known models. This area is mostly research papers rather than products, so the list is of papers. All of them are real and often cited. 

First, Veiga and colleagues in 2015 trained a model to predict slip from a BioTac, a fingertip-shaped sensor filled with fluid that measures pressure and vibration. The robot then adjusted its grip to keep new objects from sliding. 

Second, Li and colleagues in 2018 joined GelSight tactile pictures with an ordinary camera picture. A convolutional neural network read each picture and a long short-term memory network read how they changed over time. The model said whether the object was slipping. 

Third is a 2019 paper by Lee and colleagues called "Making Sense of Vision and Touch". This model joins a camera picture, a wrist force-torque signal and the arm's joint readings into one set of numbers. It was trained partly without labels, by asking the model to predict things such as whether the gripper will touch something in the next step. A robot used it to learn to fit a peg into a hole. 

Finally, Contactile's PapillArray controller works out when slip starts from its pillar sensors, as described in the frameworks book's section on grippers and hardware. It is a product, not an open model, and the maker does not publish how fast it reacts. 

The frameworks book states the practical point plainly. In September 2026 there was essentially no maintained open-source slip-detection software, and what exists is research code, mostly unlicensed and mostly written for one paper. So if you want slip detection on your robot, plan to train or write it yourself.

The next section gives a worked example of carrying a wet mug. Here is one grip on a wet mug from start to finish. A two-finger gripper with a gel sensor on each pad carries a mug that has just come out of a sink, and the outside is wet, so the friction is low. 

First, the fingers close, and the tactile model says the contact is centred. The program then reads the weight from the wrist sensor and chooses a gentle squeeze. Second, the arm lifts, and the slip model sees a jolt in the force. Because it has seen many lift jolts in training, it says no slip. Third, the arm starts to move the mug sideways, and that movement adds a sideways push. So the dots at the rim of the contact start to slide, and the slip model gives a high number for slip. Fourth, the program responds. It first slows the arm down, because the slip started when the arm sped up. It also squeezes a little harder, after checking that the new force is still below the most the mug can take. Finally, the dots stop sliding, so the slip model's number drops, and the arm carries on more slowly. 

Without the model, the first sign of trouble would be the mug hitting the floor. The order of the responses in the fourth step comes from the frameworks book's page on holding on, which lists the responses from cheapest to most expensive.

The next part covers what goes wrong. The sections above described this kind of model at its best. This list gives the five things that go wrong in practice, and what people do about each one. 

First, jolts that look like slip. Closing, lifting and stopping all shake the signal, so a model that has not seen enough of them calls them slip. People therefore add many jolt examples to the training set, all marked no slip. 

Second, objects unlike the training set. A slip on soft foam looks different from a slip on glass, so a model trained on hard objects may miss slips on soft ones. People therefore train on as wide a range of surfaces as they can. 

Third, watching the wrong sensor. A slip model reading the gripper's finger position cannot see an object sliding down between the pads, because the gap between the fingers does not change. It also cannot see an object turning between the pads. The page on holding on explains why. No amount of training fixes a sensor that cannot observe the event, so people use shear, vibration or a wrist torque reading instead. 

Fourth, being too slow to help. If the model needs a long window, or runs on a slow computer, the answer may come after the object has gone. So people measure the time from slip to answer on the real robot, and not only the accuracy. 

Finally, missing readings. If the sensor stops sending readings, a careless program treats the silence as no slip. A missing reading must be treated as unknown, and the program should stop or refuse, as the section on never falling back silently explains.

The next section explains why you would use this rather than the obvious alternative, and what it costs. It weighs the problems just listed against the alternatives. The obvious alternative is a fixed rule on the force signal, for example, if the sideways force is more than half the straight-in force, call it slip. This rule comes from the simple physics of friction, so it needs no data, it is easy to check, and it is right for many rigid objects. However, it needs the friction number of the pad and the object together, which you rarely know and which changes when the surface is wet or dusty. It also cannot tell a jolt from a slip. 

A second alternative is a second look with a camera after the grip. After the lift, a wrist camera takes a picture and checks that the object has not moved, and this needs no extra hardware and catches every kind of movement. However, it only works after the movement has happened, so it cannot catch a slip in time to stop it. 

So a learned slip model is worth it when the rule fails because friction is unknown or changing, and when the answer is needed before the object moves far. Wet, oily, dusty or unfamiliar objects are the main cases. 

What it costs you is first, a sensor that can see slip, which means a tactile sensor, a fast force sensor on the pad, or a vibration sensor, because the gripper's own finger position is not enough. Second, a training set of deliberate slips, which takes many hours of robot time. Third, your own software, since there is almost none to download. Fourth, testing for speed on the real robot, not only for accuracy. And finally, no guarantee. The model can miss a slip it has never seen. A program should still confirm, with the wrist sensor, that the object has the expected weight before a long carry.

The next part discusses the written alternative. This page has argued for learning the model, so the last question is when a written rule is enough. The written alternative is the fixed rule just mentioned, built with the tools in the sensor streams page. That page shows how to smooth a force reading, find how fast it is changing, and turn it into a flag that does not flicker on and off. The page on slip, and the checks that cannot fire, explains which sensors can see slip at all. The written rule wins for rigid objects with a known, steady friction, because it needs no data and is easy to check. The slip model wins when the friction is unknown or changing, as with wet, oily or dusty objects.

The final section is about using it in Python. Earlier sections said that you will almost certainly train this model yourself, and the chapter overview shows that training loop, because it is the same one for every kind in this chapter. So this section shows the other half, which is the part that runs while the arm carries the mug. After reading it you will be able to turn a stream of force readings into the flag that the worked example acts on. 

The code starts by loading a saved neural network and switching off the parts that only train. It sets up a window that keeps only the last twenty readings, throwing the oldest away automatically so you never have to trim a list. Then it enters a loop that runs while the robot is carrying the object. Inside the loop, it reads the wrist force and adds it to the window. If the window is not full yet, it waits for more readings. Once the window is full, it arranges the numbers into the order the network expects, because the training laid each window out one sensor at a time. It passes this window to the network without keeping any gradients, which makes it faster because nothing is being learned. 

The network outputs a raw number, which is turned into a chance of slipping between zero and one. To prevent the slip flag from flickering on and off, the code uses two limits. The flag turns on if the chance goes above zero point eight, and it only turns off again if the chance drops below zero point four. If the flag is on, the code calls a function to slow down and squeeze a little harder. 

PyTorch gives you three things here that are easy to miss. The functions to evaluate and use inference mode switch off everything that belongs to training, and make each answer faster, because the network no longer keeps the extra numbers it would need in order to learn. The sigmoid function turns the network's raw output into the number between zero and one. Python's own double-ended queue is the window that manages its own length. 

What you have to write yourself is the function to read the wrist force and the function to slow down and squeeze harder, and both of them are specific to your hardware. The first talks to your force sensor or your tactile sensor, and the second is the response that the frameworks book puts in order of cost. You also have to produce the saved model file, because there is no slip model to download. 

What you have to decide is the two limits and the window length, and the section on what goes wrong says why you cannot choose them from accuracy alone. Measure how long the whole loop takes on the computer that will sit next to the arm, from the reading arriving to the flag turning on, because a model that is right but answers after the mug has gone is no use. If the loop is too slow, shorten the window before you shrink the network, since a shorter window cuts the delay directly.
