Planning and search. This chapter is about finding a way for the arm to get from here to there without hitting anything, and about choosing well among many possible moves. It covers six techniques: sampling-based planning, numerical inverse kinematics, trajectory optimisation, graph search, sampling-based optimisation with model-predictive control, and visibility with next-best-view planning. Each one is a written set of rules that a program follows, so none of them is trained on examples.

This page is the overview of the chapter, and it answers four questions. The first two are what job these techniques do for an arm, and what each of the six does in one line. The other two are how they work together on one real move, and how this chapter connects to the rest of the book and to the learned planners of Book 6.

It is for a reader who knows what a joint, a joint angle, and a frame are, at the level of the earlier pages on frames and transforms and forward kinematics. You do not need to have met any of the algorithms before, because each page introduces its own from the start.

The first part of the page explains what planning and search is for. The book's map of techniques describes this category in one line, as finding a way for the arm to get from here to there without hitting anything. So this section says what that one line means once a real arm has to obey it.

Here is the job in everyday terms. Imagine that you are carrying a tray through a kitchen, and that you know where you are and where the table is. Between you and the table there are chairs, an open cupboard door, and a dog. So you work out a route before you move, and you pick one that keeps the tray clear of the cupboard door. In the same way, an arm has this job every time it moves in a place that has other things in it.

For an arm, the question has three parts. First, where should the joints end up? The goal is usually given as a position and a direction for the gripper, in metres and angles, but the motors need joint angles, so something must turn the one into the other. Second, which way should the arm go? There are many ways from the current joint angles to the goal joint angles, and some of them swing the arm through the box on the table, so the route must avoid those. Finally, is the route good enough to run? A route that only just misses the box, or that takes a long detour, may be allowed but still be a poor choice, so something must make it shorter, smoother, or further from the box.

The first four techniques in this chapter answer these three parts between them. Numerical inverse kinematics answers the first, while graph search and sampling-based planning answer the second, and trajectory optimisation answers the third and sometimes the second as well.

The other two techniques then answer questions that come up around a move. Sampling-based optimisation and model-predictive control answer which move is best right now when the cost is awkward or the world keeps changing. This is done by trying many moves in a model and re-planning every few moments. Visibility and next-best-view planning answer where the camera should go, which is a planning problem whose goal is to see something rather than to reach it.

What happens after that is the next chapter's job, because the pages on control and motion take the route and turn it into timed, smooth motor commands.

The next section explains where the search happens, which is the space of joint angles. To answer the second of those three parts, a planner for an arm does not search the room itself, but searches the space of joint angles instead. This one fact explains most of how planners behave, and the later page on planning a path starts from it too.

Every set of joint angles is one point in this space. So a two-joint arm has a two-dimensional space, with one axis for joint 1 and one for joint 2, while a six-joint arm has a six-dimensional space. This space is called the configuration space, and one set of joint angles in it is called a configuration.

Once the space is drawn this way, each object in the room blocks some of these points. A configuration counts as blocked when the arm, standing in that configuration, would overlap the object. So a plain box on a table blocks a curved band of configurations rather than a box. The page on sampling-based planning draws that band for the book's two-joint arm.

Why does the number of joints matter so much? One simple way to search is to cut the space into small squares, called cells, and then search the cells. A diagram on the page counts the cells when each joint is cut into ten-degree steps, which gives thirty-six steps per joint. It shows that every extra joint multiplies the number of cells by thirty-six. Two joints give one thousand, two hundred and ninety-six cells, but six joints give more than two billion cells. Each of those cells needs its own collision check before the search can use it. So a grid search, which is the subject of the graph search page, is a good tool only for two or three joints, or for the gripper's position alone. Instead, for a six-joint arm the usual tool is a sampling-based planner, which tests a few thousand random configurations rather than every cell.

Because that many joints rule out a plain grid search, the chapter's pages are split into two groups. The most-used group holds the three techniques that run on nearly every arm move. In it, a sampling-based planner finds the route, inverse kinematics turns a gripper goal into joint angles, and trajectory optimisation tidies the route. The also-used group then holds three techniques that are common but are needed for fewer jobs. Those three are graph search for small spaces and fixed roadmaps, sampling-based optimisation and model-predictive control for fast re-planning, and visibility with next-best-view planning for moving a camera.

Here is each technique in one line. First, the most-used techniques. Sampling-based planning tries random joint configurations, keeps the ones that hit nothing, and joins them up until a route appears. The rapidly-exploring random tree, or RRT, RRT-Connect, and the probabilistic roadmap are the main methods, and this is what MoveIt 2 uses by default. Numerical inverse kinematics finds joint angles that put the gripper at a given position and direction. It starts from a guess and sees how far the gripper is from the target. It then moves the joints a little to close the gap, and repeats until the gap is small enough. Trajectory optimisation starts from a whole route and improves it step by step, so that the route becomes shorter and smoother and is pushed away from obstacles. CHOMP, STOMP, and TrajOpt are the well-known methods that do this.

Next, the also-used techniques. Graph search finds the shortest or cheapest route through a network of places joined by moves, and breadth-first search, Dijkstra's algorithm, and A-star are the three main methods. On an arm it searches a grid of cells, or a small set of taught poses joined by known moves. The same page covers topological sort, which puts jobs in an order that respects what must happen before what, such as which object must be moved before another can be reached. Sampling-based optimisation and model-predictive control tries many random sequences of moves in a model, keeps the best, and runs only the first step before planning again. Random shooting, the cross-entropy method, and CMA-ES are the main ways to choose the samples, and model-predictive control, or MPC, is the plan-a-little, act-a-little loop around them. Finally, visibility and next-best-view works out what a camera can and cannot see from a pose. It then chooses where to put the camera next, so that the camera sees the most of what is still unknown.

To show where these techniques differ, the page provides a table that puts the six of them side by side. It lists what goes into each technique, what comes out, whether it gives the same answer every run, and the usual way it fails. For example, sampling-based planning takes in a collision checker along with a start and goal configuration, and outputs a route that hits nothing, though usually with detours. Because it is random, it does not give the same answer every run, and it usually fails when there is a narrow gap that random samples rarely land in. In contrast, numerical inverse kinematics takes a target pose and a first guess, and outputs joint angles. It gives the same answer from the same guess, and usually fails if no answer is found near a bad guess or near a joint limit.

Two things in the table are worth saying in words, because they change how you use the techniques. First, the two sampling methods give a different answer each run, and the later page on planning a path explains why that matters when a cell has to be checked and signed off. Second, the techniques fail in different ways, so they are often used together. A sampler is good at finding some way through an awkward space, whereas an optimiser is good at making a route nice. But an optimiser can get stuck on the wrong side of an obstacle, so a common pair is a sampler that finds a route and an optimiser, or a simple shortening step, that then cleans it up.

To show that pairing in action, a diagram follows one move of the book's two-joint arm. In it, link 1 is three metres long and link 2 is two metres long. There is a box, a post, and a jar on the table, and the arm must move its gripper to a star. The diagram has two panels. The left panel is the table seen from above, while the right panel is the configuration space. Each coloured band on the right is the set of joint angles where the arm would touch the object of the same colour on the left.

The move happens in three steps. First, inverse kinematics turns the target into joint angles. The target is a point for the gripper, and two sets of joint angles reach it: position A, at 130 degrees and 20 degrees, and position B, at 146 degrees and minus 20 degrees. They differ in which way the elbow bends, and the program picks A.

Second, a planner searches for a free route. The straight line from the current joint angles to A crosses two bands, so the arm would hit the post and then the box. Instead, an RRT-Connect planner finds a route that goes over the top of both bands. On the table, that means the arm folds its elbow sharply to pass inside the post and the box. That route is 384 degrees long, counting the joint turns added together.

Third, a shortening step makes the route tidier. It tries to replace parts of the route with straight lines, keeping each one only if it hits nothing, and the route drops to 250 degrees, with two corners.

The shortening step used here is the simplest kind, so the trajectory optimisation page covers the methods that also make the route smooth and keep it clear of obstacles. But after step three, the route is still only a list of joint angles, because it has no times and no speeds attached to it. Trajectory generation adds those, so that the motors can follow it.

The next part of the page explains how this chapter connects to the others. That one move used a camera goal, a collision checker, and a motor command, which shows how planning sits in the middle of the book's seven categories. So it takes input from the first four categories and hands its result to the sixth.

Geometry and cameras gives the planner its goal, because a camera first finds the object in pixels. Then the pinhole camera model and rigid transforms turn those pixels into a pose in the arm's frame, which inverse kinematics turns into joint angles. Searching and matching provides a piece that every sampling planner uses on every step, namely nearest-neighbour search, which finds the closest point already in the tree. Fitting and estimation, along with image and point cloud processing, build the obstacles that the planner avoids. So a table plane fitted with RANSAC, or a cluster of points from clustering, becomes a box in the planner's list of obstacles. Control and motion takes the route from this chapter and makes the motors follow it. Finally, decisions and task logic decides which move to plan next, and when the choice is between several places to go, it may use graph search itself.

Book 6 has learned versions of some of these techniques, because the page on learned motion planners covers networks that suggest routes, networks that check for collisions, and networks that do inverse kinematics. That page's advice agrees with this chapter, since for moving through open space an ordinary planner is usually the better tool. This is because it is fast enough, it checks every move, and it needs no training. So learned helpers earn their place only when the same kind of problem comes up many thousands of times and the answer is needed in the same short time every run.

Because the six pages do not have to be read in the order they are listed, the page ends with a reading order that introduces each idea before it is used. Start with graph search, because it explains breadth-first search, Dijkstra's algorithm, and A-star, which the other pages then use. Then read sampling-based planning, which is how most arm planners find a route. Trajectory optimisation and numerical inverse kinematics complete the most-used group. Next, sampling-based optimisation and MPC covers re-planning a short way ahead, many times a second. Visibility and next-best-view covers choosing where to point the camera. After that, the Book 3 page on planning a path covers the same planners in practice, including MoveIt's defaults, the planning scene, and the ways a planner can succeed and still leave you worse off. Finally, the page on programmed methods for one arm places motion planning among the other ways to program an arm.
