PID control. This page explains proportional-integral-derivative control, which is the standard way to make one joint of an arm go to a target angle and stay there. It answers four questions. What do the three parts of a PID controller each do, and how does a program run one, tick by tick? Where does a robot arm use it, and what goes wrong when it is set up badly? 

You need to know what a joint angle and a motor are, and that a speed is how fast something changes, but you do not need any control theory or calculus. Every number on this page comes from a real run of a simulation script. PID is the most used control technique in robotics, running inside the drive of nearly every joint, gripper, and camera head. It is usually the first thing to try, and often the last thing you need.

The first section answers why this is necessary. A motor does not know anything about angles. All it produces is a torque, which is a turning force measured in newton metres, in proportion to the electric current sent to it. If you want a joint at 30 degrees, something has to choose the torque that gets it there. This is hard because gravity pulls the link down, friction resists movement, and the load changes. A torque worked out in advance will be wrong. The answer is to measure and correct, over and over. An encoder reports the joint angle, so a controller can read it, compare it with the target, and choose a torque. Doing this every millisecond is called a feedback loop, and PID is simply the rule that the loop uses to turn the error into a torque.

The next section gives the core idea in one sentence. A PID controller sets the motor torque to the sum of three terms: one in proportion to the error now, one in proportion to the error added up over time, and one in proportion to how fast the error is changing. You can think of it like the cruise control in a car set to 100 kilometres per hour. If the car is going 90, the controller presses the accelerator in proportion to the 10 kilometre per hour gap. That is the proportional part. On a long hill, the car might settle at 97. The controller notices the gap has lasted a long time and slowly presses harder until it is gone. That is the integral part. When the car is gaining speed quickly, the controller eases off before reaching 100 so it does not shoot past. That is the derivative part. A joint controller does exactly the same thing with angles and torques.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step. To make the three terms easy to compare, every diagram uses the same simulated joint. It has an inertia of 0.05 kilogram square metres, friction of 0.5 newton metres per radian per second, and a steady pull of gravity of 1.0 newton metre. The controller runs one thousand times a second, and each run is called a tick. In every example, the joint rests at zero and is told to jump to 0.5 radians. This sudden change of target is called a step.

The first term is P, which pushes in proportion to the error. The error is the target angle minus the measured angle. The proportional term is that error multiplied by a number called the proportional gain, or K p. With K p set to 20, an error of 0.5 radians gives a torque of 10 newton metres. As the joint gets closer, the error shrinks, so the push shrinks with it. A diagram shows what P alone does. It overshoots the target by 31 percent because nothing slows the joint down on its way in. Then it turns back and stops short, coming to rest 0.05 radians below the target. This stopping short is called the steady-state error. It happens because the motor needs 1.0 newton metre to hold the link up against gravity, and a P controller only produces torque when there is an error. A larger K p makes this leftover error smaller, but makes the overshoot worse.

Because the P term leaves that steady-state error behind, the second term, I, pushes harder while an error lasts. The integral term adds up the error over time. On each tick, it adds the error times the length of the tick to a running total. That total, multiplied by the integral gain, K i, is the integral term. While any error remains, the total keeps growing, finding the exact force gravity needs. A diagram shows PI control with K p at 20 and K i at 30. The joint now reaches the exact target, but the overshoot is worse, at 42 percent, and the joint rings for about a second before settling. This happens because the integral term keeps pushing on the way in, having built up a large total while the joint was still short of the target.

To fix that overshoot, the third term, D, brakes when the error is changing fast. The derivative term looks at the rate of change of the error, multiplied by the derivative gain, K d. When the joint is rushing towards the target, the error is shrinking fast, so D produces a large torque in the opposite direction. This works as a brake that is strongest when the joint is fastest. A diagram shows full PID control with K p at 20, K i at 30, and K d at 1. The overshoot drops to just 4 percent, and the joint settles exactly on the target in less than half a second. 

A table collects these three runs for comparison. It shows that P alone overshoots by 31 percent and never reaches the target. PI overshoots by 42 percent and takes over a second to settle. Full PID overshoots by only 4 percent and settles in 0.38 seconds with almost zero error.

Another diagram splits the PID run into its three terms over the first second. At the start, the P term does almost all the work, providing 10 newton metres. Then the D term quickly drops to minus 4 newton metres to brake while the joint is moving fast. Meanwhile, the I term grows slowly and levels off at exactly 1.0 newton metre to counter gravity. Once the joint is at rest on the target, P and D are zero, and the I term alone holds the link up. The page notes one important detail: real controllers take the rate of change of the measured angle rather than the error. This avoids sending a massive kick of torque when the target suddenly jumps.

The page then walks through the first ticks of the PID run by hand. At tick zero, the error is 0.5, so P is 10 newton metres. The integral total is tiny, and the joint has not moved, so D is zero. The total torque is just over 10 newton metres. By tick one, a thousandth of a second later, the joint has moved slightly. P drops a fraction, I grows a fraction, but D is now negative because the joint has speed, subtracting about 0.1 newton metres. By tick one hundred, at 0.1 seconds, the joint is moving fast. P has fallen to 3.24, I has grown to 1.07, and D is braking hard at minus 3.34. The total torque is now less than gravity, so the joint is already slowing down.

These steps are then shown as a short piece of pseudocode. Every tick, the code reads the encoder and finds the error. It calculates the P term, adds to the running total for the I term, and calculates the speed for the D term. It adds the three together, clips the total torque to what the motor can actually give, and sends it to the motor.

Choosing the three gains is called tuning, and it is usually done by hand. First, you set K i and K d to zero, and raise K p until the joint reaches the target quickly with a little overshoot. Second, you raise K d until the overshoot is gone. Third, you raise K i until the steady-state error disappears. Finally, you test with the heaviest and lightest loads. The page mentions the Ziegler-Nichols method as a formal starting point, but notes it is usually too aggressive for an arm. A large improvement often comes from adding gravity compensation. If you know the pull of gravity from the arm's mass model, you can add it to the torque directly as feed-forward, leaving the I term with almost nothing to do.

Every real PID needs two fixes because real joints have limits and noise. The first fault is integral windup. If a large move asks for more torque than the motor can give, the joint moves slowly, the error stays large, and the integral total grows massively. A diagram shows this causing a huge 35 percent overshoot that takes over two seconds to settle. The fix is anti-windup, which pauses the running total while the motor is at its limit. The diagram shows that with this fix, the joint does not overshoot at all and settles in half a second. 

The second fault is derivative noise. Encoders count steps, so they do not report a smooth angle. When the angle jumps by one count, the measured speed spikes, and the D term turns that into a sudden spike of torque. A diagram shows the D term from a real encoder as a train of massive spikes, which would make a motor hot and noisy. The fix is a low-pass filter, a small calculation that smooths away fast changes. The diagram shows the filtered D term is smooth, dropping the noise significantly while barely changing the step response.

The next section explains where PID is used on a robot arm. It is used almost everywhere a measured number must be held at a target. First, in the joint position loop, often as a cascade where a position loop chooses a speed, a speed loop chooses a current, and a current loop sets the motor current. Second, for following a moving trajectory, where feed-forward helps reduce lag. Third, in electric gripper fingers, running a position loop on the finger gap. Fourth, for pointing a camera, using a P or PI loop to keep an object in the middle of the picture. Fifth, in admittance controllers to hold a specific force. Finally, to keep conveyors and turntables at a steady speed.

The page then covers where PID is useful, and where it is not. PID works best when the system responds steadily, the sensor is fast and clean, and the target changes smoothly. A table lists common failures. If the load changes a lot, people add gravity compensation or a learned arm model. If the sensor is slow or delayed, like a camera, they lower the gains or use a Kalman filter. If there is sticking friction in the gearbox, they add friction compensation. The most important failure is contact with a rigid surface. A PID position loop will push as hard as the motor allows to reach a target inside a solid table, because pushing until the error is gone is exactly what it is for. The fix for this is impedance or force control, which is covered on another page, not a better PID.

The next section lists libraries that provide PID control, because anti-windup and filtering are easy to get wrong. For C++, there is ros2 controllers and the control toolbox. For Python, simple-pid is good for scripts, and python-control is used for offline testing. Gazebo and MuJoCo have PID built into their simulated joints. On a real arm, you usually use the arm maker's tuned PID for the joints, and only write your own for outer loops like a camera head or a gripper.

The following part summarizes why PID is chosen, and what it costs. It gives a joint the ability to reach a target and hold it against gravity and friction using only its own sensor and three numbers. The alternative of open-loop control uses no feedback, but fails because real arms never perfectly match their models. Computed-torque control uses a full physics model and is highly accurate, but it is very hard to write and needs exact mass measurements. PID needs no model and is easy to tune. The costs are that you must tune the gains, they change with the load, you need anti-windup and filtering, and a position loop has no idea of contact.

The next section discusses the learned alternative. A neural network is almost never fast enough to sit inside a millisecond loop. Instead, a learned policy runs slower and gives targets for the PID to follow. A learned arm model can predict the required torque, including complex friction, and add it to the PID output as feed-forward. Learning can also pick the gains themselves, using Bayesian optimisation to test and score gains on the real joint to find good ones quickly.

The final section shows how to use PID in Python. It uses the simple-pid library because it is pure Python and easy to install. The code creates a PID object, passing in the three gains, the target angle, and the motor's torque limits. Then, inside a continuous loop, it passes the current joint angle and the time step to the PID object, which calculates the torque. Finally, it sends that torque to the motor. The library handles the bookkeeping: it clamps the integral sum to prevent windup, clamps the output, and calculates the derivative from the measurement rather than the error. However, you still have to write the code that reads the encoder and sends the torque. You must ensure the loop runs at a steady rate, and you must tune the three gains yourself on the real joint, because copying them from another arm will not work.
