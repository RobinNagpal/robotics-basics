The pinhole camera model. This page explains the pinhole camera model as a technique you can use in any program, and it answers four questions. How does a camera turn a point in the room into a pixel? How do you turn a pixel and a depth reading back into a point? How many millimetres does one pixel cover? And how do you find a point when the camera gives no depth at all?

It is for a reader who has read Book 2's pages on camera basics, which explains pixels, focal length and the four lens numbers from the start, and the page on finding one box, which works through one pixel slowly. This page does not repeat those explanations, but instead collects the formulas into one method, adds the parts those pages leave out, and shows where the method is used on an arm.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. Every pixel looks out from one point along one straight line, and a camera records only which line, never how far along it.

The one point is called the pinhole, or the camera's optical centre, and in a real camera it is the middle of the lens. The straight line through the pinhole and a pixel is called that pixel's ray. Here is an everyday example. Hold up a finger at arm's length, close one eye, and line the finger up with a distant tree. Both the finger and the tree then lie on the same line from your eye, so they land on the same spot in your eye and your one eye cannot tell which is nearer. A camera has the same limit, and the pinhole model is the arithmetic of that line.

The diagram for this section shows two rays coming out of a pinhole, drawn from the side, to show that all points along one ray land on the same pixel. A blue ray goes straight ahead and lands on the middle of the picture, at pixel u equals 160. An orange ray lands on pixel u equals 212.5. Three red points sit on that orange ray at 0.20 metres, 0.34 metres, and 0.50 metres ahead. So they are different points in the room, but all three land on the exact same pixel.

The next part of the page explains how it works. The whole model is two formulas, one for each direction, and this section writes them out, puts them in matrix form, and then adds the practical pieces around them.

Every number here uses Book 2's simulated camera, whose picture is 320 by 240 pixels. Its four lens numbers are a focal length of 277.1 pixels for both fx and fy, and a centre point of cx equals 160 and cy equals 120. It hangs 0.40 metres above the middle of a table and looks straight down.

The first formula is projection, which takes a point measured from the camera and gives the pixel it lands on. The camera's axes follow the picture, so x points right, y points down, and z points straight out of the lens, and this frame is called the camera's optical frame.

The formula is: u equals fx times x divided by z, plus cx. And v equals fy times y divided by z, plus cy. In other words, divide the sideways distance by the distance ahead, multiply by the focal length in pixels, and add the position of the middle of the picture.

For example, take a spot on top of a box that is at x equals 0.06442 metres, y equals negative 0.04110 metres, and z equals 0.340 metres from the camera. For u, you multiply 277.1 by the ratio of x to z, and add 160, which gives 212.5. For v, you multiply 277.1 by the ratio of y to z, and add 120, which gives 86.5. So the spot lands on pixel 212.5 comma 86.5. If you double all three distances, the ratios of x to z and y to z do not change, so the pixel does not change either. That is the ray from the earlier picture, written as arithmetic, and the division by z is exactly where the distance is lost.

This same rule can be written as one matrix. Libraries store the four lens numbers as a three by three grid called the intrinsic matrix, written as a capital K. Book 2 shows it arriving in ROS's CameraInfo message, in the section on K in detail. The matrix has fx, zero, and cx in the top row; zero, fy, and cy in the middle row; and zero, zero, one in the bottom row.

Projection is then one multiplication followed by one division. That means you multiply K by the point to get three numbers, and then divide the first two by the third. For our point, multiplying K by the coordinates gives 72.25, 29.41, and 0.340. Dividing the first two by the third gives the same pixel, 212.5 comma 86.5. The third number is the depth, so the division by it is the step that loses distance. You will see this form in every library and in papers, where it is often written with a scale factor: s times the vector u, v, 1 equals K times the vector x, y, z, with s equal to z.

Back-projection runs the formula the other way, and it is also called deprojection. However, it needs one extra number, which is the depth reading for that pixel from a depth camera. The formula is: x equals u minus cx, times depth, divided by fx. And y equals v minus cy, times depth, divided by fy. Finally, z equals the depth.

For pixel 212.5 comma 86.5 with a depth of 0.340 metres, x becomes 212.5 minus 160, times 0.340, divided by 277.1, which equals 0.06442 metres. Doing the same for y gives negative 0.04110 metres, and z is 0.340 metres. That is the point we started from, so the two formulas undo each other exactly. Book 2 walks through the same four steps with pictures of the two similar triangles in the section on pixel plus depth giving back the point.

However, two details decide whether the answer is right in practice. First, the depth must be the distance straight ahead, along z, not the distance along the slanted ray. Nearly every depth camera reports it this way, and the page on camera basics explains why. If a sensor reports the slanted distance instead, you must divide it by the length of the ray direction first. Second, u and v should be the middle of the pixel, because pixel number 212 covers the strip from 212 to 213 and its middle is therefore 212.5. Using 212 instead moves the point by half a pixel, which is 0.61 millimetres at this depth.

Because a depth camera gives a depth for every pixel, back-projecting all of them gives a point cloud, which is a list of 3D points, one per pixel. The page includes a diagram showing one row of depth readings becoming one row of points. It shows pixels across a table and a box. On the left is what the camera gives, which is one depth per pixel, and on the right is what back-projection makes, which is one point per pixel placed along its ray. The table pixels read 0.400 metres while the box-top pixels read 0.340 metres. To the right of the box there is a gap in the points, because the box hides that strip of table from the camera, so no pixel ever looks at it. This missing strip is called a shadow, and every point cloud from one camera has them.

However, in a program you rarely loop over pixels one at a time. Instead, you compute the x and y factors once for every pixel, and multiply the whole depth picture by them in one step. The factors never change while the camera and resolution stay the same, so they can be stored.

Back-projection also tells you how much of the world one pixel covers. At depth z, moving one pixel to the side moves the point by z divided by fx metres. For example, at 0.340 metres, one pixel covers 0.340 divided by 277.1, which is 1.227 millimetres. At 0.400 metres, it covers 1.444 millimetres. This is the most useful single number for judging whether a camera can do a job. If a gap you must measure is 1 millimetre wide, and one pixel covers 1.4 millimetres, no program can measure it.

A chart on the page shows how much of the table one pixel covers, against depth. It shows two straight lines rising as depth increases, one for the standard camera and one for a sensor with twice the pixels across. Both lines are straight because the width grows in proportion to depth, and doubling the resolution halves the width at every depth. The same rule also measures size. A box top that is 6 centimetres wide at a depth of 0.340 metres covers 60 millimetres divided by 1.227 millimetres per pixel, which is 48.9 pixels. Read the other way, a box 48.9 pixels wide at that depth is 6 centimetres wide.

The next part explains what to do with no depth, by finding where a pixel's ray meets a known plane. A colour-only camera gives no depth, but if you know the surface the object lies on, you can still find the point. The ray from the pixel is known and the plane of the table is known, so the point is where the two meet.

The method has three steps. First, turn the pixel into a ray direction in the camera's frame. Second, turn that direction into the room's frame with the camera's rotation, which the rigid transforms page explains. The ray now starts at the camera's position and runs along the turned direction. Third, find how far along the ray the table is. For a table at height zero, the point is the camera position plus the distance along the ray.

A diagram shows this with a tilted camera, which is the usual case on a wrist. The camera is 0.40 metres above the table and 0.20 metres back from its middle, looking forward and 60 degrees down. The middle pixel looks straight along the camera's axis, and its ray meets the table after a depth of 0.4619 metres. Another pixel meets the table after 0.4347 metres. Neither number came from a depth sensor, because both came from the pixel, the lens numbers, the camera's pose, and the fact that the table is flat. However, the method breaks the moment the object is not on the plane you assumed. For example, a pixel on top of a box 6 centimetres tall would give a point on the table behind the box instead.

The next section covers lens distortion, which is where the pinhole stops being true. A real lens bends light a little, so straight lines in the room come out slightly curved in the picture. The pinhole model assumes no bending, so real programs correct the bending first and then use the pinhole formulas on the corrected pixels, and that correction step is called undistortion. This bending is described by a few more numbers, usually five, called the distortion coefficients. They are measured by calibration, which also shows how large the bending can be: several pixels near the corners of a wide lens.

The section ends with pseudocode for the whole technique. It defines four functions using the lens numbers. The first function projects a point to a pixel. It checks if the point is behind the camera or on it, and if so, it stops. Otherwise, it calculates u and v using the projection formulas and returns them. The second function back-projects a pixel and a depth to a point. It checks if the depth is missing or less than or equal to zero, and stops if it is. Otherwise, it calculates x and y and returns the 3D point. The third function turns a whole depth picture into points. It creates an empty list, then loops over every pixel, calling the back-projection function with the pixel's middle coordinates and its depth. If it gets a valid point, it adds it to the list. The fourth function finds where a ray meets a plane. It calculates the ray direction, rotates it into the room's frame, and checks if the ray runs parallel to the plane or if the plane is behind the camera. If both checks pass, it returns the point where the ray hits the plane.

Note the checks in that code. A missing depth reading is common on shiny or clear objects, and many cameras report it as zero. So back-projecting a zero puts a point right on the lens, which later code will happily treat as a real object.

The third section explains where this is used on a robot arm. The pinhole model is under almost every camera measurement on an arm. First, it is used for turning a detection into a grasp target. A detector gives a box around a mug, the program takes the middle pixel, reads its depth, and back-projects it. That point, moved into the base frame, is where the gripper goes. Second, it makes a point cloud for the rest of the pipeline. Plane removal, clustering, nearest-neighbour search and ICP all start from back-projected points. Third, it is used for measuring sizes. The width of an object in pixels, times depth divided by fx, gives its width in metres. Fourth, it places a colour-only camera's detections on the table. A cheap camera without depth can still find where a part lies, by meeting each pixel's ray with the table plane. Fifth, it checks a plan by drawing it. Projection runs the other way, so to check that the arm's planned grasp point is on the object, you project it into the picture and draw a dot. Sixth, it decides whether a pose can see an object. A planner projects each object's corners, and if they land outside the picture dimensions, the view cannot see the object. Finally, it checks contact from above. When the gripper touches a part, a wrist camera can project the fingertips' known positions into the picture to see whether they line up with the part's edges.

The fourth section covers where it works, and where it does not. The pinhole model is exact for an ideal camera, but real cameras differ from it in a few ways, and each difference shows itself with a sign you can look for. The page provides a table of what goes wrong, what you see, and what to do. For example, if lens distortion is ignored, straight edges bow, and points near the picture's corners are off by several millimetres. The solution is to calibrate, then undistort the picture or the pixels first. If you use lens numbers from the wrong resolution, every point is too far out or too close to the middle. You must scale the lens numbers with the picture. If colour and depth pictures are not lined up, the object's points are shifted to one side. You should use the camera's aligned depth stream. If there is no depth reading on glass, chrome or black parts, you will see holes in the point cloud, or points on the lens at depth zero. You should skip zero depths or use the table-plane method. If you have a very wide lens, such as a fisheye, the five usual distortion numbers cannot describe the bending, so you must use a fisheye camera model instead. Overall, the pinhole model works well for any ordinary camera lens with a field of view up to about 90 degrees, after calibration. That covers almost every camera that is fixed on or near a robot arm.

The fifth section lists libraries that provide these formulas, because you rarely need to write them yourself. OpenCV provides functions for projection and undistortion in C++ and Python, and is the reference for projection with distortion. Open3D can turn a whole depth picture into a point cloud in one call. ROS provides the image geometry package, which reads the lens numbers straight from a CameraInfo message, and the depth image proc package, which publishes a point cloud from a depth topic without any code. The Intel RealSense SDK applies the camera's own distortion model. You can also use plain array arithmetic in NumPy for Python, or plain matrix arithmetic in Eigen for C++. Whatever you use, check which frame it expects. OpenCV and image geometry use the optical frame, with z ahead and y down, while a robot model's camera link frame has x ahead and z up instead.

The sixth section explains why to use this model, and what it costs. It is the rule that a camera is one point with rays out of it, and that a pixel records only which ray. So it lets you go from a point to a pixel and, with a depth, from a pixel back to a point, using four numbers that the camera reports. The obvious alternative is a lookup table: put a marker at many known places, record which pixel each lands on, and interpolate between them. Some old industrial systems did this, and it needs no model at all. However, it only works at the heights where you measured, it must be redone whenever the camera moves, and it needs hundreds of measurements. The pinhole model, in contrast, needs four numbers, works at every depth, and moves with the camera through one transform. The cost is that the model is only as good as its four numbers and its distortion numbers, so you must calibrate. It also needs a depth reading or a known plane, because the camera alone never records distance. And it describes one camera only, so a stereo pair or a colour camera beside a depth sensor needs one model per lens, plus the transform between them.

The seventh section discusses the learned alternative. No learned model replaces the pinhole model itself, because learned models that measure need the same rule. Models that guess depth from colour alone, or find named points on an object, still go through the projection or back-projection formula on this page. The real alternative is to skip the camera model and train a policy, which is a network that turns pictures straight into arm movements. Such a policy absorbs distortion and odd lenses without anyone describing them. But it needs tens to hundreds of demonstrations for each task, and a camera moved by a few centimetres can confuse it. It also cannot tell you when it is wrong, while every number in the pinhole model can be checked with a ruler.
