Pose from points: Perspective-n-Point. 

This page explains how a program finds an object's full pose from one colour picture. The object's pose is where it is and which way it is turned. That means three numbers for its position and three for its rotation, making six in all. Robotics people call this a six degrees of freedom pose. The method on this page is called Perspective-n-Point, or PnP, and it answers four questions. What does PnP need? How does it find the pose? What happens when some of its inputs are wrong? And why is a flat printed marker hard to read when it faces the camera squarely?

The page assumes you already know about the pinhole camera model, which turns a point into a pixel, and rigid transforms, which describe a pose as a rotation and a shift. It also builds on the page about calibration, which already uses PnP once to find the board in each picture. This page opens that step up. Every number here comes from a real run of a diagram script using a specific camera. That camera has a resolution of 320 by 240 pixels, a focal length of 277.1 for both fx and fy, and an optical centre at 160 for cx and 120 for cy.

The idea in one sentence is that if you know where some points are on an object, and you can see which pixels they land on, there is only one pose of the object that puts all of them on those pixels. 

Think of an everyday example. You are lost in a town, but you can see a church tower, a bridge, and a tall chimney. You know where each one is on your map, and you can see in which direction each one lies from where you stand. Only one spot on the map gives those three directions at once, so that spot is where you are. Sailors call this taking a fix. PnP is the same idea. The map is the object's own shape, written as points in millimetres. The directions are the pixels, because each pixel is a direction from the camera. The answer is the object's pose relative to the camera, which can also be turned around to find the camera's pose relative to the object.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step, starting with what goes in and what comes out. PnP needs three things before it can start. First, it needs the object's points, measured in the object's own frame. For example, the corners of a 6 centimetre cube would be at plus or minus 30 millimetres in the x and y directions, coming from a drawing or a printed pattern. Second, it needs the pixel where each of those points appears. PnP does not look at the picture itself; something else, like a corner detector or a neural network, finds these pixels. Third, it needs the camera's lens numbers and its distortion, which calibration measures. 

Each point must be paired with its own pixel, and that pairing is called a correspondence. PnP trusts the pairing completely. What comes out is a rotation and a shift, which together make a transform that moves a point from the object's frame into the camera's frame.

The next step relies on reprojection error. Suppose you guess a pose. With that guess, you can place each object point in the camera's frame and project it to a pixel. The distance between where a point reprojects and where it was really seen is its reprojection error, measured in pixels. PnP searches for the pose with the smallest reprojection error. It combines the errors of all the points into one number called the root mean square. To find this, you square each distance, take the mean, and take the square root. When the pose is right, this number is about as large as the noise in the pixels, usually a few tenths of a pixel.

Because the projection formula divides by depth, the error is not a simple straight-line function of the pose. So, PnP works in two stages: a first guess, then refinement. For the first guess, a short formula gives an approximate pose directly. The simplest one is the direct linear transform, which treats the twelve numbers of the transform matrix as unknowns and solves them all at once using six or more points. Better formulas exist, like efficient PnP, which needs only four points, or perspective-three-point, which uses exactly three. 

After the guess comes refinement. Starting from the guess, the program nudges all six pose numbers at once in the direction that shrinks the error fastest, usually using the Levenberg-Marquardt method. It repeats this until the error stops falling. The first guess matters a lot, because refinement only walks downhill from where it starts. If the start is poor, it can settle in the wrong valley.

The page shows a worked run using a red 6 centimetre cube. The cube stands on a table, turned 20 degrees. The camera is 0.40 metres above the table, 0.20 metres back, and looking 60 degrees down. It can see six corners of the cube. The true pixels were given 0.3 pixels of random noise, which is what a good corner detector achieves. Then, the refinement was given a deliberately poor start, with the cube in the middle of the table and not turned. 

The diagram shows six known corners, where a rough guess puts them, and where the fitted pose puts them. On the left, the first guess puts the corners far from their true pixels, and red lines show the large reprojection errors. The root mean square error is 50.46 pixels. On the right, after refinement, every guessed dot sits perfectly on its target cross. A table shows how the error fell one step at a time. It started at 50.46 pixels, dropped to 5.25 in the first step, then to 0.43 in the second step, and settled at 0.30 pixels by the third step. The final pose was 3.3 millimetres and 0.51 degrees from the truth. For comparison, the direct linear transform first guess on the same six points gave an error of 2.80 pixels, and was about 5 millimetres and 4.6 degrees off. It is a good place to start, but not a place to stop.

The next part explains how many points are needed. A pose has six unknown numbers, and each point gives two measurements, its horizontal and vertical pixel coordinates. Three points give six measurements, which is just enough, but they can fit up to four different poses. So a fourth point is needed to choose between them. In practice, three points give up to four answers, which is used inside RANSAC. Four points give one answer if they are well spread, like the corners of a square marker. Six or more points make the answer steady because the noise averages out. Spread matters just as much as the count. Points that are close together give a poor pose, and the object should fill a fair part of the picture.

The section ends with the pseudocode for the whole process. First, the program takes the object points, the pixels, and the camera calibration. It undistorts the pixels. Then, it gets a first guess for the rotation and shift using a solver like efficient PnP. After that, it loops up to 30 times. In each loop, it projects the points using the current pose, finds the errors between these and the true pixels, and calculates the step that shrinks the sum of squared errors fastest. It applies this step to the pose, and stops early if the error stops falling. Finally, it calculates the root mean square error. If the error is greater than about 2 pixels, it reports failure. Otherwise, it returns the final pose and the error.

The third section is about running PnP inside RANSAC, for when some matches are wrong. In real pictures, a feature matcher might pair a pattern on one side of a box with a similar pattern on the other side. One wrong pairing can pull the whole pose away because refinement tries to satisfy every pairing at once. RANSAC, which stands for random sample consensus, is the standard cure. 

It works like this. First, pick a few pairings at random, usually three or four. Second, find the pose that those few imply. Third, reproject every object point with that pose and count the pairings whose error is under a limit, such as 3 pixels. These are the inliers that agree with the pose. Fourth, repeat this many times and keep the pose with the most inliers. Finally, refine that pose using only its inliers.

The page shows a test on a printed box with 40 matched features. The true pixels were given 0.5 pixels of noise, and 12 of the 40 pixels were replaced with random places in the picture. The RANSAC algorithm drew 6 pairings per try and made 200 tries. The diagram shows the result. On the left, refinement on all 40 pairings produced a pose that was nearly 50 millimetres and 47 degrees away from the truth, making it useless. On the right, RANSAC found exactly the 28 right pairings. It caught all 12 wrong ones and dropped none of the right ones. The final pose was just 0.4 millimetres and 0.1 degrees from the truth. 

To know how many tries are enough, you use a formula. A try succeeds when every pairing it draws is right. With 70 percent right pairings, a draw of six is all right with a chance of 0.7 to the power of 6, which is about 12 percent. To be 99 percent sure of at least one clean draw, you need about 37 tries. A draw of four is all right 24 percent of the time, and needs about 17 tries.

The fourth section discusses a flat target, and why face-on is unstable. Many robot cells find objects with a fiducial marker, which is a printed black-and-white square like an ArUco or AprilTag marker. The four corners of the square are exactly a PnP problem with four points on one plane. However, there is a trap. When the marker is nearly face-on to the camera, two different poses fit the corners almost equally well. One is tilted a little towards the camera, and the other is tilted the same amount away. 

The diagram shows a 40 millimetre marker tilted 12 degrees one way, and 12 degrees the other. Seen from the side, they face clearly different ways, 24 degrees apart. But the four corners each pose puts in the picture differ by at most 1.27 pixels. At 5 degrees of tilt, the gap is only 0.53 pixels, so a corner detector with 0.3 pixels of noise cannot reliably tell the two apart. The only clue is perspective, because the near edge looks a little longer than the far edge, but that difference is a fraction of a pixel.

A chart and table show how wrong the marker's facing direction comes out against how far it is tilted. When the marker is exactly face-on, at zero degrees of tilt, the facing direction is typically 7.4 degrees wrong, and the worst tenth of runs are 14.9 degrees wrong or more. At 5 degrees of tilt, the solver actually chose the completely mirrored pose in 19 percent of runs. But as the tilt increases, the error drops. At 30 degrees of tilt, the typical error is only 1.3 degrees, and the solver never chooses the wrong pose. The distance to the marker, by contrast, came out about 2 millimetres wrong at every tilt. The takeaway is that a small, face-on marker gives a good position but a poor rotation.

In practice, people solve this in a few ways. First, they tilt the camera or the marker so it is seen at 20 to 45 degrees. Second, they make the marker bigger in the picture, which makes the perspective clue larger. Third, they use several markers spread apart. Fourth, they use a rotation they already know, like the table's up direction. Finally, they filter over time using a Kalman filter or a simple median, because a rotation that flips from frame to frame is a clear sign of this problem.

The fifth section lists where PnP is used on a robot arm. It runs wherever a program knows an object's shape and can find points on it in a colour picture. First, it is used for reading a fiducial marker on a fixture or a tray to get its pose in one picture. Second, it is used in calibration to find the board's pose in the camera's frame. Third, it is used with keypoint models, where a neural network finds named points on an object, and PnP turns them into a pose. Fourth, it is used for matching against a stored picture of a product, running PnP inside RANSAC. Fifth, it is used by a wrist camera looking at a fixed marker to check if its own calibration has drifted. Finally, it is used for placing a part in a fixture by looking at drilled holes or printed corners.

The sixth section covers where PnP works, and where it does not. Because it is exact geometry, it only fails when its inputs are wrong or too weak. A table lists common failures. If some pairings are wrong, you will see a large error or a jumping pose, so you should run PnP inside RANSAC. If a small flat marker is seen face-on, the rotation will wobble or flip, so you should tilt the view or use a bigger marker. If the points bunch together in the picture, the pose will change a lot with small noise, so you should use points spread over the whole object. If lens numbers are wrong, the pose will be consistently off, so you must calibrate and undistort the pixels first. If the object's model does not match the real object, you will see a steady error well above the pixel noise, so you must measure the real object. If you have a poor first guess with few points, refinement can settle on a wrong pose, so you should use a proper first-guess solver. Finally, if the object is symmetric, the answer might flip, so you need a feature that breaks the symmetry. PnP also cannot find points itself, and gives no answer for an object whose shape you do not know. For those, a depth camera and a matching method like iterative closest point are used.

The seventh section lists libraries that provide PnP. It is in almost every computer vision library. OpenCV provides it in its calib3d module for C++ and Python, with functions like solvePnP and solvePnPRansac, and flags for solvers like efficient PnP and square markers. OpenCV also has an aruco module for finding ArUco markers. The AprilTag library provides it in C and Python, finding both poses of a tag and keeping the one with the smaller error. PoseLib offers fast minimal solvers with RANSAC built in. There are also ROS 2 marker packages that publish each marker's pose directly.

The eighth section asks why you would use PnP, and what it costs. PnP gives you a full six degrees of freedom pose from one colour picture in well under a millisecond, and its reprojection error tells you how well it fits. The obvious alternative is a depth camera, which needs no printed marker. However, depth cameras struggle with shiny, black, and see-through surfaces, and their depth noise is a millimetre or more. PnP needs only a colour camera, works on objects that depth cameras cannot see, and is precise when points are well spread. The cost is that you need the object's shape, a reliable way to find the points, a calibrated camera, RANSAC for wrong pairings, and a planned viewing angle for flat targets.

The ninth section looks at the learned alternative. A keypoint model finds named points on an object in a messy picture, and PnP still turns those points into the pose. The network does the finding, and the geometry does the rest. A render-and-compare model draws the object's CAD model at a guessed pose and corrects the guess until the drawing matches the photo. Learned models win in clutter and on objects with no marker. But PnP alone still wins when you can put a marker on the object, because it needs no training, no graphics processor, and its reprojection error warns you if the pose is wrong.
