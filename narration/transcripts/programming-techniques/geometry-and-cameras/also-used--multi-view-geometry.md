Multi-view geometry: depth from two pictures. This page explains how a program finds depth from two pictures of the same scene, without a depth sensor. One picture only tells you the direction of each point, but two pictures, taken from two known places, tell you where the point is. The page covers five ideas, and the first two of them work together. Triangulation finds a point from two lines of sight, and epipolar lines say where to look for a match in the second picture. Rectified stereo turns the shift between two side-by-side pictures into depth, while parallax finds a height from how far a point shifts when the camera slides. And the ground-plane constraint gets depth from a single camera when the point is known to be on the table.

It is for a reader who has read the pages on the pinhole camera model and rigid transforms. Book 2 already uses some of these ideas. The section on baseline, not count, shows why two views far apart beat many views close together, and the section on two photos from one moving camera measures an object's width and height by moving the camera. So this page explains the geometry underneath both. Every number here comes from a real run of the diagram script with Book 2's camera, which has a resolution of 320 by 240 pixels, a focal length f x and f y of 277.1, a centre c x of 160 and c y of 120, and sits 0.40 metres above the table.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. A pixel tells you the direction of a point. Two pixels of the same point, seen from two known places, tell you where the two directions cross, and that crossing is the point. 

So here is an everyday example. Hold a finger up in front of your face, then close your left eye and then your right. The finger jumps sideways against the wall behind it, and a near finger jumps a lot while a far one jumps a little. Your brain uses that jump to judge distance, and two cameras, or one camera that moves, can do the same, because they can measure the jump in pixels. Everything on this page depends on knowing where the two viewpoints are relative to each other. On a robot arm that knowledge is often free, because the arm itself moved the camera, and its joints report exactly how far.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step, starting with triangulation, which is where two rays cross. The previous section said that two directions cross at the point, so this section starts with the arithmetic of that crossing. Each pixel defines a ray, which is a straight line that starts at the camera and runs out into the scene. The pinhole camera model gives its direction as u minus c x divided by f x, v minus c y divided by f y, and 1, in the camera's frame, and the camera's pose turns that direction into the room's frame.

Triangulation means finding the point where two rays cross. With perfect numbers they cross exactly, but with real, noisy pixels two rays in 3D usually miss each other by a little. So the program looks for the point that is closest to both rays. The standard way writes two straight-line equations for each view and solves them together, which is the same kind of step as the direct linear first guess in the page on pose from points.

Here is a worked example, in which a wrist camera looks straight down from 0.40 metres and sees a spot on top of a red box at pixel 212.5 comma 86.5. The arm then moves the camera 80 millimetres to the side and turns it 13.2 degrees so that it still faces the box, and in the second picture the spot is at pixel 211.9 comma 86.0. 

The diagram shows two wrist-camera poses, the two rays, and where they cross. On the left, the two rays cross on top of the box, and triangulating the two exact pixels gives 64.4, 41.1, 60.0 millimetres, which is the true spot. On the right is a close-up, where each pixel is moved by 0.5 pixels, in opposite directions. The rays now cross at 64.0, 40.5, 65.1 millimetres, which is 5 millimetres higher. Almost all of the error is along the line of sight, because two rays that meet at a narrow angle slide along each other easily. The diagram uses dashed lines to show each ray moved by 1.5 pixels, and a diamond between them shows the region where the answer could land.

The distance between the two viewpoints is the baseline, and a wider baseline makes the rays meet at a wider angle, which makes the diamond shorter. A table shows the results of repeating the example 2,000 times with 0.5 pixels of random noise on each pixel, for four baselines. The numbers show that as the baseline grows, the error falls. For an 18 millimetre baseline, the median error is 11.3 millimetres, and the worst tenth of runs have an error of 27.0 millimetres or more. But for a 200 millimetre baseline, the median error drops to just 1.3 millimetres, and the worst tenth are only 2.7 millimetres or more. The run agrees with the formula from the baseline, not count page, where the error equals the depth squared times the pixel noise, divided by the focal length times the baseline. So doubling the baseline halves the error.

The next step is epipolar lines, which tell you where to look for the match. Triangulation needs to know which pixel in picture 2 shows the same point as a pixel in picture 1. Finding that pixel is called matching, and it is the hard part, but geometry makes it much easier. 

The pixel in picture 1 is a ray, and the point is somewhere along that ray, although we do not know where. So now look at that whole ray from camera 2. A straight line in the room looks like a straight line in a picture, so the match must lie somewhere on one line in picture 2. That line is called the epipolar line, and it shrinks the search for the match from the whole picture to one line.

For a second worked example, camera 2 is now 80 millimetres to the side, 30 millimetres forward, and turned 13.2 degrees. There is also a second red box on the table that looks the same as the first one. A diagram shows how a pixel in view 1 becomes a line in view 2. The spot is at 212.5 comma 86.5 in view 1, and its epipolar line in view 2 is shown as a purple line. The true match, at 211.9 comma 110.8, lies exactly on that line, while the look-alike box is 56.0 pixels away from it and therefore cannot be the match, however similar it looks. The page on tracking and association uses exactly this test to match objects between two views. However, the test fails when two candidates both lie on or near the line. Then geometry alone cannot choose, and the program needs appearance or a third view.

The next part explains the essential matrix in plain words. The epipolar line comes from one 3 by 3 matrix called the essential matrix, E, which holds how camera 2 is turned and shifted relative to camera 1. If you write a pixel with the lens numbers taken out, as x equals u minus c x divided by f x, v minus c y divided by f y, and 1. Then for any point seen in both pictures, x 2 transposed times E times x 1 equals 0, where E is the cross product of the shift t, multiplied by the rotation R. R is the turn from camera 1 to camera 2, and t is the shift. The equation says one thing in plain words: the two rays and the line between the two cameras all lie in one flat plane. They must, because they form a triangle with the point at its tip. So E times x 1 is the epipolar line in picture 2, in lens-free units.

In our example, t is negative 77.9, 30.0, 18.3 millimetres in camera 2's frame, and the equation gives negative 3.5 times 10 to the power of negative 18 for the true match, which is zero apart from rounding. When the pixels still carry the lens numbers, the same matrix is written with them folded in. This is F equals the inverse of K transposed, times E, times the inverse of K. F is called the fundamental matrix, and K is the 3 by 3 matrix of lens numbers.

The essential matrix can also run backwards. Given five or more matched points, and no knowledge of how the camera moved, a program can find E, and from it R and the direction of t. This is how a camera works out its own movement from pictures alone, and it always uses RANSAC, because some matches are wrong. However, it cannot find the length of t, because two pictures of a small scene taken close together look exactly like two pictures of a large scene taken far apart. So the scale must come from somewhere else, like the arm's own movement, a known object size, or a depth reading. On a robot arm, the arm's joints almost always know the movement, so the program uses the known R and t directly.

The next idea is rectified stereo, which turns disparity into depth. A stereo camera is two cameras side by side, fixed to one bar, and the distance between them is its baseline. When the two cameras point the same way and their rows line up, the pair is called rectified. Real stereo cameras are never built perfectly, so their pictures are corrected in software to make them rectified, and that correction uses a stereo calibration.

In a rectified pair, every epipolar line is simply a row of pixels, so a point in row 86 of the left picture is in row 86 of the right picture. The only difference is how far along the row it appears, and that difference is the disparity, which is u left minus u right, measured in pixels. Depth then follows from one division: depth equals f x times the baseline divided by the disparity.

So here is the worked example, with a 50 millimetre baseline, which is typical of a small stereo depth camera. A diagram shows the same row in both pictures, and how depth comes from the disparity. The spot on the box top is at u equals 212.5 in the left picture and 171.7 in the right one, in the same row. So the disparity is 40.75 pixels, and 277.1 times 0.050 divided by 40.75 equals 0.340 metres, which is the right depth.

However, a chart in the diagram shows the catch. Depth falls as one over the disparity, so a far point has a small disparity, and a one-pixel mistake there costs a lot. A table gives the numbers, showing how a mistake of one pixel or a quarter of a pixel affects different depths. Good stereo matchers reach about a quarter of a pixel. At a depth of 0.20 metres, the disparity is 69.3 pixels, and a one-pixel mistake only changes the depth by 2.8 millimetres. But at a depth of 2.00 metres, the disparity is just 6.9 pixels, and a one-pixel mistake changes the depth by 252.3 millimetres. The error grows with the square of the depth, so stereo is precise close up and poor far away. For a table-top arm, where most things are under 0.6 metres away, it works well.

To get depth for every pixel, a stereo matcher searches each row of the right picture for the best match of each left pixel. That search needs texture, because a blank white table has nothing to match, and neither does a shiny surface that reflects different things into each camera. So many stereo depth cameras add a projector that throws a dot pattern onto the scene to give plain surfaces some texture. The page on sensors compares these.

The next method is parallax, which finds height from a sliding camera. A wrist camera can make its own stereo pair. The arm slides the camera sideways, and the program compares the two pictures. How far a point moves in the picture is its parallax, which is the same thing as disparity, except that the baseline comes from the arm rather than from a fixed bar.

This is especially useful for heights. A camera looking straight down at a table sees every table point shift by the same amount, which is f x times the baseline divided by H, where H is the camera's height. A point above the table is closer to the camera, so it shifts more, and that extra shift tells you how far above the table it is.

A diagram shows a camera sliding 40 millimetres, where the table shifts 27.71 pixels and the box top shifts 32.60 pixels. In this worked example, the camera is 0.40 metres above the table and slides 40 millimetres. A mark on the table shifts 277.1 times 0.040 divided by 0.40, which is 27.71 pixels. The spot on the box top shifts 32.60 pixels. So its depth is 277.1 times 0.040 divided by 32.60, which is 0.340 metres, and its height is 0.40 minus 0.340, which is 60.0 millimetres, the true height of the 6 centimetre cube.

A mistake of half a pixel in the shift gives 54.7 millimetres or 65.1 millimetres instead of 60.0, which is a 5 millimetre error. This is about the same size as in the triangulation example earlier, because it is the same geometry. A longer slide makes it smaller. The table's shift is a useful check, because every table point should shift by 27.71 pixels. If they do not, the slide was not what the arm reported, or the camera was not looking straight down. The page on programmed methods works through the same idea for an object's width.

The final method is the ground-plane constraint, which uses one camera and a known table. Sometimes one picture is enough. If a point is known to lie on the table, its depth is wherever its ray meets the table. So the table acts as the second view, because it is a second surface that the point must lie on, and a ray and a plane cross at exactly one point. The pinhole camera model page works the method through step by step.

However, the constraint is only as good as the claim that the point is on the table. Here is what happens when the claim is wrong. The straight-down camera sees the spot on top of the 6 centimetre cube at pixel 212.5 comma 86.5. If the program assumes the spot is on the table, it places it at 75.8, 48.4, 0 millimetres, while the truth is 64.4, 41.1, 60 millimetres. So the position on the table is 13.5 millimetres wrong, as well as the height. With a tilted camera that looks 60 degrees down, the same 60 millimetre mistake puts a point 44.1 millimetres too far away. This is because a slanted ray travels a long way sideways for each millimetre of height.

So the ground-plane constraint suits flat things that really lie on the table, like sheets, coins, or the bottom edge of an object where it touches the table. For the top of an object, use one of the two-view methods above, or subtract the object's known height before intersecting the ray with a plane at that height.

The section ends with pseudocode for the three most common jobs, written as plain steps. First, to triangulate one point from two views with known poses, the program calculates the ray for each pixel using the camera poses and lens numbers. It then finds the point closest to both rays using linear least squares. If the shortest distance between the two rays is more than a few millimetres, it rejects the match because the pixels were not the same point.

Second, to match along the epipolar line, the program calculates the essential matrix from the two known camera poses, and turns it into the fundamental matrix. It multiplies this by the first pixel to get the line in the second picture. It then finds candidates in picture 2 within 2 pixels of that line, and picks the one that looks most like the feature in picture 1.

Finally, to find height from a sideways slide of a downward camera, the program measures the shift in pixels for the same point. Depth is the focal length times the slide length divided by the shift, and height is the camera height minus that depth. As a check, table points must shift by the focal length times the slide length divided by the camera height.

The next section describes where these five methods are used on a robot arm. Two views appear on a robot arm more often than it seems, and here are the concrete places. First, most depth cameras on arms are stereo pairs with a dot projector. The rectification and the disparity search run inside the camera, and the depth picture that comes out is f x times the baseline divided by the disparity for every pixel. Second, when measuring with a wrist camera, the arm moves the camera between two poses and triangulates corners, holes or edges. The baseline comes from the joints, so it is known exactly. Third, for heights of objects a depth camera cannot see, like glass and shiny metal, their outlines still shift with parallax. Fourth, when matching the same object in two cameras, a cell with two fixed cameras uses epipolar lines to decide which detection in one picture is the same object as a detection in the other. Fifth, when reading items on a flat table, a single colour camera above a conveyor uses the ground-plane constraint to place flat parts, with no depth sensor. Finally, a camera that moves through many poses and triangulates many points builds a 3D model of the scene.

The next section covers where it works, and where it does not. The uses just mentioned all depend on knowing the two camera poses and matching the right pixels. Two-view geometry is exact, so its failures come from the inputs. A table lists the common ones. If the baseline is short for the distance, you will see depth that jumps by centimetres between frames, so you should move the camera further between views or get closer. If there is a wrong match, you get a triangulated point far from anything, or a large gap between the rays, so you should match along the epipolar line, reject large gaps, and use RANSAC. On plain or shiny surfaces, you see holes or noise in the stereo depth picture, so you should add a projected dot pattern or use a learned matcher. If the camera poses are wrong, table points will not shift by the expected amount, or there will be a consistent tilt in the depth, so you must recalibrate the hand-eye transform or read the arm's pose at the exact moment of the picture. If the object moved between the two pictures, heights and depths will make no sense, so take both pictures quickly, or use a stereo camera which takes both at once. If a point assumed on the table is not on it, positions are pushed away from the camera by several centimetres, so only use the ground-plane constraint for things that lie flat. Finally, if you try to get scale from pictures alone, you will see a correct shape at the wrong size, so you must take the scale from the arm's movement or a known object.

Two views also cannot see what neither camera sees, so a point hidden behind something in one view cannot be triangulated. And depth from stereo is poor far away, because the error grows with the square of the distance.

The next section lists libraries that provide these tools. OpenCV provides the two-view building blocks in C++ and Python, with functions to triangulate points, find the essential matrix, and compute epipolar lines. It also has a stereo module to calibrate a pair, rectify it, compute disparity, and turn disparity into points. ROS 2 has a stereo image processing package in C++ that turns a stereo pair into a point cloud topic. PoseLib finds the essential matrix and the camera movement with RANSAC. COLMAP builds a 3D model from many pictures by triangulating matched features from the command line. And depth camera software, like the Intel RealSense SDK, runs the stereo matching inside the camera itself.

The next section answers why you would use two views, and what it costs. It is the geometry of two rays crossing. So it gives you depth and 3D points from ordinary colour pictures, and it tells you where to look for a match. The obvious alternative is a single depth camera and nothing else, which gives a depth for every pixel with no matching step in your program. That is the right choice for most table-top arms. However, depth cameras fail on glass, shiny metal and black surfaces, and their fixed internal baseline sets their accuracy. Two-view geometry with a wrist camera instead works on any surface whose outline you can see, and lets you choose the baseline. For example, a 200 millimetre slide is about eleven times more precise than an 18 millimetre built-in stereo baseline. The ground-plane constraint needs no second view at all, but it only works for things that lie flat.

The cost is this. You need to know the two camera poses accurately, which means a good calibration and pictures taken while the arm is still. You need a reliable match for every point. You need a second picture, which takes time when the arm has to move. And you need a wide enough baseline, which the working space may not allow.

The next section looks at the learned alternative. The page on depth from pictures covers two learned models for this job. A learned stereo model does the matching with a network, then turns each shift into a depth with the same rule as this page, so its answer is still in real metres. It is suggested when you pick your own cameras, or work in bright light or at longer range, though like a written matcher it still struggles on a plain white surface. A monocular depth model guesses depth from one picture with no second view and no matching. But its errors are often several centimetres at one metre, many versions give no fixed scale, and it is best kept for rough jobs such as telling the foreground from the background. The geometry on this page still wins when you need a measured depth for a grasp, want no graphics processor, or can choose a wide baseline by moving the wrist camera.

The final section is about using it in Python. It shows the two calls you are most likely to write, because they cover the two different situations the page described. Triangulating a handful of matched points suits an arm that moves its own camera, while a dense disparity map suits a fixed stereo pair. After reading it you should know which of the two your robot needs.

Triangulation comes first. Each camera is described by a 3 by 4 projection matrix, which is the lens matrix K multiplied by the rotation and shift that carry a point from the world into that camera. The code defines the projection matrix for the first view to define the frame, and the second view from the arm's joints. It then passes the two matrices and the pixel coordinates in each view to OpenCV's triangulate points function, and divides the result by the fourth number to get the 3D points.

A fixed stereo pair is the other case, and there the work is matching every pixel rather than a few. The code uses OpenCV's semi-global block matcher to compute the disparity between the left and right rectified images. It then uses the reproject image to 3D function to turn the result into points using the matrix Q that stereo rectification produced during calibration.

What the libraries do for you is the arithmetic that was set out earlier. The triangulate points function solves the two straight-line equations per view and returns the point closest to both rays. The block matcher searches along the epipolar line for every pixel, which is possible only because rectification has already made those lines horizontal, and it returns the disparity in sixteenths of a pixel, which is why the code divides by 16.

What you still write yourself is what goes in. Triangulation needs the two pixels to be the same physical point, and finding that pairing is the job of image features and matching. It also needs both camera poses, which on an arm you read from t f 2 at the moment each picture was taken. For the stereo path you have to run the calibration and rectification first, keep the Q matrix they produce, and then mask out the pixels where the disparity came back as the no-match value rather than letting them become points at an absurd distance.

What you have to decide or measure is the baseline and the search range. The baseline is the distance between the two viewpoints, and the earlier table showed what it buys: at 0.34 metres, going from 18 millimetres to 80 millimetres took the median error from 11.3 millimetres to 2.5 millimetres, so this is the single most important choice on the page. The number of disparities then sets how close an object can be and still be found, because a nearer object shifts further, and it costs time in proportion. The block size trades noise against detail, since a larger block gives a smoother disparity map and loses the edges of small objects. All of these are measurements you make once on your own rig rather than numbers to copy.
