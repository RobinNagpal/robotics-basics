Least-squares fitting. 

This page explains least-squares fitting, the standard way to find the line, plane, or circle that best matches a set of measured points. It answers four questions in order: what "best" means, how the best shape is found by hand and by a program, where a robot arm uses it, and when it gives a wrong answer. It is for a reader who knows what a point cloud and a coordinate frame are, and who has read the chapter overview. However, you do not need any linear algebra beyond knowing that a matrix is a table of numbers. Every number mentioned comes from a real run of the diagram script. 

Least squares is the most used fitting technique in robotics because it sits inside so many other methods. It is inside plane finding, circle measurement, camera calibration, point cloud alignment, and numerical inverse kinematics. So it is worth understanding well, since most of the other techniques in this chapter are built on top of it.

The first section covers what this page answers. A camera or a depth sensor never gives points that lie exactly on a shape. The points along a straight box edge wobble a little to either side. Since a flat table is measured in the same way, its points sit a millimetre or so above and below the real surface. Yet the arm needs one straight edge, one flat table, and one circle with one centre, which the measured points do not give it directly. 

Least-squares fitting turns the wobbly points into that one shape, which is described by a few numbers called its parameters. A line in a picture has two of them, its slope and its offset, while a plane has three. A circle on a table also has three: the two coordinates of its centre and its radius. So fitting means choosing those parameters, which comes down to picking a few numbers that suit many measured points at once. This page shows how least squares chooses them for lines, planes, and circles, and how to check that the result can be trusted. Then it shows where the method breaks, which is the reason the next page on RANSAC exists.

The idea can be stated in one sentence: choose the shape that makes the sum of the squared distances from the points to the shape as small as possible. 

Here is an everyday example. You hang a picture rail along a wall, and mark five heights where you want the screws, but your marks are a little uneven. So no straight rail can pass through all five of them. You do not want the rail to pass exactly through any two marks and miss the other three badly. Instead, you want it to pass as close as possible to all five at once. If you measure how far each mark is from the rail, square those five distances, and add them up, you get a total for that rail. Then the best rail is the one where that total is smallest. 

Squaring matters for two reasons, and the first is that every distance becomes positive. Because of that, a mark above the rail cannot cancel out a mark below it. Squaring also makes a large miss count much more than a small one. That is why a two-millimetre miss counts four times as much as a miss of one millimetre. As a result, the shape that comes out runs through the middle of the points instead of through any two of them.

The next part explains how it works, step by step, starting with residuals and the sum of squares. The distance from a point to a candidate shape is called the residual. For a line written as y equals m times x plus c, the usual residual is the vertical gap. This is the point's measured y, minus the y the line gives at that point's x. Here m is the slope, which is how much y rises for each one of x. Then c is the offset, which is the value of y where x is zero. Once every point has a residual, you square them all and add them up, and that total is the sum of squared errors. Each candidate line has its own sum, so least squares simply picks the line whose sum is smallest.

The page shows a diagram of five points along the edge of a box, found by a camera, measured in millimetres. It compares two lines. The first is a line drawn through just the first and last points. It has zero error at those two ends, but large errors at the three points in between, which add up to 6.45. The second is the least-squares line. It misses every point by a little, but the squared errors add up to only 2.72. That is the smallest total any straight line can reach for these points.

To see where that total of 2.72 comes from, the page works through the example by hand. The five points have x values of 0, 10, 20, 30, and 40. Their y values rise from 1.0 to 19.4. Think of every possible line as one point on a map, with the slope m on one axis and the offset c on the other. Then give each point on that map the sum of squared errors of its line. The result is a bowl, because the total error is high at the edges of the map and lowest near the middle. A diagram draws that bowl as contour lines, like the height lines on a hiking map. Each ring joins lines with the same total error. The least-squares line is exactly at the centre. The line through the two end points sits further out, outside the ring marked 6, since its own total is 6.45.

At the bottom of a bowl, the ground is flat in every direction. So the best line is the one where a small change to the slope or the offset does not change the total error. Writing those two conditions out gives two ordinary equations, called the normal equations. For a line, the first equation is: the sum of x squared, times m, plus the sum of x, times c, equals the sum of x times y. The second equation is: the sum of x, times m, plus the number of points, times c, equals the sum of y. 

Putting the five measured points into those sums gives 3000 m plus 100 c equals 1560, and 100 m plus 5 c equals 55.3. Solving these gives a slope of 0.454 and an offset of 1.98. So the best line is y equals 0.454 x plus 1.98. The residuals of that line are all under one millimetre, and their squares add up to 2.72. That is the number at the lowest point of the bowl. 

The typical distance of a point from the line is called the root mean square residual, or RMS residual. It is the square root of 2.72 divided by 5, which comes to 0.74 millimetres. Because it is one number in millimetres, the RMS residual is a useful summary of how well a fit went. 

Because there is one normal equation per parameter, a shape with more parameters works in exactly the same way. A computer then solves all of the equations at once. In matrix form, the equations are written as A-transpose times A times p equals A-transpose times b, where each row of A holds one point's values, b holds the measured values, and p holds the parameters. You do not need to solve these by hand, since every numerical library has a function for it.

The page then gives the steps as pseudocode to fit any shape whose error is a straight sum of parameters times known values. This covers lines, planes written as z equals a times x plus b times y plus c, polynomials, and circles. The code creates an empty table A and an empty list b. It loops through each point, adding a row to A containing the values that multiply each parameter, and adding the measured value to b. It then solves the normal equations to find the parameters, calculates the residuals, and computes the RMS residual. In practice, a library solves the problem with a method called QR or the singular value decomposition, instead of forming A-transpose times A directly, because those methods lose fewer decimal places.

Next is how to fit planes using the singular value decomposition. A table or a wall is a plane. You could fit a plane using vertical residuals, which works well enough for a table seen from above. However, it fails for a wall, because a wall is nearly vertical, and a vertical plane cannot be written as z equals something. The vertical gaps also measure the wrong thing on a steep surface. 

Instead, the better way measures each point's distance at right angles to the plane. It uses a tool called the singular value decomposition, or SVD. In plain words, it does this. First, it takes the average of all the points to find the centre, which the plane passes through. Second, it subtracts the centre from every point. Third, the SVD finds the three directions in which the points spread out, one at right angles to the next, and how far they spread in each: the longest spread, the middle one, and the thinnest one. Fourth, for points on a flat surface, the thinnest direction points straight out of the surface, so it is the plane's normal, the direction the plane faces. Finally, the plane is all points whose offset from the centre is at right angles to the normal. 

The thinnest direction is exactly the one that makes the sum of squared right-angle distances smallest, making it a least-squares fit too. A diagram shows this result for 150 points on the tilted face of a board. The points spread 92.4 millimetres along the longest direction and 57.3 millimetres along the middle one. Along the thinnest direction, they spread only 0.93 millimetres, and that thinnest direction is the normal. This spread of 0.93 millimetres is also the RMS distance of the points from the plane, matching the one millimetre of noise that was added to the points. 

Because the three spreads come back as well, they carry a free check. If the thinnest spread is not much smaller than the middle one, the points do not lie on a plane at all. They might lie on a curved surface, so a program should test the spreads before trusting the normal. This same method finds the long and thin axes of an object to determine which way it lies.

The next part covers circles, which need a trick to keep things simple. Lines and planes drop straight into the normal equations, but a circle does not. The natural error is each point's distance from the circle, but that error does not make a straight sum of parameters. A short piece of algebra fixes this. A point on a circle satisfies the equation: x minus a, squared, plus y minus b, squared, equals r squared, where a and b are the centre and r is the radius. Multiplying that out and moving terms around gives: x squared plus y squared equals 2a times x, plus 2b times y, plus k, where k is a constant based on the radius and centre. Now the unknowns a, b, and k appear only multiplied by known values. This is the same shape of problem as the line, with three parameters instead of two. You can solve it with the same pseudocode, and then recover the radius from k, a, and b. This is called the algebraic circle fit.

A diagram shows why this fit matters on a real arm. A camera looking at a glass from the front sees only the near half of its rim. The diagram shows 18 rim points from the near half of a circle, with a little noise. The algebraic fit correctly finds the centre and radius. However, the plain average of those points lies 30 millimetres towards the camera, well off the true centre. If a gripper were sent to the average, it would hit the front of the glass. The algebraic fit does have one weakness: below a quarter of a circle, it tends to give a radius that is too small, which requires a second step of nonlinear least squares to correct.

The final step of how it works is that the leftover error tells you how good the fit is. Least squares returns a line even for points on a curve, and a plane even for points on two surfaces. The only sign that the shape is wrong is the leftover error. A careful program always returns the RMS residual with the fit and compares it with the sensor's known noise. If a depth camera is good to about one millimetre and a plane fit leaves an RMS residual of 0.9 millimetres, the plane is real. If it leaves three millimetres, something else is in the points, like an object on the table or a second surface.

The next section lists where least squares is used on a robot arm. These fits turn up across perception, calibration, sensing, and movement. First, finding the table's height and tilt. After RANSAC picks the points that belong to the table, an SVD plane fit gives the most accurate plane to measure objects against. Second, measuring a round object. A program fits a circle to the rim of a cup or glass to find where to aim the gripper. Third, finding which way a face points. Before a wrist turns a board to lie flat, it fits the face with the SVD, then refits with closer points to drop neighbouring edges. Fourth, finding a straight edge in a picture. Edge pixels along a box side are fitted with a line to give its angle. Fifth, correcting a sensor. You can plot a distance sensor's readings against known true distances and fit a line to find the correction. Sixth, calibration. Camera calibration fits focal length and distortion to checkerboard corners, while hand-eye calibration fits where the camera sits on the arm. Seventh, aligning two point clouds. Iterative closest point uses the SVD to find the rotation and shift that line up matched points. Eighth, moving the arm to a pose. Numerical inverse kinematics repeatedly solves a small least-squares problem for how much to turn each joint. Finally, learning a contact constant. A robot can fit the friction of a table from many pushes.

The following section explains where least squares is useful, and where it is not. It is the right choice when every point belongs to the shape, the noise is small and spread evenly, and you know the shape in advance. In that case, it is exact, fast, and needs no settings. Because the errors are squared, its big weakness is stray points. One point far from the rest pulls the answer hard. If one point out of five is a bad pixel, it can move the whole line. 

The page provides a table of common ways least squares goes wrong and their standard cures. If some points belong to something else, the RMS residual will be much larger than the sensor's noise, and the shape will tilt towards the stray points. The cure is to use RANSAC to pick the good points first, or a robust loss. If there are two surfaces in the same points, the thinnest SVD spread will not be much smaller than the middle one. The cure is to split the points first with clustering or RANSAC. If the shape is wrong, like fitting a line to a curve, the residuals will not be random. The cure is to fit the right shape. If a circle is fit to a short arc, the radius comes out too small. The cure is a geometric fit or using a known radius. If points cover a very small area, the fit changes a lot with noise. The cure is to measure over a wider area. If some points are more accurate than others, you use weighted least squares. And if the errors are not a straight sum of parameters, the simple solve gives a biased answer, so you use nonlinear least squares, which repeats a linear fit to improve the guess each time.

Since these cures are standard, you rarely write the solve yourself. The next part lists libraries that provide it. In Python, NumPy is the first thing to reach for, using its linear algebra least squares and SVD functions. SciPy provides nonlinear least squares with robust losses. OpenCV provides line and ellipse fits on image points in C++ and Python. Eigen is the matrix library under most C++ robotics code. Open3D and the Point Cloud Library fit local planes to get normals. Ceres Solver handles large nonlinear problems like calibration, and scikit-learn offers the same fits with a machine-learning interface. A plane fit with NumPy is just three lines of code: subtract the mean, call SVD, and take the last row of the result as the normal.

The next section asks why we use least squares, and what it costs. It gives the arm clean numbers from noisy points and a measure of how well the shape fits. The obvious alternative is to use only a few special points, like the ends of an edge. That takes no maths, but it throws away most of the data and lets the noise on those few points decide the answer. Least squares uses every point, so the noise averages out. Another alternative is to minimise the plain sum of distances instead of squared distances. That resists stray points better, but it has no direct solution. It needs repeated steps that are slower and can stop at the wrong answer. Squared distances give a direct, exact solution in one step. 

Three costs come with this. First, you must know which shape to fit before you start. Second, every point must belong to that shape, because one stray point can move the answer a long way. Third, the answer always looks confident, so you must check the leftover error yourself.

The page then compares this with the learned alternative. Fitting a known shape, like a table plane or a cup's rim, has no learned replacement. The shape's formula is known, and least squares gives its best fit exactly, in one step. Learning takes over only when nobody knows the shape in advance. Linear regression is actually least squares itself. A Gaussian process or a small neural network can learn a curve that nobody wrote down, such as a depth camera's error against distance. However, with a few input numbers and tens to hundreds of examples, classical methods do as well as a network or better. A network pulls ahead only when the input is a picture, a point cloud, or a long recording, and there are many examples.

The final section shows how to use it in Python. A least-squares fit comes down to solving one linear system, and NumPy already has the routine. The page shows a short program doing a line fit and a plane fit. The line fit uses numpy dot linalg dot lstsq. It takes a matrix where each row has the x value and a one, along with the measured y values. It returns the slope and offset, and the leftover error. The plane fit uses numpy dot linalg dot svd. It subtracts the mean from a patch of table points, calls SVD, and takes the last row of the result as the normal direction. It then calculates the thickness of the points along that normal. 

The libraries do the solve and nothing else. The least squares function factorises the matrix and returns the parameters, the sum of the squared misses, the rank of the matrix, and its singular values. A rank lower than the number of unknowns means the data does not pin all of them down, and a large spread in singular values means the answer is fragile. What you have to write is the matrix, which is the same as writing down the model. The circle trick is just a rearranged matrix that turns a curved fit into a call to the same routine. You also have to write the check afterwards, because the function always returns numbers and never refuses. 

Finally, you have to decide what counts as a good fit. Only you know whether a leftover error of 0.18 millimetres is fine for the job, and only you know the sensor's own noise to compare it against. If the leftover error is far larger than the sensor's noise, either the shape is wrong or some points do not belong to it. You also have to decide the units, because the function will not notice if you mix metres and millimetres.
