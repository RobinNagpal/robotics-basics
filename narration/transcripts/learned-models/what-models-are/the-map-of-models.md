The map of models. 

This book, Learned Models, describes many learned models, and most of them are neural network models. They have different names, take in different things and give out different answers, so this page is the map of all of them. It sorts the neural network models into seven kinds, which this book calls categories, and for each category it says what the models do and where to read about them. It also lists every page of the book's chapter on classical machine learning, which are the learning methods that are not neural networks.

The page answers three questions: what are the seven kinds of model, where does each kind do its job when one robot arm does one task, and in what order should you read the chapters of this book?

It is for a complete beginner who has read the earlier documents in this chapter, especially the page on what a model is. You can also come back to it at any time, when you want to see where one chapter fits among the others.

The first section explains how one task uses seven kinds of model. The easiest way to see all seven categories is to follow one task. A person says to a robot arm, "Put the red mug in the sink." The arm has a camera on its wrist, and there is a red mug and a blue mug on the counter, with a sink at one end.

To do this, the robot must do several separate things. First it must understand the words, and then it must find the red mug in the camera picture, and not the blue one. It must work out the mug's exact shape and position in space, so that it can choose where to put its fingers. Then it must move there, pick the mug up and carry it, while feeling whether the mug is slipping. And it helps if it can predict what will happen when it lets go. So each of these jobs is done by a different kind of model.

A diagram shows this task with the seven kinds of model numbered. Each number sits next to the part of the scene that the model works on: the words, the camera picture, the mug's shape, the finger positions, the path, the sink and the fingertips. A real robot does not always use all seven, because many robots use only two or three, and some large models do several of these jobs at once. But every model in this book does at least one of these jobs.

The next part of the page describes the seven categories. Each category has a chapter overview that lists every kind of model in it.

First are seeing models. They turn a picture into names, boxes, outlines, poses or depth, which means they take a camera picture and say what is in it and where. The simplest ones give one name for the whole picture. But others draw a box around each object, trace its exact outline, find special points such as a mug's handle, or guess how far away each pixel is. Some can even be told what to look for in ordinary words, such as "the red mug". So in the mug task, a seeing model finds the red mug in the picture.

Second are 3D models. They work on 3D points and whole scenes instead of flat pictures. A depth camera gives many points, each with a position in space, and together these points are called a point cloud. 3D models can say which points belong to the mug, or guess the shape of the side of the mug that the camera cannot see. They can also build a full 3D copy of the room from many pictures. So in the mug task, a 3D model gives the mug's exact shape and position, which keeps the gripper from bumping into it.

Third are grasp models. They decide where and how to hold an object, so they take a picture or a point cloud and give one or more grasps. A grasp is a position and direction for the gripper, together with how wide to open its fingers. Some grasp models also say where a suction cup should go, or which part of an object is meant to be held, such as a mug's handle. Others instead score a grasp to say how likely it is to work. So in the mug task, a grasp model chooses where the fingers go on the mug.

Fourth are movement models. They decide how the arm should move, moment by moment, so they take what the robot sees and feels now and give the next movement, or the next few movements. Many of them learn by copying people who drove the robot, while others learn by trial and error, in the real world or in a simulator. Some only help a programmed planner, by checking paths or suggesting them. So in the mug task, a movement model guides the arm to the mug and on to the sink.

Fifth are language models. They understand words, and connect words to pictures and actions. For example, a language model can turn "put the red mug in the sink" into a list of steps. A vision-language model can instead answer questions about a picture, such as "is the mug in the sink now?". A vision-language-action model goes further and takes a picture and an instruction and gives arm movements directly. So in the mug task, a language model turns the person's words into steps: find the red mug, pick it up, and put it in the sink.

Sixth are world models. They predict what will happen next if the arm does something, so they take the state of the scene now and a planned action and give the state that should follow. Some predict positions, some predict whole future pictures, and some predict how cloth or liquid will move. A robot can use this to try out actions in its imagination before doing them for real. So in the mug task, a world model predicts whether the mug will land upright if the gripper lets go at a certain point.

Finally, touch and body models make sense of touch, force and the arm's own body. They take signals from touch sensors on the fingers, from force sensors in the wrist, or from the arm's own motors. So they can say whether the fingers are touching something, how hard they are pressing and whether the object is slipping. They can also notice when the arm has bumped into something it should not have, and learn how the arm itself really moves. So in the mug task, a touch model notices if the mug starts to slip out of the fingers.

The page then puts all seven categories side by side in a table. It shows what each model is given, what it gives back, and an example question it answers. For instance, seeing models are given a camera picture and output names, boxes, or depth, answering a question like "Where is the red mug in this picture?". Language models are given words, often with a picture, and output steps or arm movements, answering "What steps does 'put the red mug in the sink' need?". Touch and body models take touch, force, and motor signals, and output contact, force, or slip, answering "Is the mug slipping out of my fingers?". The table shows a clear pattern. The first three categories look at the world, while the next one, movement models, acts in it. Language models connect people's words to the rest, world models look ahead in time, and touch and body models check what is happening at the fingers and inside the arm.

The next section lists every page in the book, so you can find any one of them from here. The first chapter explains the ideas that every later page uses, covering what a model is, how it learns, what is inside a neural network, the four learning signals, where data comes from, and this map of models. 

The second chapter covers classical machine learning, which are the learning methods that are not neural networks but are used next to them on robots. They learn from a short list of measured numbers, often with only tens or hundreds of examples. Its pages are split into two groups: most used general tools like linear regression and decision trees, and also used methods with a narrower job like movement primitives and support vector machines.

After that, each of the seven family chapters has an overview page, followed by pages split into the same two groups. The most used group holds the kinds of model that robot arm projects use most often, or that matter most, so you should read these first. The also used group holds kinds of model that are used often, but less, because some do a narrower job, while others are newer and not yet in everyday use. 

A large table lists all these pages. For example, in the seeing models chapter, object detection and segmentation are in the most used group, while image classification and depth from pictures are in the also used group. In the movement models chapter, behaviour cloning and diffusion policies are most used, while reinforcement learning and learned motion planners are also used. The split is about how often a page's kind of model is used, not about how good it is, so an also-used model can still be the right choice for your task. For example, if your robot must learn a skill by trial and error, reinforcement learning is the page to read, even though it sits in the also-used group.

The last chapter is about making models work on an arm. It is not a family of models, because it is about what every model needs before a real arm can rely on it. Its most used pages cover fine-tuning a model to your own robot, running a model inside the loop that drives the arm, and evaluating how it fails. Its also used page covers uncertainty and confidence, which tells the robot when a model is unsure.

The next part explains when each kind of model does its job. While the earlier map showed where each model works, a timeline diagram here shows when they are busy during the mug task. The task is split into six steps: hear the words, find the mug, choose the grip, reach and close the gripper, carry the mug to the sink, and let go. The diagram shows that language works first and only briefly. Seeing keeps working as the arm reaches, because the mug may move. Movement, world, and touch models then take over once the arm is moving. This is one possible way to build the task, and not the only one. For example, a robot built around a single vision-language-action model would have one long bar that covers language, seeing, and movement at once.

The following section is about how the categories connect. The seven categories are separate chapters, but the models in them depend on each other. The output of one is very often the input of the next. A seeing model finds the mug, and then a 3D model takes the points inside the mug's outline and gives its shape. A grasp model takes that shape and chooses a grasp, and a movement model, or a programmed planner, moves the arm to that grasp, while a touch model checks the grip. This chain, from picture to movement, is the most common way to build a picking robot. 

Some models cross the borders between two categories or more. An open-vocabulary seeing model uses language to know what to look for, so it belongs partly to seeing models and partly to language models. A vision-language-action model does the jobs of seeing, language, and movement in one network. A world model can be used inside a movement model, so the movement model can try actions in its imagination first. 

The categories also share their data sources. Seeing and language models learn mostly from pictures and text, often from the internet, while movement models learn mostly from demonstrations and simulation. Touch and body models learn from the robot's own sensors. And almost all of them run inside the same control loop on the robot.

The next part helps you find a method by the job it does. Most people come to these books with a job in mind, not a method, so the page provides a large table starting from the job. For each job the arm must do, it lists the written programming techniques and the learned models that can do it. For example, if the job is to find an object in a picture, written techniques include thresholding and colour masks, while learned models include object detection and segmentation. If the job is to choose a grasp, written techniques include morphology and least-squares fitting, while learned models include six-degree-of-freedom grasps and grasp quality models. If the job is to predict what happens next, written techniques include arm dynamics, while learned models include learned dynamics models and video prediction models. 

Most jobs have both a written and a learned answer. However, two jobs in the table only have one side. First, no learned model is in common use to check whether the arm can reach a pose, because numerical inverse kinematics already gives an exact answer quickly. Second, no written technique can follow an instruction in free wording, because a person can say the same thing in too many ways to list them all, so this requires a vision-language model. Many real arms mix the two sides, so a learned model finds the object and written techniques do the rest. The classical machine learning methods appear in the rows where the input is a few measured numbers. Those jobs are correcting a sensor, tuning a controller's gains, learning a motion from a few demonstrations, and scoring grasps or spotting faults from logged numbers.

The page then gives a suggested reading order. You can read the chapters in any order, because each one explains its own terms, but some are easier to follow after others. The suggested order starts with the first chapter on what models are, because everything else uses its words. Second is classical machine learning. You should read it early if your inputs are a few measured numbers and you have only tens or hundreds of examples. Its linear regression page is the simplest learned model there is, making the weights of a neural network easier to picture. Third is seeing models, because most robot arms start with a camera, and most other models use what a seeing model finds. Fourth is 3D models, which take the step from flat pictures to positions in space. Fifth is grasp models, which use what seeing and 3D models give to decide how to pick an object up. Sixth is movement models, which decide how the arm moves. Seventh is language models, which are easier to follow once you know seeing and movement models, because the largest of them combine both. Eighth is world models, which predict the future, making the most sense once you know what a movement model does with a prediction. Ninth is touch and body models, finishing the families with the sense that works closest to the object itself. Finally, read the chapter on making models work on an arm once you have a model you want to use, as it takes any model from a notebook to a real arm. If you have one specific job in mind, you can jump straight to its chapter. Inside each chapter, read the overview first, then the most-used pages, and then whichever also-used pages fit your task.

The next section suggests where to read next. It points out that the seeing models overview is the next chapter in the suggested order. It also mentions that the page on running a model on a robot explains the loop that every one of these models runs inside, and it points to other books in the series for topics like foundation models and learned methods for one arm.

The final section is about using these models in Python. Almost every model on this map is used from Python in one of two shapes, and knowing which shape a method takes tells you in advance what work it will be. The page describes the code for both.

The first shape is for the classical methods of chapter two. In this shape, you train the model yourself on a table of data you collected, which takes seconds. The code simply creates a model, like a random forest classifier, fits it to your data, and then uses it to predict new answers.

The second shape is for the neural networks of every other chapter. In this shape, you almost never train from scratch. Instead, you build the network layers and load somebody else's trained numbers into them. These trained numbers are a large download that somebody spent a great deal of computing time producing. The code uses PyTorch to build the network, loads the saved weights, sets the network to evaluation mode, and then passes the robot's observation through it without calculating gradients. When loading the file, the code specifies to load weights only, which stops it from running any hidden code stored in the file, an important step when downloading from the internet.

The libraries follow the categories closely. Every classical method is used through scikit-learn, while PyTorch sits under everything else. Seeing and language models usually come through Ultralytics or Hugging Face's transformers package, and movement and world models come through the research code released with each paper. What you have to write yourself is the same in both shapes, and it is not the model. It is the code that turns the robot's sensor readings into the input the model expects, and the code that turns the model's output into commands the arm can follow. What you have to decide is which category your problem belongs to, and the table of jobs earlier on the page is the tool for that.
