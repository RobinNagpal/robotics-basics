System identification: measuring the numbers a model needs. 

This page explains system identification, which means finding the numbers inside a physical model of the arm from measurements of the arm itself. Examples include a joint's friction, the mass of the part in the gripper, and the stiffness of a springy finger. The page answers five questions about numbers like these. How do you move the arm so that the data can tell the numbers apart? How do you fit them? How do you follow a number that drifts over time? How sure can you be of each number? And which end of that range should the arm act on?

It is for a listener who is familiar with the page on least-squares fitting, because that page explains parameters, residuals, and the least-squares solve, and this page uses all three. The part on drifting numbers also uses one idea from the Kalman filter page. Every number on this page comes from a real run of a Python script.

The first section explains what this page answers. Each of those five questions matters because a controller, a planner, or a simulator uses a model of the arm. A model here means a set of equations, such as saying the torque a joint needs is its friction plus the weight it lifts. Those equations have numbers in them, like a friction value, a mass, a distance, or a stiffness. The equations themselves come from physics and are usually right, but the numbers in them are usually not known well. 

A datasheet gives a typical value, not the value for your specific arm, because friction changes from one gearbox to the next and falls as the gearbox warms up. The mass of the part in the gripper changes with every pick, and a rubber finger pad gets softer with wear. So system identification measures these numbers on the real arm rather than trusting the datasheet. You move the arm in a planned way, record what happens, and fit the numbers so that the model's equations match the recording. The result is a model that describes your arm today, plus an error bar that says how far each number might be off.

The next part gives the idea in one sentence. Move the arm so that each unknown number has a visible effect, record the result, fit the numbers by least squares, and state how sure you are of each one. 

That one sentence covers the whole method, and an everyday example shows why each part of it is needed. You want to know how much a suitcase weighs and how much the wheels drag, but you have only a luggage scale that measures how hard you pull. If you pull it at one steady walking speed, you learn only the total pull. This means you cannot say how much of it is drag that grows with speed, and how much is a fixed amount of rubbing. That is why you pull it slowly, then quickly, then slowly the other way, and now the two effects show up differently. Because they show up differently you can separate them. Choosing those different speeds is the probe move, and working out the two numbers from the pulls is the fit.

The page then breaks down how it works, step by step. Since the probe and the fit are easier to follow on a real example, the worked example uses one joint of a small arm. That joint needs torque to overcome friction, where torque is the turning force a motor gives, measured in newton-metres. Most arm motors report their torque, or a current that is proportional to it.

First, you write the model so the numbers sit on their own. A common model of joint friction has two parts, and each part has its own number. First, viscous friction grows in step with speed, like stirring honey. Its number is b, in newton-metres per radian per second. Second, Coulomb friction is a fixed amount of rubbing that always opposes the motion, whatever the speed. Its number is c, in newton-metres. Written out, the torque at a steady speed w, in radians per second, is b times w, plus c times the sign of w. The sign of w is plus one when the joint turns one way and minus one when it turns the other way. 

The important point is that the unknowns b and c are each multiplied by something you know. So each reading gives one row of a linear equation: a known row made of w and the sign of w, times the unknown pair b and c, gives the measured torque. Least squares solves exactly this kind of equation, and many physical models can be written this way. For example, a payload's weight enters an arm's torque as the mass times a known function of the joint angles. The page on arm dynamics shows the full equations for a whole arm, where the rows that multiply the unknowns are often called the regressor.

Once the model is written that way, the next choice is the probe move, which is the motion you run to collect the data. People call the quality of a probe move its excitation, which means how strongly it makes each unknown number show up in the data. 

The simulated joint has true values of 0.25 for b and 0.40 for c, and its torque readings scatter by 0.05 newton-metres, which is the sensor noise. A diagram shows two probe moves of forty readings each. A rich probe holds eight speeds in both directions, from minus 2 to plus 2 radians per second, taking five readings at each. A poor probe, however, holds speeds near 1 radian per second, all in one direction. The diagram shows the fitted friction values from 300 repeats of each probe with fresh noise. The rich probe's answers sit in a small cloud around the true values, and its fitted b ranged from 0.204 to 0.294. However, the poor probe's answers lie along a long thin line. Its fitted b ranged from minus 0.025 to 0.589, and one answer even had negative friction, which is not possible.

The reason is simple, because at a speed of about 1 radian per second the torque is about b plus c. As a result, the poor probe measures that sum very well, but it cannot tell how the sum splits between b and c. Every point along the line gives nearly the same sum. The rich probe, however, includes slow and fast speeds, so a change in b and a change in c change the torques in different ways. 

A number called the condition number measures this, and it compares how well the best-measured and the worst-measured combination of the unknowns are pinned down. A value near 1 is ideal, so the rich probe's rows, with a condition number of 4.9, are much better than the poor probe's 28.3. If the poor probe held exactly one speed, the condition number would be infinite and the fit would have no single answer.

So the rules for a good probe follow from all of this. First, make each unknown change the readings in its own way. For friction, use slow and fast speeds, in both directions. Second, cover the range the arm will use. A number fitted from slow moves may be wrong for fast ones. Third, keep the probe safe by staying inside the joint limits, the speed limits, and the workspace, and start gently. Finally, check the condition number of the rows before running the probe on the arm. It needs only the planned speeds, not the readings.

Once the probe has been run, the rows and the readings are in hand, so the fit is one least-squares solve, as the least-squares page explains. One real run of the rich probe gave readings such as minus 0.940 newton-metres at minus 2 radians per second, and 0.880 newton-metres at 2 radians per second. The fit gave b equals 0.2285 and c equals 0.4205. The residuals, which are the gaps between each reading and the fitted curve, have a spread of 0.043 newton-metres, which is close to the sensor's true noise of 0.05. That is a sign that the model has the right shape, because nothing is left over except noise.

A picture shows the forty torque readings at eight speeds, and the fitted friction curve. The slope of each half of the curve is b, and there is a jump where the speed crosses zero equal to two times c, because the rubbing changes direction. A narrow pale band around the curve shows two spreads of the fit's uncertainty, showing how sure the fit is.

That band comes from an error bar on each fitted number. An error bar is the spread of a fitted number: how far it would move if you ran the same probe again with fresh noise. It comes almost free with a least-squares fit, in three steps. First, estimate the sensor's noise from the residuals. Square them, add them up, and divide by the number of readings minus the number of unknowns. Here that is 40 minus 2, which is 38. The square root is the 0.043 newton-metres mentioned earlier. Second, multiply that squared noise by the inverse of the table of rows transposed, times the table of rows. The result is the covariance of the fitted numbers: a small table that gives each number's squared spread and how the numbers' errors are linked. Third, the square root of each number on the diagonal of the covariance is that number's error bar. 

For the run above this gives b equals 0.229 plus or minus 0.012, and c equals 0.421 plus or minus 0.017. Both true values lie within two error bars of the fit. The covariance also shows a link between the two numbers, because the errors of b and c have a correlation of minus 0.91. When b comes out too high, c usually comes out too low. This matters when you use both numbers together. For example, the torque the joint needs at 1.5 radians per second is 1.5 times b, plus c, which is 0.763 newton-metres. Its error bar, worked out with the link, is only 0.0075 newton-metres. However, if you ignore the link and treat the two errors as separate, you get 0.025 newton-metres, more than three times too cautious. This is because the fit knows the total torque at the speeds it measured far better than it knows either number alone.

However, the number you need is often not the fitted number itself, but something worked out from it. Carrying an error bar through a formula is called error propagation. For example, working out a part's mass from the elbow torque and the distance to the part. The formula is mass equals torque divided by the product of gravity and distance. For a formula made only of multiplying and dividing, there is a simple rule: the relative spreads, which means the spread divided by the value, add in squares. The page works this out for a torque of 1.20 newton-metres with a spread of 0.04, and a distance of 0.150 metres with a spread of 0.006. The relative spread for torque is 0.033, and for distance it is 0.040. The square root of those two squared and added is 0.052. As a result, the mass is 0.815 kilograms with a spread of 5.2 percent, which is 0.043 kilograms. Notice that the distance, not the torque, is the larger source of doubt, so to improve the answer you should measure the grasp position better rather than the torque.

The rule is exact only for a straight-line formula, and a division is not straight. A Monte Carlo check therefore tests it by brute force. You draw many random torques and distances, each from its own bell curve, work out the mass for every pair, and look at the spread of the results. A diagram shows a histogram of twenty thousand Monte Carlo masses. The bell curve the formula predicts sits on top of it, showing they agree well. The best value is 0.815 kilograms, and the cautious end is 0.900 kilograms.

Whether the error bar comes from the formula or from Monte Carlo, a fitted number with an error bar is a range, not a point. The arm still has to make one decision, so the safe habit is to act on the end of the range that fails gently if you are wrong. That end of the range is called the cautious end. For the payload, a heavier part is the dangerous case, because it needs more torque to stop, takes longer to brake, and puts more load on the gripper. So the planner should use the upper end, which with two spreads is 0.900 kilograms. 

Which end is cautious depends on the decision. First, for payload mass for a speed limit or a lift check, use the upper end. Second, for friction for feed-forward, use the lower end of c, because too much added torque makes the joint push itself when it should stop, or shake around the target. Finally, for the stiffness of a springy finger to limit squeezing force, work out the distance from the upper end of the stiffness.

The cautious end handles a number you have measured once, but some numbers change while the arm works. For example, a gearbox's friction falls as it warms up. Running the probe again and again stops the arm's real work. Instead, the arm can refine the numbers from its normal moves, one reading at a time. Recursive least squares does this, because it keeps the current fitted numbers and a covariance table, like a Kalman filter. Each new reading nudges the numbers towards it. 

However, a number that stays constant is the wrong assumption for friction that drifts. A forgetting factor, written as the Greek letter lambda, fixes this, and it is a number just below 1. At each step, all older readings count lambda times as much as before. A diagram shows recursive least squares estimates of viscous friction over 600 readings, against a true value that falls from 0.30 to 0.20. It compares three forgetting factors. A factor of 1 never forgets; it is smooth but trails far behind, ending up at 0.258. A factor of 0.98 follows the fall with a short lag, ending at 0.207, but shakes slightly. A factor of 0.90 remembers only about 10 readings; it follows fastest but shakes more than twice as much as 0.98. This is a trade-off, because forgetting fast follows changes but passes on more noise.

The page then gives the steps as pseudocode. It describes three short functions. The first is the batch fit with error bars, which solves the least squares equation, calculates the residuals and noise, and finds the covariance and error bars. The second is one step of recursive least squares, which predicts the reading, calculates a gain, updates the numbers based on the prediction error, and updates the covariance table using the forgetting factor lambda. The third is a Monte Carlo spread function, which repeatedly draws inputs from bell curves, applies a formula, and returns the average and spread of the results.

The next section lists where system identification is used on a robot arm. It appears wherever a model's numbers decide how the arm moves or what it believes. Examples include joint friction for feed-forward, payload identification after a grasp, and finding the full arm's masses for a model-based controller. It is also used for collision detection, where a monitor compares expected torque with reported torque. Other uses are finding the stiffness of a springy finger, measuring contact and friction with a table, making a simulator match the real arm, and camera placement, which is a geometric model fitted from a probe of many arm poses.

The page then explains where the method is useful, and where it is not. It works best when the physics is known and only the numbers are not, when the numbers multiply known quantities, and when the arm can be moved safely through a probe. A table lists ways it can go wrong. For example, if the probe does not excite every number, you will see huge error bars or impossible values like negative friction. The solution is a richer probe. If the model is missing an effect, the residuals will show a pattern rather than random scatter. You should add the missing term or a learned correction. If speed and torque readings are out of step in time, the fit changes when the probe changes speed quickly. You must line the readings up in time first. If you use a forgetting factor while the arm stands still, the numbers will jump on the first move after a pause, so you should only update while the arm moves enough. If the forgetting factor is too small, the numbers shake from reading to reading. If there are rare large readings, like a bump, one reading moves the fit a long way, so you should throw away readings with a large residual. Finally, if the physics is too complex to write down, like for cloth or a tangled cable, no small set of numbers will fit, and you need a learned model.

The next part covers libraries that provide system identification. For a model written as rows times unknowns, the fit is one call to a least-squares routine. In Python, NumPy provides functions for the fit, the covariance, and the condition number. SciPy handles models that are not just rows times unknowns. For C++ and Python, Pinocchio builds the rows for a whole arm's masses and inertias from its description file. MuJoCo has a system identification toolbox for simulators, and MATLAB has a whole toolbox for this. Python also has the uncertainties library to carry error bars through formulas, and padasip for recursive least squares. However, recursive least squares itself is about ten lines, and many projects write it themselves.

The page then discusses why to use system identification, and what it costs. It gives the arm a model that matches this arm today, and a range that says how far to trust it. The obvious alternative is to use the datasheet or the design files, which costs nothing. However, a datasheet gives a typical value, and friction varies from unit to unit. A payload's mass is not in any datasheet, and datasheet values come with no error bars. A second alternative is domain randomisation, which means training across many random guesses of the numbers. Identifying the numbers first lets you randomise only over the real error bars. The costs of system identification are that you need a model with the right terms, the probe takes time on the real arm, and the numbers go stale as the arm wears or warms up, meaning you must re-run the probe or track the numbers.

The next section looks at the learned alternative. Neural networks can learn how the arm's body behaves from its own recordings, capturing effects no textbook equation has. However, they need far more data, their internal numbers have no physical meaning, and they can behave oddly in movements they have not seen. The usual choice is residual learning: keep the identified physics model and train a small network only on what it gets wrong. System identification is the right first step, so you add the learned correction only when the residuals still show a pattern.

The final section shows how to use it in Python. It describes a short program that fits the three numbers of a single joint from a recorded trace. The model says the torque the motor applied is the joint's inertia times its acceleration, plus a viscous friction that grows with speed, plus a Coulomb friction that depends on the direction of turning. The code reads a log of time, speed, and torque. It calculates acceleration from the speed. Then it builds a matrix with one row per reading, and three columns for the unknowns: acceleration, speed, and the sign of the speed. It passes this to NumPy's least-squares function, which returns the inertia, viscous friction, and Coulomb friction. The code then uses the leftover error to calculate the variance, covariance, and error bars.

On a made-up four-second probe move, the fit returns values very close to the true numbers, with small error bars. The condition number is 18.6, which is small, telling you the probe move really did separate the three effects.

The page points out that NumPy does the solve, the matrix inverse, and the condition number. There is no special system identification function being called; the identification is entirely in how you built the rows. Writing that matrix of rows is the same act as choosing the model. If the joint has a gravity term, that would be a fourth column. For a whole arm, a library like Pinocchio builds the rows for you, returning a matrix with sixty columns for a six-joint arm. You still have to choose which of those columns your probe move actually excited.

What you have to decide or measure is the probe move and what you do with the error bars. The probe move must change speed and acceleration independently, or the fit cannot tell inertia and viscous friction apart. You check the design with the condition number. You also have to smooth the speed before differentiating it to find acceleration, because differentiating a noisy signal amplifies the noise. Finally, you decide which end of the error bar to act on. For a payload or a torque limit, you must use the cautious end rather than the middle.
