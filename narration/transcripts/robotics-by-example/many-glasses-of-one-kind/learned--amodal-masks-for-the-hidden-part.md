Solution 10, amodal masks for the hidden part.

This solution uses a learned approach for the decider. It uses the exact same instance segmenter as solution 9, and places it in the same part of the pipeline. However, there is one key change. Instead of training each mask against only the pixels that the camera can actually see, each mask is trained against the entire silhouette of its glass.

The physical setup of the robotic cell is described fully on the earlier page dedicated to the cell. That page covers the layout, the two camera positions from the top and the side, all four sensors, and the terminology used throughout the project. Because of this, everything that follows here will focus only on what is specific to this tenth solution.

<!-- section: introduction | Introduction -->

The introduction to this page explains how to make a learned instance segmenter report the part of a glass that nothing in the picture shows. It is the smallest of the three solutions built on a large pre-trained model. The model, the weights it starts from, the pictures it trains on, and the code that consumes its answers are all the same as in the page about a fine-tuned instance segmenter. What differs is only the answer each mask is scored against during training.

That small change is worth a document because of a failure the rest of the project cannot detect. When something stands in front of a glass, or when the edge of the picture falls across it, the mask of that glass stops early. The footprint worked out from a mask that stops early is wrong in the one way that gets past every check this project has: it looks like the footprint of a smaller glass standing somewhere slightly different. A mask that includes the missing part hands the glass on as a glass of its own, with the part nobody saw marked as asserted rather than measured.

By the end of the page, you will understand what the words modal and amodal mean, and why the label needed to train the amodal version costs nothing in a simulator when it is expensive and arguable on real photographs. You will learn what changes in the model and what does not. The text explains why a mask claiming pixels nobody saw is a prediction rather than a measurement, and how far it should then be trusted. It also covers how to tell whether the completion works, because the usual measure of a segmenter gives the wrong answer here. Finally, you will hear what running it shows, the one way of using the completion that ruins the answer while every check still passes, what stops such a model from inventing glass where there is none, and why the whole idea stops dead at the one case the problem says to watch hardest.

<!-- section: the-problem-this-solves | The problem this solves -->

The next part of the page explains the problem this solution solves. 

Imagine four to six glasses stand on the table. They are all of one kind, the kind is known, and they are solid, so the depth camera sees them. The job is to say which pixels belong to which glass, to give each glass a position on the table, and to give each a rough footprint width. Nothing is picked up and no shape is measured here.

To see the failure this solution repairs, one step further down the pipeline has to be described, because that is where the damage appears rather than in the mask itself.

A mask is a picture the same size as the photograph in which every pixel is just yes or no, where yes means "this is glass". An instance mask is one mask per object, so a picture holding several glasses comes back as several masks rather than as one region covering all of them.

What happens to each instance mask is the arithmetic already used in the part of the project about clustering on the table. Every pixel of the mask has a depth reading, so it becomes a point in the room, and dropping the height turns it into a point on the table. A glass seen from the top is a circle, so the points of one glass should fill a disc, and fitting a circle to that disc returns a centre, which is where the glass stands, a width, which is how wide its footprint is, and a fit error, which is how far the points sit from the fitted circle on average.

Those three numbers are then checked, and that check is the safety net the whole project rests on. The kind on the table is known and a kind has a range of footprint widths it allows, so a fitted width outside that range means the thing found is not one glass of this kind, and it is reported as doubtful rather than acted on. A large fit error means the points are not disc-shaped, which again means the group is not one glass. The check is arithmetic rather than judgement, and it is what makes a learned component tolerable anywhere in this project: the model proposes and the geometry disposes.

Two of those three numbers are what the code beside this document computes, and the third is not. That code takes the axis from the points at the top of the glass and the width from how far the cloud spreads around that axis, so a centre and a width come out of it and no fit error does. The fit error belongs to the circle fit described in the clustering section, so every mention of one from here on is a number that solution has and a run of this one does not.

Now put something in front of a glass. The camera sees the near object's surface where the far glass would otherwise have been, so the far glass's mask loses every pixel behind that surface. What is left is not a smaller copy of the glass. It is a crescent, cut along one side, with every remaining pixel lying towards the side the camera could still see.

The diagram here shows this: a mask cut short by an object in front of it leaves a crescent of points on the table, and the small, displaced circle fitted to that crescent is shown against the true footprint.

Follow what the circle fit does with that crescent, because each of the three numbers goes wrong differently and only one of them goes wrong loudly.

First, the centre moves. A fit uses every point it is given and nothing else, so the centre it returns sits where the points it was given are arranged around. The surviving points are all on one side of the true centre, so the fitted centre is pulled towards that side, and the more of the glass is hidden the further it is pulled.

Second, the width shrinks. The crescent spans less of the circle than the whole disc does, so the circle that best explains it is smaller than the true one. The fitted width comes out under the truth, and again by more the more of the glass is hidden.

Finally, the fit error stays small. This is what makes the failure dangerous. The surviving points have not become noisy or scattered; they are a clean patch of a clean disc, and a clean patch of a disc is well explained by a smaller disc. So the number whose job is to say "these points are not a glass" says nothing at all, because as far as it can tell they are a perfectly good glass.

Put the three together and the outcome is a plausible wrong answer. The width is inside the range the kind allows, because a kind whose range runs from a small tapered glass to a large one has room for the shrunken figure. A short measurement does not look like an error, it looks like a shorter glass. The fit error is small. Both checks pass. So the pipeline reports a glass with a believable width at a position where no glass is standing, and nothing anywhere in the run contradicts it. In a run with no fit error in it, which is the run beside this document, the failure is quieter still, because the one number that might have objected is never computed at all.

Compare that with the failure these checks were designed for. When two glasses merge into one region the fitted width comes out far wider than any glass of this kind can be, the check fires, and the group is reported as doubtful. That failure is loud. This one is quiet, and a quiet failure is worse in a cell where the next step is an arm moving towards where the answer said the glass was.

A mask comes back cut short for two quite different reasons. They appear on different tables and only one of them needs a crowded one, so they are worth separating before either is answered.

One reason is a neighbour standing in the way. Two facts about the kind on the table are what make that possible at all, and they are worth setting out, because between them they decide which tables it appears on.

The first is splay. Seen from the top, a glass's outline is thrown outwards away from the point directly below the camera, and the taller the glass the further out it goes. So a tall glass's outline covers ground in the picture well beyond where the glass actually stands, and whatever stands beyond it along that direction is behind that outline.

The second is the range of sizes inside one kind. This kind ranges the widest of the four the cell handles, with the tall end more than twice the height of the short end, so a tall glass and a short glass of the same kind are splayed by very different amounts. It is that difference which lets one outline sweep across the other.

Put the two together and a tall glass standing between the camera and a short one takes a bite out of the short one, and it does so at the gap the cell guarantees rather than needing the glasses closer than the cell allows. Nothing has to be contrived. But the arrangement has to line up for it: the two glasses have to lie along one line running out from the point below the camera, close together along that line, and differ a great deal in height. Swing the pair across that line instead, or space it further out, or make both glasses the same height, and the bite goes.

The other reason is the edge of the picture, and no neighbour takes part in it. The survey does not photograph the zone from one place. It works from three stations spread along the zone, so each station's frame is offset from the zone and part of the zone lies at or past that frame's edge. Splay then works exactly as it does against a neighbour. The further a glass stands from the point below the camera, the further out its rim is thrown, so a glass standing well inside the zone can still have its rim thrown over the edge of the frame. What the station returns is the near part of the footprint and nothing of the rest.

Downstream the two are one failure. Whichever of them cut the mask, what is left is a crescent of a disc, and everything about a mask cut short passing the check applies to it unchanged: the fitted circle is too small and displaced, the width is one the kind allows, and the fit error is the fit error of a clean patch of a clean disc.

Both are measured with the project's own renderer, at the cell's own survey height and from the three stations the cell computes, and they come out very differently.

A neighbour in the way is uncommon rather than the normal case. Across spawned scenes of this kind, only a small share of glasses lose any pixels at all to a neighbour, and a smaller share again lose every pixel at one station, though none loses them at more than one. Complete covering is possible at the guaranteed gap between centres, and an arrangement that produces it can be written down, but the cell's placement rule puts glasses where it rarely happens.

The edge of the picture, however, is the ordinary case. Nearly every glass is cut by it at one station or another, most glasses have no station at all that returns a whole footprint, and roughly one in six have exactly one. So the crescent this document is about is not a rare arrangement waiting on a crowded table. It is the usual shape of what one station hands the arithmetic, and the part of the project about asking the stations to agree records the same measurement, where it is the reason the stations are asked to agree.

That is worth saying plainly, because it changes what this solution is worth. The quiet failure described above, a footprint too small and displaced, with a width the kind allows and a fit error a genuine glass would give, is not a rare failure kept on the books for safety's sake. It is what one picture ordinarily returns. So the truncated footprint is worth taking seriously on two counts rather than one: the failure is ordinary, and nothing in the run says when it happens, because both checks pass and a position where no glass stands is acted on. Which of its two causes a completion reaches is the next question.

Completion reaches the first cause and not the second, and that is measured. Completing behind a near glass and completing past the edge of the picture are different problems. The first asserts pixels inside the frame, where a mask has room to hold them; the second would have to assert glass where the mask has no pixels at all, and a mask cannot hold a pixel the picture does not have. So the whole silhouette of a glass the frame cut is the visible mask of that glass, because a silhouette rendered from that same station stops at that same edge, and a run bears it out. On the layouts the cell spawns for itself this solution and the fine-tuned instance segmenter score alike, and the renderer's own exact silhouettes, handed to the shared arithmetic with no model anywhere in the chain, score no better there than its exact visible masks. The geometric check survives either way, because the ring outside the frame is already part of the blind region the clustering computes, and what the cell does about the frame edge is to survey from three stations and ask them to agree.

Both causes appear in the test harness, which is what lets either of them be measured rather than argued. The simulation harness renders its own top view from higher than the cell's survey height and takes one picture rather than three: the higher the camera the less splay throws each outline, and one frame from that height holds the whole zone with room around it. The code for this solution does not use that view. It stands the camera at the cell's own survey height and takes the three pictures a survey there needs, so the frame edge cuts masks in every run of it. The neighbour needs more than the height, because the cell's placement rule spaces glasses too well for it, so the harness builds crowded layouts on purpose and the test command asks for them by name. Neither cause has to be argued from geometry rather than watched.

A neighbour in the way needs the crowded layout: glasses lining up along one line out from the point below the camera, close together along it, with the tall ones in front of the short ones. The frame edge needs nothing of the sort, only a glass near the edge of a station's picture, which is where most glasses are at some station. The frame edge is also the cause a whole silhouette cannot describe, so the amodal target and the visible one are the same mask wherever the frame edge alone did the cutting, and they come apart only where a neighbour is in the way. That is why this solution is scored on the crowded layouts as well as on the spawned ones, and the two scorecards are read apart rather than averaged into one number.

<!-- section: the-main-idea | The main idea -->

The next part of the page explains the main idea. It follows from the diagnosis and it is one sentence long: ask the model for the whole silhouette of each glass, not just the visible part of it.

If the mask covers the whole silhouette, the glass is a region of its own, with its outline where the glass's outline really runs. The region is drawn around the whole shape rather than around the sliver the camera saw. Because of this, the sliver is attributed to the glass it came off instead of being swallowed into the region of the glass in front, and a sliver too thin to be worth proposing on its own is proposed as part of something whole. Nothing downstream changes, because what comes out is still a mask per glass.

The price is just as simple, and it decides what the completion may be used for. Some pixels in such a mask are pixels where the camera saw another object's surface. This means the model is asserting that glass continues underneath what is in front of it, and an assertion is not an observation. Worse, the depth reading at such a pixel is the other object's reading, so it says how far away that object is and nothing about this glass. The whole silhouette says how far a glass reaches, never how far away it is, and everything that consumes it has to know which of its pixels are which.

The rest of the page works through that trade. It covers the two words the idea needs and the everyday version that makes them intuitive, along with why the training label is free here. It explains what changes in the model and what does not, what follows from part of the mask being asserted, how to measure whether the completion works, and what running it shows. Finally, it outlines what bounds the risk of the model inventing glass, and the case none of it reaches.

<!-- section: what-modal-and-amodal-mean | What modal and amodal mean -->

The next part of the page explains what the terms modal and amodal mean. 

These two words come from psychology rather than from computer vision, and the psychological meaning is the one that makes them easy to remember. Here, a mode means a sense, such as seeing, hearing, or touching. Something is modally present when it arrives through a sense, so the part of a glass whose own surface the camera sees is modally present in the picture. Something is amodally present when the perceiver has it without any sense delivering it. The part of a glass hidden behind another object is amodally present: you know it is there, you know roughly where its edge runs, and no light from it reached the camera.

Applied to masks, that gives the pair of terms used from here on. A modal mask covers the pixels where the object's own surface is what the camera sees. An amodal mask covers the pixels the object would occupy if nothing stood in front of it. The amodal mask always contains the modal one, and the difference between them is the hidden part. 

The diagram illustrates this by showing a glass with another glass in front of it in three ways. First, it shows the modal mask covering only the visible pixels. Next, it shows the amodal mask covering the whole silhouette. Finally, it shows the hidden part, which is the difference between the two.

The everyday version of this concept is worth stating, because it shows that the amodal answer is the normal one and the modal answer is the odd one. Look at a cat sitting behind a garden railing. What you see, strictly, is a set of vertical strips of cat separated by bars. What you report is one cat. Nobody has to be taught this, and the alternative report, which would be several slices of cat of various widths, is so strange that it takes an effort to produce. A modal segmenter is a system that reports the slices. This is exactly what it was asked for and exactly what the picture contains. It becomes a problem only when the next step assumes a whole object, and here the next step is a circle fit, which assumes precisely that.

One more property matters for training. On an unobstructed glass, the modal and amodal masks are the same, because there is no hidden part to complete. So, training against amodal targets costs nothing on the easy scenes, and there is a free test available later: on a glass with a clear view, a model that adds anything at all is adding something wrong.

<!-- section: why-the-label-costs-nothing-here | Why the label costs nothing here -->

The next part of the page explains why the label costs nothing here. 

Everything discussed so far is standard, and so is the reason why amodal segmentation is not simply the default everywhere. On real photographs, creating the label is expensive and arguable. For example, an annotator looking at a picture of a cat behind a railing has to draw the cat's boundary where the bars cross it. This means drawing a line through a place nobody can actually see. Two careful people will draw it differently, and there is no way to check who was right. Because of this, amodal datasets are smaller than modal ones, their labels carry a disagreement that cannot be removed, and a model trained on them is fitted partly to the annotators' guesses.

None of that applies here, however, and the reason is the simulator. Because the simulator holds the entire 3D scene, it can be asked to render it more than once. If you render the scene with everything in place, you get the picture the model trains on. If you render it again with all the glasses but one taken away, from the exact same camera pose, the identity map of that second render marks every pixel that glass would occupy if nothing were in front of it. That gives you the amodal mask exactly, with no guessing involved.

A diagram illustrates this process, showing the full scene render next to individual renders for each glass with the other glasses removed. It shows the exact masks each render yields, including the hidden part of the glass, which is obtained by subtraction.

Three things follow from this approach, and each is worth having. 

First, the label is exact rather than just agreed upon. The boundary of the hidden part is exactly where the simulator's geometry puts it, and it would be in the exact same place if the scene were rendered again tomorrow. 

Second, the hidden part is available on its own. By subtracting the modal mask from the amodal one, you can separate the part of the answer that was easy to see from the hidden part that was the whole point of the exercise. This separation is what makes the measurements discussed later on the page possible. 

Finally, the visible fraction of every glass in every scene is known. By dividing the size of the modal mask by the size of the amodal one, you can calculate exactly how much of that glass the camera could see.

<!-- section: what-changes-in-the-model-and-what-does-not | What changes in the model, and what does not -->

The next part of the page explains what changes in the model, and what does not. 

With the label in hand the change is easy to state, because the architecture is unchanged. It is the same instance segmenter as solution 9, which is torchvision's Mask R-CNN ResNet-50 FPN version 2. It starts from the same weights fitted to a large collection of everyday photographs, and is fine-tuned on this cell's pictures with one class, "glass". Nothing is added to it, nothing is taken away, and no layer changes shape.

What changes is what the mask branch is scored against. Where solution 9 compares each predicted mask with the pixels the camera can see of that glass, this one compares it with the whole silhouette. The loss is the same loss, the optimiser is the same optimiser, and the pictures going in are the same pictures. A diagram here illustrates this, showing the same architecture drawn twice with the same weights and the same input. They differ only in the target the mask branch is scored against, with visible pixels on one side and the whole silhouette on the other.

That is the lesson a network trained from scratch draws from its two heads, as covered earlier, and it is worth having in the general form because it is one of the most useful things to know about this kind of model. One network answers different questions by changing its last layer and its target. The body learns to describe what is in the picture, and the question being asked lives almost entirely in what the final layer produces and what that output is compared with. Here even the last layer keeps its shape, so the question lives entirely in the target.

However, one consequence of the change is not optional, and it follows from how the model works. It works in two stages. It first proposes rectangles that might contain an object, and then, for each rectangle it keeps, it predicts a mask inside that rectangle. The mask is produced on a small grid covering the rectangle and stretched to its size, so it physically cannot extend past the rectangle's edge.

Now notice what the amodal target asks for. The whole silhouette of a partly hidden glass sticks out beyond the visible part of it, so the smallest rectangle containing the amodal mask is larger than the smallest rectangle containing the modal one. If the rectangles are still trained against the visible extent, every completion is clipped at the rectangle's edge and the model is being asked for something it has no room to express.

So the box target has to become amodal too, and the rectangle each glass is trained to be found in is the one containing its whole silhouette, which the same second render supplies. That has a further implication worth being plain about. The proposal stage is now learning to propose a rectangle larger than the evidence in the picture, which is harder than proposing one round a visible blob.

While the statement that only the target changes is true of the code, it is not true of the task. The code change is small and the task change is not. Solution 9's mask branch traces a boundary that is present in the picture, which is a question about where the evidence stops. This one traces part of a boundary that is not in the picture, which is a question about what a glass of this kind looks like and about which of the two objects at a boundary is in front. The second question needs the model to have learned the shape of the kind, and it needs the near-and-far relation to come out right, because completing the wrong one of the two objects produces a mask extending over a glass that is actually nearer the camera.

So the same architecture may need more training, or more capacity, to do this job as well as it does the modal one. Whether it has enough of either is not known, and this document will not assert it. It is something to measure, and the honest expectation is that the mask score over the hidden part will be clearly worse than the score over the visible part.

<!-- section: a-mask-that-is-partly-a-prediction | A mask that is partly a prediction -->

The next part of the page explains why a mask that is partly a prediction must be handled carefully. An amodal mask claims pixels where the evidence is actually some other object's surface. The camera looked at those pixels and saw the near glass, so for every pixel of the hidden part, the model is not reporting what it saw, but what it believes is behind what it saw. Because of this, a footprint fitted to such a mask as it stands is a prediction rather than a measurement, and how much of a prediction it is varies from glass to glass within the same picture. The rest of this section explains how to avoid fitting a footprint the wrong way, and what the completion is actually worth once the two kinds of pixels are kept apart.

The diagram shows one reported answer split into its observed part and its asserted part. It shows the fit error being computed over the observed part only, with the visible fraction carried alongside as a confidence score.

The model should not hand on one silhouette with the join hidden. It should hand on the observed part and the asserted part as two separate things. This is because each downstream consumer has its own tolerance for the asserted part, and none of them can apply that tolerance if the two parts have been merged. Separating them costs nothing. The modal mask is predictable from the same features by the same kind of branch. Even without a second branch, the observed part can be recovered, because the depth reading at a pixel tells you whether the surface there is at the far glass's distance or at the near object's distance.

The arithmetic is the first and strictest consumer of that split. The asserted pixels are identified and left out of it, so the place and the width come entirely from the pixels the camera saw, and nothing is inferred in their place. Guessing what an asserted pixel would be worth is a geometry question, and doing so here would be arithmetic nobody asked for. You can read which pixels are asserted straight from the answer itself, rather than from any ground truth. Where two reported outlines overlap, both glasses lie along those rays, and the camera saw the nearer of them.

Leaving the asserted pixels out has to be done on purpose, because a silhouette arrives as one mask, and the arithmetic will take any mask it is given. Feeding the asserted pixels in is the one implementation mistake worth stating on its own, because it passes every check. An asserted pixel's depth reading belongs to whatever stood in front of the glass. So, the point it gives sits on that other object, somewhere between the camera and the glass being reported, and a patch of such points drags the answer across the gap between the two. 

The cost of this mistake is measured over the partly hidden glasses of the crowded layouts, using the renderer's own exact masks and no model anywhere in the chain. Naming the asserted pixels and leaving them out places a glass close to where it stands. Feeding them in instead puts the place about four times further out, and makes the fitted footprint roughly twice as wide as it should be. Across the whole scorecard, it multiplies the number of pairs merged into one report by more than ten. 

Nothing complains while this happens. The mask looks like a better mask, the footprint stays round, and the width stays inside the allowed range. What comes out is a plausible wrong answer of exactly the sort the first half of this document is about, reached by the repair rather than by the failure the repair is for.

When you put the split and the exclusion together, the value of the whole silhouette sits where you might not first look for it. Because the asserted pixels are left out, they contribute nothing to the place and nothing to the width. The measurement is exactly what the observed pixels alone would have given, and the completion supplies no measurement at all, because it has no reading to supply. What it does supply comes earlier than the measurement, and is worth more. The model proposes one region per glass, drawn around the shape the glass really has. This means the visible part of a hidden glass is attributed to the glass it belongs to, instead of being swallowed into the region of the glass in front, and the rectangle has room to hold the whole shape instead of stopping where the evidence stops. A glass that a modal segmenter leaves out of its report altogether is reported here as itself, and that is where the gain measured further down comes from.

The other side of this is that uncertainty is reported rather than hidden. Where the completion covers a glass the camera barely saw, what arrives is an outline with too little seen inside it to place, and such an outline is handed on as doubtful rather than placed from nothing. This solution raises that doubt far more often than the approach on the page about a fine-tuned instance segmenter does, and that is the completion working rather than failing. The glass is proposed, and the arithmetic says plainly that what was seen of it will not place it. A glass reported as doubtful is a result in this project, and not a failure.

Once the two parts are separate, their ratio is available, and it is the visible fraction defined above. Like the vote spread covered on the page about a network trained from scratch, it is a confidence score that costs one division and comes from the answer itself, rather than from the model's opinion of itself. It should travel with every reported glass, and it should be calibrated once. To do this, run the trained model over held-out renders where the truth is known, and record how far the fitted centre and width land from the truth at each level of visible fraction. This turns the raw ratio into a statement about expected error, rather than an uninterpreted number.

You can trust such an answer for a position to go and look at without hesitation, and that is what the completion is for. A slightly wrong position just costs a picture, and a glass reported in a slightly wrong place is better than a glass not reported at all. 

However, you may never trust it for a measurement that decides a grip, and the reason can be given exactly. The asserted pixels supply no reading, so the width is fitted only to the part the camera saw. For a mostly hidden glass, that part is a crescent, and a crescent gives a width under the truth. The completion neither invents that width nor repairs it. This project's rule is that the last millimetres are felt rather than driven. The fingers close until they touch and then check the width, and the glass comes down until the rim touches and then checks the weight transferred. A width fitted to part of a glass is exactly what that rule keeps away from the gripper. So, the width reported here is just a rough figure for planning and for the range check, and the real measurement still comes from the look from the side and from the fingers.

Finally, the fit error stops being a check over the whole mask. The fit error works as a check because real points scatter. Points off a real disc form a noisy disc, and a group that is not a disc produces a fit error saying so. But the asserted pixels were drawn by a model that has learned what a glass of this kind looks like from above, which means it has learned to draw circles. Those pixels lie on a circle by construction. Because of this, the fit error over the whole amodal mask is always small, whatever the truth is, and a check that always passes is not a check. 

The repair must not be skipped. You must compute the fit error over the observed part only. Those points are measurements, they scatter like measurements, and asking whether they lie on one disc is a genuine test of the group they came from. A region that has swallowed a neighbour, or a strip of table, will answer no to that test. What it cannot test is the completion, which supplies no point on the table to be tested. The check that does test the completion is covered later.

<!-- section: measuring-whether-it-works | Measuring whether it works -->

The next part of the page explains how to measure whether the model is actually working. The ordinary measure of a segmenter misleads here in two ways. This matters, because a model of this kind can be built, trained, and declared successful while completing nothing at all.

The ordinary measure is overlap, often called intersection over union. This is the number of pixels both the predicted and the true mask contain, divided by the number either of them contains. It is one for a perfect match, and zero when the two share nothing. 

The diagram illustrates three ways of scoring the same amodal prediction: overlap against the visible truth, overlap against the whole silhouette, and overlap over the hidden part alone. It also shows a footprint error curve against the visible fraction underneath. 

The first trap is to score the overlap against the visible truth, perhaps by keeping the measurement code from the previous solution, which compares each predicted mask with the pixels the camera can see. If you do this, every pixel of a correct completion lies outside that truth, so every one of them is counted as a mistake. The better the completion, the more such pixels there are, and the lower the score. Because of this, a model completing perfectly scores worse than one completing nothing, and the highest score available goes to a model that has learned to ignore the amodal target entirely. The general lesson here, which is explained more fully on the page about a network trained from scratch, is that a score that rewards doing nothing will be optimised by a model that does nothing.

The obvious correction is to compare the prediction against the whole silhouette, but on its own, that is still a poor guide. For most glasses, the hidden part is a minority of the silhouette, and for a glass with a clear view, it is nothing. Because of this, the overlap mostly reports how well the model traced the visible boundary, which is what the previous solution did and which the model was already good at. Averaged over a dataset, a model that never completes scores close to one that completes everything correctly, with the difference buried under the part both find easy.

So, the second measure has to isolate the part that is the point. You need to compute the overlap over the hidden part alone, which the simulator supplies exactly by subtraction. That number ignores every pixel the model could have got right by tracing a visible edge. Therefore, it is the only mask score here that says anything about the completion itself, and it is the one to watch during training.

However, both of those measures score masks, and a mask is not what this solution is for. What the pipeline consumes is a centre and a width, so the error in those is most of what decides whether this solution earns its place. To measure this, run the trained model over held-out renders, fit the footprint to each reported glass, and compare the centre and the width with the truth. Then, report the result as a curve against the visible fraction rather than as one average. A single average is where this failure hides, because the well-seen glasses are numerous and nearly perfect, and averaging them with the badly hidden ones gives a comfortable figure describing no case in particular.

Finally, count the glasses found, missed, and merged beside it, because what this solution changes shows up in those counts before it shows up in any footprint. A completion that attributes a crescent to the glass it came off adds a glass to the answer; it does not improve the footprint of a glass that was already there.

<!-- section: what-running-it-shows | What running it shows -->

The next part of the page explains what running the solution shows. These measures were taken over held-out scenes that the training never saw. They were taken at the cell's own survey height and from its three stations, on both kinds of layout, and scored by the standard scorecard shared by every pipeline in this project. The results fall into three findings, which are worth taking in order. The first finding says the idea works, the second says how much room is left for improvement, and the third says where the idea buys nothing at all.

First, on a crowded layout, the idea works. Compared to solution nine, using the same architecture, the same borrowed weights, and the same scenes, this solution finds about one in ten more glasses. Furthermore, the worst place error among the glasses it reports comes down by about a quarter. As explained in the part of the page about attribution rather than measurement, the reason for this improvement is attribution. What the solution adds are hidden glasses whose visible parts are correctly attributed to them, rather than just providing better measurements for the glasses that solution nine had already found.

Second, on a crowded layout, the performance is also at its ceiling. If you hand the renderer's own exact silhouettes to the same arithmetic, with no model anywhere in the chain, no more glasses are found than this solution finds. So, whatever it still misses is not missed because it needs a better segmenter. Instead, it misses them because a glass with no visible pixels at a station has nothing to extend. As covered in the section about completely hidden glasses, this sets a hard limit that no amount of training can move.

Finally, on a spawned layout, the completion buys nothing, and that is the honest half of the result. This solution and solution nine score identically there, right down to the same worst place error. Even exact whole silhouettes score the same as exact visible masks. The reason is that on a table the cell lays out for itself, what cuts a mask is the edge of the picture. A silhouette rendered from that station is cut off at that exact same edge, so the amodal target is exactly the same as the visible target, and the completion has nothing to add. This is why the two kinds of layout are scored apart from each other. If they were averaged into one number, it would hide the whole result.

<!-- section: the-risk-of-inventing-glass-where-there-is-none | The risk of inventing glass where there is none -->

The next part of the page discusses the risk of inventing glass where there is none. A model trained to extend evidence has an obvious failure direction, and it is the mirror image of the failure this solution is for. It can extend evidence that did not need extending, or extend a scrap of evidence into a whole object that is not actually there.

Two facts about this cell make that concrete. First, a narrow strip of glass pixels looks much the same whether it is the visible sliver of a mostly hidden glass, or simply the edge of something that ends there. The first should be completed, while the second should not. Second, splay stretches every outline in a picture from the top outwards, so the far edge of an outline can look cut off when the glass merely ends. This failure is loud where the one it replaces is quiet, and three cheap checks bound it.

It is worth pointing out to the reader that these three checks are work still to do, rather than work already done. The code provided with the document splits every reported mask into its observed and asserted parts, and keeps the asserted pixels out of the arithmetic, which is what the earlier part about reporting the two parts separately asks for. However, on the strength of what it then finds, it refuses nothing. No width is tested against the range the kind allows, no asserted part is intersected with the blind region, and the arithmetic it shares computes no fit error for anything to be tested against. Even the visible fraction is the plainest gap of the lot, because the split that would give it is computed for every reported mask and the ratio costs just one division, yet still no such number travels with the answer. So what the completion earns on its own is simply a position good enough to look from. The three checks below are what a cell has to wrap around this model before a completed mask may be acted on.

The first check uses the kind's own range of widths. Inventing glass means reporting a glass where none stands, and every report carries a width, so the range check from the start of the document has real work to do again here. The pixels of an invented region are pixels the camera saw something at, like bare table or the edge of a glass that ends there. They give points on the table and a width of their own, and a width outside the range this kind allows is reason enough to refuse the report and hand it on as doubtful. Notice what has changed. Against a truncated modal mask, the check was useless because the truncated width looked like a legal smaller glass. But against an invention, it catches the thing directly, since claiming a glass means claiming a footprint, and a claimed footprint either fits the kind or does not.

The second check is the one recovered at the end of the last section, and what it tests is the group of pixels rather than the completion. You fit a circle to the observed points and to nothing else, because an asserted pixel carries no reading to make a point from. Then you ask how far those points sit from the circle, which asks whether what the camera saw is a patch of one glass's disc. A region that has swallowed a neighbour, or a strip of table, or two glasses at once, answers no. The completion itself is left to the third check, which looks at the asserted pixels directly.

The third check does not involve the model at all, and it is the strongest of the three because it is geometry. As explained on the page about clustering on the table, the system already computes which pieces of the table no ray from the lens reached, using the glasses it found and the recorded camera pose. It calls that the blind region. A model claiming a glass continues behind the near glass is claiming something about a part of the scene the camera could not see, which is allowed. But a model claiming a glass continues across a patch the camera had a clear view of, and where bare table came back, is contradicting a direct observation.

So, you intersect the asserted part with the blind region for that picture. Anything asserted outside it is wrong on arithmetic alone, with no reference to the model, the training set, or the kind. The check is cheap, it needs nothing this project does not already have, and it is the reason a learned completion can be let near the arm.

Finally, there is a free test for the quiet version of this failure. One form of invention escapes all three checks: a model that completes a little on every glass, whether or not anything is in front of it. This reports footprints that are all slightly too wide and displaced slightly outwards, and no single answer looks wrong. The aggregate catches it, and the aggregate is free. On a glass with nothing in front of it, the amodal mask must equal the modal one. So you keep only the unobstructed glasses in the held-out scenes and measure what the model adds to them. The correct answer is nothing, and any systematic addition is a bias worth knowing about before the model is trusted.

<!-- section: how-the-concepts-fit-together | How the concepts fit together -->

The next part of the page explains how all these concepts fit together. 

Everything discussed so far forms a single chain, and it is worth following in order because each stage builds on what the previous one produced. It starts with a picture taken from the top. The model proposes rectangles that might contain a whole glass, including any parts that are hidden. For each rectangle it keeps, it predicts an amodal mask, which covers the entire silhouette of the glass, along with a score. 

Next, this mask is split into two parts. The first is the observed part, where the depth reading confirms that the surface seen actually belongs to this glass. The second is the asserted part, which is everything else. Every pixel in the observed part is then treated as a point on the table. However, an asserted pixel does not become a point at all, because the depth reading underneath it belongs to whatever object is standing in front. 

From those observed points, the system calculates the place and the width of the glass. It also calculates a fit error wherever the arithmetic computes one. Every one of these measurements comes only from the part of the glass that was actually seen. The silhouette has already finished its job by simply deciding which pixels belong to this glass.

After that, three checks have to run, and any one of these checks can reject the glass. First, the calculated width must fall inside the range allowed for that kind of glass. Second, the observed points must all sit on a single disc. Finally, the asserted part of the mask must lie entirely inside the blind region, which is the area that the geometry says this specific camera pose could not possibly see. 

If a glass passes all three checks, it is reported with its center, its width, and its visible fraction. If it fails any of them, it is reported as doubtful, along with a note about which check it failed. It is then handed off to a separate routine that moves the camera, which is explained on the page about moving the camera.

There are three main things worth holding on to from this process. First is the target decision. This is essentially the entire solution, because everything else remains exactly the same as in solution nine. Second is the separation decision, which is what keeps the system honest. This is crucial because the arithmetic and every surviving check rely on keeping the observed pixels and the asserted pixels strictly apart. The third and final point is the arithmetic decision, which is unchanged from every other solution discussed here. The model proposes a silhouette, and then the allowed range of widths, the observed points, and the blind region make the final decision to accept or reject it.

<!-- section: when-the-glasses-are-completely-hidden | When the glasses are completely hidden -->

The next section covers what happens when the glasses are completely hidden. Everything so far is about a glass with some of itself in the picture. This part is about a glass with none, and it is the honest limit of the whole idea, so it is worth being exact about why no amount of training moves it.

Start from what completion is. Amodal completion extends evidence. The model sees a boundary that stops, sees a surface in front of where it stopped, and continues the boundary behind that surface in the way a glass of this kind would continue. Every part of that description begins with something in the picture. The visible sliver says where the glass is, how wide it is, and how far the completion has to reach.

Now take the sliver away. A glass covered completely contributes no pixels, so there is no boundary that stops, no partial outline to continue and no scrap of surface to say which glass of the kind's range this is. There is nothing to extend, and a model that extends nothing produces nothing. The diagram shows a glass with a sliver visible being completed correctly, beside the same pair arranged so that nothing of the far glass reaches the picture, leaving the completion no evidence to start from.

The argument can be put more strongly than just saying it does not work, and it is worth putting that way because more training is the first thing anybody proposes. Take the scene with the covered glass, and the same scene with that glass removed. The two produce the exact same picture, pixel for pixel. No function of the picture can tell them apart. Not this model, not a larger one, and not one trained for longer on more scenes, because the thing that differs between the two scenes left no trace in the input. That is a fact about the input rather than about the model, and training cannot change facts about the input.

A model could be trained to mark a glass that might be behind this one, since the simulator can supply that label too, and it is worth saying exactly what such a model would be doing. It would be reporting where glasses tend to stand in scenes like this one, which is a statement about the range of arrangements rather than about this specific arrangement. That is inventing a scene rather than reading a picture, and it would mark a glass behind every tall glass, including all the times there is nothing there. Trading a silent miss for a confident invention is a bad trade where the next step is an arm moving, and it is the trade this project's rules refuse. Anything doubtful is reported, never guessed.

A guess about a part of the scene nobody observed is a question about geometry rather than about appearance, and the right machinery for it is not a segmenter. This project already has that machinery, in three pieces.

First, where a glass could have been hiding is worked out by the step that clusters on the table. This takes the glasses that were found, their widths and heights, and the camera's pose, computes the pieces of the table no ray from the lens reached, and keeps the ones large enough to hold the smallest footprint this kind allows.

Second, which of those patches is worth spending a picture on is decided by the step that asks if anything is hiding there, because moving the arm is the expensive resource here and most blind patches are empty.

Finally, taking the picture belongs to the step that moves the camera, which turns a place worth looking at into a pose the arm can reach.

One thing this solution does contribute there is that completion needs less of a glass than any other method here. Because of this, the point at which hiding becomes complete is further away with this model than without it, and glasses that would have gone missing entirely under a modal segmenter are found and placed from the sliver that is left, which is what the crowded layouts measure. The boundary moves, but it does not disappear. Beyond wherever it now sits, this solution has nothing to say, and says so.

<!-- section: a-worked-example | A worked example -->

The next part of the page walks through a worked example. Everything in this example follows from the cell's own geometry, and it takes one arrangement of glasses through three different positions.

First, consider the arrangement itself. A tall glass and a short glass of the same kind stand at the closest separation the cell guarantees, and the camera looks down from the top. The short glass is placed further out from the point directly below the camera than the tall one, along the same line running outwards from that point. Perspective splay throws both outlines outwards, and because the tall glass is taller, its outline is thrown further, meaning it sweeps across the ground where the short glass stands.

Next, consider what a standard modal segmenter reports in this situation. The tall glass comes back whole with a correct footprint. However, the short glass comes back as just a crescent down one side. When a circle is fitted to this crescent, the fitted centre is pulled towards it, and the fitted width comes out smaller than the true width. The fit error remains small because a patch of a disc is easily explained by a smaller disc. This incorrect width still lands inside the range allowed for this kind of glass, because a kind that spans everything from a small tapered glass to a large one has room for a short measurement. As a result, two glasses are reported with believable widths, but one of them is reported standing where no glass is actually standing, causing the arm to go exactly where it was told, which is the wrong place. Alternatively, the crescent might never be proposed as a region of its own at all, meaning only one glass is reported where two actually stand.

Now look at what this amodal solution reports instead. The short glass's mask includes the part hidden behind the tall glass, so the short glass forms a region of its own, and the crescent is attributed to it rather than being swallowed into the tall glass's region. The observed part is just the crescent, and the asserted part is the rest of the shape. This means the visible fraction is low, and this low fraction travels with the answer. The asserted pixels carry the tall glass's depth readings, so they are left out of the calculation. Instead, the place and the width come entirely from the crescent. The resulting place is good enough to send a camera to, and the width is under the true value. Because the crescent is a patch of one glass's disc, the fit error over the observed points is small. Furthermore, the asserted part lies in the tall glass's own shadow, inside the blind region, so the system does not claim anything in areas where the camera had a clear view. None of the three checks has anything to object to. Therefore, the glass is reported, placed well enough to look at again, and flagged as mostly inferred.

This flag is highly useful. Because the visible fraction is low, the reported width is only used to plan, not to grip. The arm moves to the side, stands back at the measuring standoff, and looks level. From this new vantage point, nothing is in front of the short glass. Its modal and amodal masks now coincide, and its footprint is actually measured rather than predicted. The amodal completion decided where to look, and the new look decided what is true.

Finally, consider what happens if you push the pair of glasses the other way. If you put the short glass exactly behind the tall one along that outward line, its whole outline disappears under the tall glass's splayed outline. There is no crescent at all, and therefore nothing to extend. Only one region comes back where two glasses stand, and the footprint fitted to it is the tall glass's own correct footprint. In this case, nothing in the answer is wrong; it is simply short by one glass. No check fires, because every check here only evaluates something that was actually found. That specific situation is the case handed over to geometry, and no version of this amodal solution can answer it.

<!-- section: running-it-yourself | Running it yourself -->

The next part of the page explains how to run the code yourself. The code for all three pre-trained solutions lives in a single folder and shares one environment, which is described in the readme file for problem two's pre-trained code.

You start by running a setup command once. This installs the environment and fetches the initial weights that the solution uses to start its fine-tuning.

After that, you can run the training and testing commands. These commands require you to specify the name of the solution, which in this case is amodal.

The standard test command scores the layouts that the cell spawns for itself. However, you can also specifically request the layouts where one glass actually stands in front of another. These crowded layouts are the only ones where this solution will perform differently from solution nine. You run this by using the test-crowded command, again specifying the amodal solution.

There is one more command, called floor, which runs the same scenes without any model at all. Instead, it feeds the renderer's own masks directly into the same arithmetic across both kinds of layouts. This establishes the baseline, or floor, that all other numbers are compared against, and it is how the cost of feeding in the asserted pixels was measured.

Finally, if you specify the solution name as mask R C N N during training, it will train solution nine instead. This uses the same architecture on the same scenes, but with the visible masks as targets. Running both the amodal and mask R C N N solutions through both the standard and crowded test commands is how the results in the section on what running it shows were arrived at. Those results demonstrated that completing the hidden parts pays off on the crowded layouts, while on the standard spawned layouts, the two solutions cannot be told apart.

<!-- section: what-it-needs | What it needs -->

The next part of the page covers what this solution needs. It requires everything that solution nine needs, along with two things of its own. 

First, it needs a deep learning framework and an environment to run it in. This is a large dependency for a cell where the recommended answer is just a page of arithmetic. It also needs the pre-trained weights fetched once, which is a file large enough that it belongs outside the repository. Next, it needs a graphics processor. The machine used here is an Apple M4 with a ten-core integrated graphics processor and memory shared between the processor and graphics. This is reached through the framework's MPS backend, falling back to the main processor otherwise. There is no NVIDIA card and no CUDA anywhere in this project, and the shared memory is exactly why a model of this size fits. Finally, it needs a file of weights that is kept in step with the world. Changing the lighting, the camera, the table, or the range of sizes a kind of glass is drawn from will leave the file quietly out of date, and no test of the code will notice this.

The two extra needs are both about the training data. First, it needs one extra render per glass per scene. This is because the amodal label for a glass is the identity map of that scene with the other glasses taken away, meaning rendering time grows with the number of glasses in a scene. That is a real cost, but still a small one, because it uses machine time rather than a person's time.

Second, it needs a training set spread across the whole range of visible fractions. The cell's standard placement rule produces a mixture weighted towards well-seen glasses. A model trained on that mixture will rarely meet the hard cases and will optimise for the easy ones. So, scenes have to be spawned with glasses far more heavily covered than the rule would ordinarily produce. The easy scenes must be kept too, in proportion, so the model does not learn that something is always hidden. The training set is built in this way, with the crowded layouts standing beside the spawned ones.

One difficulty is shared with the other two pre-trained solutions. It is the largest risk in all three, and it must not be buried, which is that the pictures are not photographs.

The cell's renderer produces a depth reading per pixel and a glass identity per pixel, but it does not produce colour. The model, and the weights it starts from, expect an ordinary colour photograph. To handle this, the code makes one. It shades the depth into a grey picture and repeats that picture across the three colour channels. This is a genuine difference from the pictures the weights were originally fitted on, and it is not a small one. Those weights carry knowledge about texture, shading, the colours things tend to be, and what edges in real photographs look like. A grey depth shading has none of those properties. How much of the borrowed knowledge survives is not known, and it is the first thing the measurements should be read for.

This matters slightly more here than it did for solution nine. Completion is a judgement about which of two surfaces is in front and about how a shape continues. In real photographs, the cues for that are largely about appearance, such as texture stopping, shading turning, or a shadow falling. A grey depth shading keeps the geometric cues but loses the rest. Fortunately, geometric cues are the stronger ones for upright solids on a flat table, so there is reason to expect this to work. However, a reason is not a result.

<!-- section: where-it-is-strong-and-where-it-breaks | Where it is strong and where it breaks -->

The next part of the page covers where this solution is strong and where it breaks. 

First, it reports a partly hidden glass as a glass of its own, which is the whole point of the solution. A standard modal segmenter will either swallow the crescent of a hidden glass into the region of the glass in front of it, or leave it out entirely for being too thin to propose. This solution, however, hands that crescent on and correctly attributes it to the glass it came from. On a crowded layout, this finds about one glass in ten more than before, and brings the worst place error down by about a quarter. However, what it does not do is measure the part nobody saw. Those hidden pixels carry another object's depth readings, so they are left out. This means the footprint is still only the footprint of the part that was seen, which makes it too small and displaced in the quiet way the first half of this document describes.

Second, it does nothing about the edge of the picture. This is the more common of the two causes, and that is measured rather than guessed at. If a glass has its outline cut by the camera frame, its whole silhouette is cut too, because a silhouette rendered from that station stops at that exact same edge. There is nothing to assert here, so this solution and solution nine give the exact same answer on every layout the cell spawns for itself. To deal with the frame edge, the cell instead surveys the scene from three different stations and asks them to agree.

Third, this approach costs almost nothing over solution nine. The architecture, the starting weights, the training pictures, and the arithmetic downstream are all exactly the same. The only change is the target that each mask is scored against, and the simulator supplies the label for that.

Fourth, part of its answer is asserted, and the model cannot explain itself. The asserted pixels are kept out of the arithmetic, so their cost is not a wrong measurement, but rather the standing invitation to use them as one. For example, a run that hands the whole silhouette on instead of just the visible part will place a glass several times further from where it actually stands, while every check still passes. Furthermore, a model that extends evidence can sometimes extend evidence that did not need extending. The three checks wrapped around it are what make this tolerable, and in the code beside this document, those checks are still to be built.

Finally, it is blind to a glass that is hidden completely. If there are no pixels, there is nothing to extend. That is simply a fact about the input, and it is a limitation shared with every method here that works from pixels. Instead, that problem is handed over to geometry.

<!-- section: the-general-ideas-behind-this | The general ideas behind this -->

The next part of the page explains the general ideas behind this solution. 

Nothing here is new. Amodal segmentation is an established task with its own datasets and measurements, occlusion reasoning is older than learned vision, learning from labels a simulator produces is standard in robotics, and the perceptual phenomenon the whole thing is named after was described in the middle of the last century. What is unusual is the combination: a cell narrow enough that a completion can be checked against a known range of shapes, and a simulator that makes the otherwise expensive label free and exact.

The first major concept is amodal completion and amodal instance segmentation. The task of predicting an object's whole extent, rather than only its visible pixels, was posed for learned vision by Li and Malik in their paper Amodal Instance Segmentation. They worked around the labelling difficulty by pasting objects over other objects, so that the whole extent was known by construction. Later, Zhu and colleagues had people annotate whole extents on real photographs for their paper Semantic Amodal Segmentation. They measured how well the annotators agreed, which is the cost this document escapes. Follmann and colleagues put the amodal target into an instance segmenter of exactly this shape in their paper Learning to See the Invisible. Ke, Tai and Tang go further in Deep Occlusion-Aware Instance Segmentation with Overlapping BiLayers. They model the occluder and the occluded object as two layers at once, which is the principled version of the near-and-far judgement this solution needs its model to make implicitly.

Amodal completion is normally the right tool where a later step needs the whole object rather than just the visible piece of it. This includes picking from a cluttered bin where the grasp is planned on the object's shape, tracking where an object passing behind something should stay one object, and reasoning about depth order. It is right here for the first of those reasons, because the circle fit is a computation on a whole shape.

It is not the right tool where the visible extent is what is wanted, which covers most measurement. If the question is how much of a surface can be seen, or where exactly it can be touched, a completion answers a different question and makes the answer worse. It is also poor where there is no strong prior on shape, because completing an object needs knowledge of how objects of that sort continue, and a model without it extends boundaries arbitrarily. For more details, you can consult the linked page on amodal perception, which covers both the phenomenon and the computational task.

Behind amodal completion sits an older and more general idea: occlusion reasoning and layered scene models. A picture is not a flat arrangement of regions, but a stack of surfaces at different depths. Wang and Adelson made that stack the representation itself in their 1994 paper Representing Moving Images with Layers. Hoiem, Efros and Hebert attacked the piece this solution needs directly in their 2011 paper Recovering Occlusion Boundaries from an Image. They asked, given a boundary between two regions, which of the two is in front? That question is unavoidable here, because completing the wrong side of a boundary produces a mask extending over a glass that is actually nearer the camera.

Occlusion reasoning is normally the right tool whenever a decision depends on what is behind something, such as planning a view, planning a grasp, or deciding whether an object has gone away or merely gone behind something. It is at its best when the geometry is simple enough to compute exactly, which is why this cell can do part of it with arithmetic. It is not the right tool when a scene is too irregular for the hidden region to have a usable form, which is the common case. There, the usual approach is to trace rays through cells, making the answer approximate. It is also no substitute for looking again. Knowing a region is unobserved tells you to observe it, and no amount of reasoning produces the observation.

The third idea is learning from labels that cost nothing. This is what makes this solution practical rather than merely correct, and every learned solution here rests on it. When the training pictures come from a renderer, the renderer already knows the answer, so the label is a by-product of making the picture rather than separate work. Richter and colleagues gave the clearest demonstration of the argument in their paper Playing for Data: Ground Truth from Computer Games. The amodal case is where it pays best of all. A renderer can exactly answer what an object would look like with nothing in front of it, whereas a person looking at a photograph cannot answer it at all.

The catch is that a model trained on renders has learned the renderer. The standard answer to this is domain randomisation, as described by Tobin and colleagues. This means varying everything you are not trying to teach over a range wider than reality, so the model cannot use any of it as a shortcut. Here, that means varying the light, the table's colour and texture, the glasses' tint and proportions, the camera pose, the exposure, the picture noise and the arrangement itself. Meanwhile, you hold fixed the lens, the upright glasses and the flat table, which are the task rather than nuisances.

This is normally the right approach wherever labels would otherwise be drawn by hand and a simulator of the task exists, which covers most of robotics. It is especially right where a person could not produce the label reliably even with unlimited time. It is not the right approach where the thing being learned is precisely what the simulator gets wrong. A renderer's treatment of appearance is its weakest part, so a model trained on renders to judge a fine appearance question is learning from the wrong teacher. That is the risk hanging over this solution's grey depth shading, which is why the risk is written down plainly rather than argued away.

The last idea is the psychology of amodal completion in human vision. This is the oldest idea, and it is where the word comes from. In 1964, Michotte, Thinès and Crabbé named the phenomenon in their work Les compléments amodaux des structures perceptives. They noted that part of a perceived object is present to the perceiver without being delivered by any sense. In his 1979 book Organization in Vision, Kanizsa showed with figures rather than argument how strong and how automatic the effect is, including cases where a boundary is perceived across a region containing nothing at all. In 1991, Kellman and Shipley turned it into a testable theory in their paper A theory of visual interpolation in object perception. They set out the geometric conditions under which two visible fragments are joined into one object behind an occluder, which is, in effect, a specification of what a completion model is supposed to compute.

Two things from this research matter here. First, completion in people is governed by the fragments and by nothing else. What is joined is decided by how the visible edges run towards each other. That is the restriction this document insists on, and it is why a glass with no fragments is outside the scope. Second, the effect is not a guess a person can inspect or switch off, which is a warning when reading a model's output. A completion looks exactly as solid as an observation, and the only way to tell them apart is to keep a record of which pixels came from where.

The psychology is the right thing to consult about what a completion should produce, because it describes a system that solves this problem well. It is the wrong thing to consult about how far to trust one, because a person does not have to hand a footprint to a machine that will move towards it. For more on this, you can refer to the linked pages on amodal perception and the Gestalt principle of good continuation, which is the rule of thumb the interpolation theories make precise.

<!-- section: where-it-sits-among-the-other-solutions | Where it sits among the other solutions -->

The next part of the page explains where this sits among the other solutions. 

This solution is exactly like solution nine, but with one change. Because of this, the comparison with solution nine is the only one that needs care, and it comes down to a single trade.

First, consider what the change buys. A partly covered glass is reported as itself, which in a crowded layout means about one glass in ten more is found, and the worst place error is about a quarter lower. Solution nine's masks are modal, so a partly covered glass gives it a crescent shape. That crescent is either swallowed into the region of the glass in front of it, or left out entirely for being too thin to propose. However, what the change does not buy is the footprint of the part nobody saw. The completion supplies no depth reading, and it does not reach the edge of the picture at all. Covering by a neighbour is the one cause the two solutions differ on, and on every layout the cell spawns for itself, they score exactly alike.

Next, consider what the change costs. There are four costs, and solution nine pays none of them. First, the glasses it reports that solution nine would have left out are the mostly hidden ones, so the widths it adds are the widths of crescents and have to travel with the visible fraction that says so. Second, the asserted pixels have to be named and kept out of the arithmetic before anything is fitted. A run that hands the whole silhouette on instead places a glass several times further from where it stands than solution nine would, with every check still passing. Third, the rectangles have to be proposed around whole silhouettes, which is a harder detection task. Finally, there is a new failure direction, which is inventing glass where there is none. Against the approach called cluster on the table, the comparison is more interesting, because that solution attacks the same difficulty from the opposite side and the two do not overlap at all. Its blind-region arithmetic asks where a glass could have been, and it looks at no pixels. It takes the glasses it found, their widths and heights, the camera's pose, and the splay geometry, and computes which pieces of the table no ray reached and which of those are large enough to hold the smallest footprint this kind allows. This solution, on the other hand, asks what the whole shape of the glass it can partly see is, and it looks only at pixels. It takes a fragment and extends it, and it has nothing to say about a place where no fragment exists.

Those are complements rather than alternatives. This solution shrinks the set of glasses that go missing by needing less of a glass to be visible. The blind-region arithmetic bounds what is left by naming the places a missing glass could still be standing. Neither can do the other's job. No completion finds a glass with no pixels, and no geometry says which glass a sliver of pixels came off.

The rest of the folder fits around those two. The solutions for splitting the blob in the picture, and a network trained from scratch, work from visible pixels only, so both inherit the truncated-footprint failure. The network trained from scratch names amodal segmentation as the repair without performing it. This solution performs it, and the part of the failure it reaches is which glass a truncated mask belongs to. The solution that segments anything and then keeps the glasses has the same gap, but it cannot close it this way, because its masks come from a model used as downloaded.

And where this solution stops, which is a glass covered completely with no fragment to extend, is where the argument leaves appearance altogether and becomes geometry. That is the second half of the cluster on the table solution, and not this document.
