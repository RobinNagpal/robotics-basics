Trajectory generation. This page explains trajectory generation, which is the step that turns a planned path into a target for every joint at every instant. It answers four questions. How do you join a list of waypoints into a smooth curve, and how do you then decide how fast to go along it without breaking the motors' limits? Why do smooth starts and stops matter so much on a real arm, and which libraries do this for you?

It is written for a reader who has read the overview of this chapter and the PID control page, which explains the loop that follows the targets made here. You also need to know what speed and acceleration are. Every number on this page comes from a real run of the diagram script.

Every arm that moves smoothly uses trajectory generation, whether you see it or not. In ROS 2 it happens inside MoveIt after planning, and again inside the controller between the points it is given. So knowing what it does explains a lot of odd behaviour, such as moves that take longer than expected, tools that wobble after the arm stops, and arms that jerk when driven from a slow stream of targets.

The first section explains what this page answers. A planner gives a path, which is a list of joint angles, called waypoints, that the arm should pass through. But it does not say when, so the same path can be driven in one second or in ten. The joint controller needs something else, because every millisecond it needs a target angle for each joint. So something must turn the list of waypoints into a trajectory, meaning a target angle for each joint at every instant, from the start of the move to the end.

That something must also obey the motors, because each joint has a highest speed and a highest acceleration it can manage, both given in the arm's data sheet. A trajectory that asks for more gives a joint that falls behind, a controller that hits its torque limit, and sometimes a safety stop. It should be gentle as well, because a move that starts or stops suddenly shakes the arm, spills what it carries, and blurs the pictures from a camera on the wrist. So this page shows how a trajectory is built to be both fast and gentle, and what each choice costs.

The idea in one sentence is that a trajectory generator joins the waypoints with a smooth curve and then chooses how fast to move along it at each moment, as fast as the limits on speed, acceleration and jerk allow, starting and ending at rest.

Here is an everyday example of the same choice, a lift in a tall building, whose route is fixed straight up the shaft. What the lift chooses is only how its speed changes, and it does not jump to full speed, because the passengers would be thrown to the floor. Instead it speeds up gently, travels at a steady speed, and slows down gently, and a good lift even softens the moment the speeding-up begins and ends. Since its designers chose the fastest ride that stays comfortable, a trajectory generator does the same thing for each joint of an arm.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step. First, it defines four quantities, because a trajectory has to control speed as well as position. Each one is the rate of change of the one before it. Position is the joint angle, in radians, where one radian is about 57.3 degrees. Speed is how fast the angle changes, in radians per second. With its direction included it is called velocity. Acceleration is how fast the speed changes, in radians per second squared. Finally, jerk is how fast the acceleration changes, in radians per second cubed.

The motors limit the first three of those directly, since speed is limited by the motor and gearbox, while acceleration is limited by the torque the motor can give. Jerk has no hard limit, but a sudden change of acceleration is a sudden change of torque, and that is what shakes the arm. So the rest of this page is about keeping all four under control.

Since position is the first of those four quantities, the first job is to join the waypoints into a continuous angle. The simplest way to do that is with straight lines, moving each joint at a steady speed from one waypoint to the next. The first diagram shows this for one joint with four waypoints, at zero, one, two and three seconds, and it also shows the other common choice, a cubic spline. The top panel shows the angle, while the bottom panel shows the speed.

With straight lines the speed is 0.8 radians per second, then minus 0.3, then 0.5, and at each waypoint it jumps from one value to the next in no time at all. Because a jump in speed needs an infinite acceleration, which no motor can give, the controller falls behind at every corner and the arm lurches. The speed also jumps from zero to 0.8 at the start and from 0.5 to zero at the end.

A spline is a curve made of pieces joined so that there are no corners. A cubic spline uses a cubic polynomial, a curve of the form a, plus b times t, plus c times t squared, plus d times t cubed, for each piece between two waypoints. The four numbers of each piece are chosen so that the curve passes through both waypoints, and so that the speed and the acceleration are the same on both sides of every waypoint. The version used here also sets the speed to zero at the start and at the end, so the joint starts and stops at rest. Finding all the numbers then comes down to solving one small set of linear equations, with one equation per waypoint.

The spline's speed changes smoothly, but two things about it are worth noticing. First, it peaks at 1.12 radians per second, which is higher than any of the straight-line speeds, because it swings a little past some waypoints. Second, its highest acceleration is 4.08 radians per second squared, so if this joint is limited to 2, the spline breaks that limit. This means a spline with fixed times gives smoothness without knowing anything about limits, which is the job of the next two steps: speed profiles and time-parameterisation.

Since a spline on its own knows nothing about limits, the speed along it has to be chosen separately. The simplest case is a move from rest at one angle to rest at another. Take a move of 1.2 radians, with a speed limit of 1.0 radian per second and an acceleration limit of 2.0 radians per second squared.

The fastest move that obeys both of those limits has three phases. First, it speeds up at the full acceleration of 2.0 until the speed reaches the limit of 1.0. That takes 0.5 seconds and covers 0.25 radians. Second, it travels at the full speed of 1.0. Slowing down at the end will also cover 0.25 radians, so 0.7 radians are left for this middle phase, which takes 0.7 seconds. Third, it slows down at the full acceleration, taking 0.5 seconds and 0.25 radians, to arrive at rest.

The whole move takes 1.7 seconds. Plotted against time, the speed rises in a straight line, stays flat, and falls in a straight line, and that shape is a trapezium, which is why this is called a trapezoidal profile.

However, a short move may never reach full speed. For a move of 0.3 radians, speeding up for 0.5 seconds would already use 0.25 radians and slowing down another 0.25, so there is no room for a steady-speed phase. Instead the joint speeds up for half the distance and slows down for the other half, which makes the speed plot a triangle. This move takes 0.775 seconds, and its top speed is 0.775 radians per second, which is below the limit.

The trapezoid is fast and simple, but its weakness is the acceleration, because at the start of each phase the acceleration jumps. It jumps from zero to 2.0, from 2.0 to zero, from zero to minus 2.0, and back to zero. Each of those jumps is a sudden change of motor torque.

Because those jumps in acceleration are the trapezoid's weakness, an S-curve profile adds a limit on jerk. This means the acceleration is no longer allowed to jump, and it has to ramp up and down in straight lines instead. That splits each speeding-up phase into three, with the acceleration rising, then steady, then falling, so the whole move has seven phases. The speed plot now has rounded corners and looks like a stretched letter S at each end, which gives the profile its name.

Take the same 1.2 radian move again, this time with a jerk limit of 10 radians per second cubed. The acceleration rises from zero to 2.0 over 0.2 seconds. It stays at 2.0 for 0.3 seconds. Then it falls back to zero over another 0.2 seconds, bringing the speed to 1.0 radian per second. Speeding up takes 0.7 seconds and covers 0.35 radians, and slowing down is the mirror image of it. That leaves 0.5 radians to travel at full speed, which takes 0.5 seconds, so the whole move takes 1.9 seconds.

The next diagram shows the trapezoidal and S-curve profiles for the same move, with one quantity per panel. The positions look almost the same, and the speeds differ only at the corners. But the difference is clear in the bottom two panels. The trapezoid's acceleration jumps, so its jerk is infinite at four instants, marked by red lines, while the S-curve's jerk never goes beyond 10.

The S-curve costs time, because here it takes 1.9 seconds instead of 1.7, which is about 12 per cent longer. So the next section shows what that extra time buys.

The extra time buys a steadier tool, because no arm is perfectly stiff. The links bend a little and the gearboxes twist a little, so the tool sits on the end of what behaves like a stiff spring. This means that when the joint's acceleration jumps, the tool is left behind for an instant and starts to swing, and that swing does not stop when the joint stops.

The diagram script models exactly this. The tool is joined to the joint by a spring that lets it swing 4 times a second, with very little damping, as a long, light arm might. It sits half a metre from the joint. Then the script drives the joint with each profile and records how far the tool swings.

The diagram shows that during the move both tools swing by a couple of millimetres, so the difference only shows up after the joint stops, in the grey area. After the trapezoidal move the tool is still swinging by up to 0.33 millimetres, and the swing dies away slowly, while after the S-curve move it swings by only 0.08 millimetres.

But how much the S-curve helps depends on how fast the arm itself swings. A table gives the largest swing left after the stop, for the same two moves, over a range of arm swing rates from 2 to 8 times a second. Three things stand out in that table. First, the S-curve leaves less swing for every rate listed. Second, it helps most when the arm swings fast compared with the 0.2 seconds the acceleration takes to ramp, which here means from 4 swings a second upwards. And third, for slow swings of 2 or 3 a second, neither profile is gentle enough, because the whole move is only a few swings long. The swing at 3 a second is especially large, leaving 7.84 millimetres of swing for the trapezoid and 3.83 millimetres for the S-curve, because the changes of acceleration in this move happen to line up with the swing and add to it. So for such an arm you would lower the jerk limit or the acceleration limit.

This is why the wobble matters on a real arm. A wrist camera that takes a picture while the tool is still swinging gets a blurred or shifted picture. That is why the wrist camera waits for a settle time before it takes one. In the same way, a gripper that closes while the tool is still swinging closes in the wrong place. So a smoother profile makes that wait shorter, and it often wins back the time the S-curve cost.

So far only one joint has moved, but an arm moves several joints at once, and each one has its own distance to go. If each joint simply used the fastest profile for its own distance, they would all start together and finish at different times.

A diagram shows this problem and its solution. The left panel shows three joints moving 1.2, 0.6 and 0.2 radians, each as fast as it can, so they finish at 1.70, 1.10 and 0.63 seconds. In between, the tool follows a bent route, because early on all three joints move and later only the first joint does, and that route is not the one the planner checked for collisions.

The right panel shows the usual fix, which is called synchronisation. The slowest joint sets the time of 1.70 seconds. Then the others are stretched to finish at the same moment, by lowering their top speed in proportion to their distance. This means every joint is the same fraction of the way through its move at every instant. So in joint space the arm moves along a straight line from start to goal, which is the line the planner checked. A point-to-point move on an industrial arm works this way.

The moves above each went from one pose to one other pose, but a planner's path has many waypoints and it bends between them. A trapezoid or S-curve for each straight piece would stop the arm at every waypoint, so instead the whole path is timed at once, and that is called time-parameterisation.

The idea behind it is to separate where from when. The path is described by one number, s, that runs from zero at the start to one at the goal, and every joint angle is a fixed function of s. This means time-parameterisation only has to choose how fast s increases at each moment.

At each point along the path, each joint's speed is the rate of change of s times how steeply that joint's angle changes with s there. So the speed limit of each joint sets a highest rate for s at that point. That rate is low where the path bends sharply, or where one joint has to move a lot for a small step along the path. The acceleration limits then add a similar rule for how quickly the rate of s can change. So the algorithm finds the fastest rate for s at every point that obeys all these rules at once, speeding up wherever it can and starting to brake early enough before every tight spot.

Two well-known algorithms do exactly this. Time-optimal trajectory generation, or TOTG, is MoveIt's default. It finds the fastest timing that respects the joint speed and acceleration limits. Time-optimal path parameterisation by reachability analysis, or TOPP-RA, does the same job with a different method. It works through the path in small steps and keeps the range of rates for s that can still be reached and still stop in time. It can take more kinds of limit, such as torque limits.

But neither of those two limits jerk, so Ruckig is the usual tool for that. It computes a jerk-limited trajectory from any current state, even a moving one, to a target, and it is fast enough to run inside a control loop every millisecond. That makes it the choice when the target keeps changing, as it does when the arm follows a moving object or a person jogs it with a joystick. MoveIt also uses Ruckig to smooth a timed path. However, it is worth knowing that the free version of Ruckig calls a cloud service when it is given intermediate waypoints.

An S-curve works the same way, with seven phases instead of three, but its formulas are much longer, which is one reason people use a library for it.

The fourth section covers where trajectory generation is used on a robot arm. Because every smooth move needs targets at the controller's rate, it turns up at several places. First, after every planned move, MoveIt runs time-parameterisation on every path its planners return, and optionally Ruckig smoothing after that. Second, inside the trajectory controller, ROS 2 is given a list of timed points, so between them it fills in a target for every tick by interpolation. Third, industrial point-to-point and straight-line moves use the synchronised trapezoidal or S-curve profiles described above. Fourth, carrying something that can spill or slide uses a lower acceleration and jerk limit to keep the object in place without slowing everything down. Fifth, before a picture or a grasp, an S-curve profile cuts the wobble after the stop, making the wait shorter. Sixth, it smooths a slow stream of targets, like those from a camera loop or a learned policy, filling in the targets between them to prevent torque kicks. Finally, following a moving target means the goal changes during the move, so an online generator such as Ruckig recomputes a smooth, limited trajectory from the current state every tick.

The fifth section explains where it is useful, and where it is not. Trajectory generation is useful whenever the arm moves through free space along a known path. But it stops being enough when the path itself must change during the move, or when contact matters more than timing. A table lists common problems and their fixes. If the limits in the arm's description are wrong or too high, the following error grows mid-move or the controller hits its torque limit; the fix is to measure the real limits and lower the scaling factors. If a trapezoid on a light, springy arm causes the tool to wobble, the fix is an S-curve with a jerk limit. If straight lines between many waypoints cause the arm to lurch, the fix is a spline through the waypoints, then time-parameterisation. If a spline with fixed times means the arm cannot keep up, the fix is time-parameterisation instead of fixed times. If the goal moves during the move and the arm stops and starts, the fix is an online generator that starts from the current speed. If joints are timed separately and the tool leaves the planned route, the fix is to synchronise the joints so all finish together. If the path bends sharply and the arm slows almost to a stop, the fix is to smooth the path first. Finally, if the trajectory is followed perfectly but contact with something means the part is still not in place, the fix is impedance and force control for the contact phase.

The sixth section lists libraries that provide trajectory generation. Writing a trapezoid yourself is easy, but writing a correct jerk-limited, synchronised, online generator is not, so most people use a library. A table lists the well-known ones. Ruckig is used for jerk-limited, time-optimal, online generation from any state. TOPP-RA handles time-parameterisation of a whole path, with speed, acceleration and torque limits. MoveIt 2 provides the default timing after planning, and optional jerk smoothing, while its Pilz industrial motion planner provides synchronised trapezoidal profiles for point-to-point and straight-line moves. The ROS 2 joint trajectory controller fills in targets between timed points by spline interpolation every tick. SciPy, Orocos KDL, and Drake provide various splines and trapezoidal profiles. For most arms running ROS 2, the practical choice is to let MoveIt time the path, and then to reach for Ruckig directly only when the target changes during the move.

The seventh section discusses why you need trajectory generation, and what it costs. It turns a path into a target for every joint at every tick, keeping each joint within its limits and starting and ending at rest. The obvious alternative is to skip it and send the goal straight to the joint controller as a step. But the P term in a PID controller answers the full error at once, so the joint takes off at whatever speed the motor allows and overshoots, and the integral term winds up. The route is then whatever the controllers happen to do, rather than the one the planner checked. The second alternative is to always use the fastest possible profile, but that causes a springy arm to keep swinging after the stop. The costs of trajectory generation are these. You must know the real limits of every joint. A jerk limit makes every move longer. Time-optimal timing runs every joint at its limit somewhere, leaving no margin for a heavier load. And because the timing is worked out before the move, a change of goal mid-move needs an online generator rather than a fresh plan.

The eighth section covers the learned alternative. A learned policy decides where to go next, so it does part of this job itself. An action chunking transformer gives a whole chunk of targets at once and blends the chunks where they overlap, so its motion is smooth without a hand-written profile, and its timing is copied from the person who showed the task. That wins when the right speed depends on the task. But a policy does not know the joints' limits, and it sends targets far more slowly than the controller runs. So a trajectory generator still sits under it, filling in the ticks between targets, and a programmed check keeps speed within set limits. For a planned move through free space, the generator alone is the right tool. Between the two sit movement primitives, which learn the shape of a move from demonstrations and replay it smoothly, but still need the limits checked on what they produce.

The final section shows how to use trajectory generation in Python. It provides two code examples so you can generate a jerk-limited move and a spline through waypoints, and know which choices the library leaves to you.

The first example uses Ruckig, because it is jerk-limited and able to start from whatever state the arm is in. The code sets up two joints, defining their current positions, velocities, and accelerations, as well as their target positions. It then sets the maximum velocity, acceleration, and jerk for each joint. Finally, it runs a loop that updates the generator every four milliseconds, sending the new positions and velocities to the joints, and passing the end state of that tick as the start state for the next. The loop runs until the move is finished, taking 1.383 seconds, with both joints synchronised to finish together.

The second example uses SciPy for when you already have waypoints and only want a smooth curve through them. The code defines a list of times and a list of waypoints, and creates a cubic spline that passes through them, ensuring zero speed at both ends. It then reads the position, velocity, and acceleration from the spline at a specific time.

The two libraries do very different amounts of work for you. Ruckig gives you the jerk limit, the synchronisation between joints, and the ability to re-plan from the current state on any tick. SciPy gives you only the curve. It never looks at your limits, so it will happily hand back a spline whose speed is twice what the arm can do, and checking that is your job.

What you write in both cases is the loop and the sending. Neither library runs a timer, so you call the update function once per tick from your own fixed-rate loop. What you have to decide or measure is a short list, but every item on it changes how the arm moves. The speed and acceleration limits come from the arm's manual, and they are per joint. The jerk limit is almost never in a manual, so you find it by lowering it until the wobble after the stop disappears. In the SciPy example, the times are your choice as well, and choosing them too close together produces a spline the arm cannot follow. Finally, the units are yours to keep straight, because the libraries do not know whether your numbers are radians or degrees and will not warn you.
