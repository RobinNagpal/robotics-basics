Control and motion. This chapter is about turning a planned path into smooth, safe motor commands. It covers five techniques: proportional-integral-derivative, or PID, control, trajectory generation, arm dynamics, safety monitoring, and impedance and force control. All five are written sets of rules that a program follows many times a second, which means none of them is trained from data. 

This overview answers four questions: what job do these techniques do for an arm, what does each of the five do in one line, how do they work together on one real move, and how does this chapter connect to the rest of the book and to learned movement models. It assumes you know what a joint, a joint angle, and a motor are, and it helps to know about planning and search, because this chapter starts where that one stops. You do not need any control theory beforehand. Every number mentioned comes from a real run of a diagram script that simulates a joint, a move, and a contact, rather than drawing them by hand.

The first part of the page explains what control and motion is for. The main job is turning a planned path into smooth, safe motor commands. Think of carrying a full mug of coffee from the kitchen to a desk. Since you already know the route, what is left is how you walk it. You do not start at full speed, because the coffee would slop, and you do not stop dead at the desk for the same reason. While you walk, you keep correcting your hand, because the mug is never quite where you meant it to be. Then when you put the mug down, you lower it until you feel the desk, and only then do you stop pushing.

Because an arm carrying a mug faces the same problem, it has the same three jobs every time it moves. First, it must decide how fast to go along the route at each moment, without going faster, or speeding up faster, than the motors allow. Second, it must make each motor actually follow that plan, even though gravity, friction, and the load all push the joint off course. Third, it must behave sensibly when it touches something. A mug put down on a table, a peg pushed into a hole, and a person bumping the arm all need the arm to give way a little instead of pushing harder.

The planner from the planning and search chapter does none of those three jobs, because all it hands over is a list of joint angles to pass through. It says where to pass but not when, and it knows nothing at all about motors or contact.

Because those three jobs are so different from one another, the work is split into layers, where each layer answers one question and hands its answer to the layer below it. 

The trajectory layer answers the question of where each joint should be at each instant. A trajectory is simply a path with times attached to it. Since the same path can be driven slowly or quickly, and with gentle or sudden changes of speed, this is the layer that chooses which of those it will be.

Next, the controller layer answers what torque each motor should produce right now, so that the joint is where the trajectory says. Torque is a turning force measured in newton metres. The controller reads the joint's sensor, compares the reading with the target, and chooses a torque. It does this again and again, often a thousand times a second. This repeated correction is called a feedback loop, because the measurement is fed back into the next decision.

The controller does that job better when it knows the arm's own body, and the arm's dynamics are what describe that body. They say how much torque each joint needs to hold the arm up against gravity and to speed it up. Because a controller can add that torque before any error appears, it follows fast moves far more closely than one that waits for an error to show up first.

Then, the contact layer answers what should happen when the arm touches something. A plain position controller has only one answer, which is to push harder until the joint is where it was told to be. Instead, the contact layer lets you choose the behaviour, such as acting like a soft spring or stopping when the force passes one newton.

Beside all three layers runs a safety monitor. It does not command the arm itself. Instead, it watches the commands and the measurements, and it stops the arm when a speed, a force, a position, or a distance to a person goes outside its limit, or when a loop stops sending commands. These layers appear in ROS 2 as the ros2_control framework and its controllers, which is covered in the framework chapters, while this chapter explains the techniques inside them.

The next section introduces the five techniques. They are split into two groups based on how often you will meet them. The most used group holds four techniques that run on nearly every arm, every time it moves: PID control, trajectory generation, arm dynamics, and safety monitoring. Even an arm that only moves between fixed poses uses all four, although the dynamics and safety checks are often hidden inside the arm maker's controller. The also used group holds impedance and force control. This is used often, but only in tasks where the arm touches things on purpose, such as pressing, inserting, or being guided by hand. It also needs an arm or a sensor that supports it.

Here is what each of the four most used techniques does. First, PID control makes one joint follow its target. It adds up three pushes: one in proportion to the error now, one that grows while an error lasts, and one that brakes when the error is changing fast. It is the loop that runs inside nearly every joint of nearly every arm. Second, trajectory generation decides where each joint should be at each instant. It turns a list of waypoints into smooth curves, and gives them a speed profile that keeps within the motors' limits of speed, acceleration, and jerk. Third, arm dynamics works out the torque each joint needs. This is inertia times acceleration, plus the pull of gravity, plus a part that grows with speed. It holds the arm still with no error, lets a controller follow fast moves closely, and lets a simulator predict how the arm will move. Fourth, safety monitoring watches the arm's software limits, like joint positions, speeds, torques, forces, and the distance to people. A watchdog, which is a timer that every new command must reset, stops the arm when a loop stops sending commands. Speed and separation monitoring slows the arm as a person comes closer, and stops it if they come too close.

Finally, the remaining technique in the also used group is impedance and force control. This decides how the arm behaves in contact. It makes the arm act like a spring with a stiffness you chose, stops a move when a force sensor fires, and keeps contact forces inside a limit.

The page then presents a table setting these five techniques side by side. For each technique, it lists what goes in, what comes out, how often it runs, and the sign of a bad setup. The general pattern is that the planning and trajectory layers run once per move or at slower rates, while the control, dynamics, and safety layers run very fast, typically five hundred to a thousand times a second. 

For example, trajectory generation takes in waypoints and joint limits, and outputs a target angle for every tick of the controller. It runs once per move. If it is set up badly, the arm jerks at the start and end, or the tool wobbles after it stops. In contrast, PID control takes in the target angle and the measured angle, and outputs a torque or a motor current. It runs a thousand times a second or more. If a PID controller is set up badly, the joint overshoots and rings, or stops a little short of the target. As a final example, impedance and force control takes in the target pose and the measured force or position, and outputs a force, a torque, or a changed target position. If it is set up badly, the arm pushes too hard on contact, bounces off, or buzzes against a hard surface.

To show how these layers work together on one move, the page describes a simulated move of a two-joint arm. The diagram shows one move passing through the three layers, broken into three panels over a two point four second trial. The left panel shows the planner's output of four waypoints, along with the smooth curve that the trajectory layer draws through them. The middle panel shows the timing, meaning the speed of each joint over the move. The right panel shows the error of each joint, which is the target angle minus the measured angle.

Three things in this process are worth noticing. First, the planner's waypoints have no times of their own, so the trajectory layer is what chose them. In this run, it gave each stretch of the path a share of the two point four seconds in proportion to its length, so both joints start and end at zero speed. 

Second, the controller never follows the trajectory exactly, because it lags behind while the joint is moving. The largest error is three point four degrees on joint one and four point three degrees on joint two. This lag is called the following error, and it is perfectly normal. This is why a controller with its tolerances set to zero will always fail a move. 

Third, the error is still not zero when the trajectory ends. At two point four seconds, joint one is still one point eight five degrees off, and half a second later it is still zero point nine six degrees off. This happens because the controller keeps working after the trajectory has stopped changing, and it closes the remaining gap slowly. This is called settling time, and it means a move that is done by the clock is not always done at the joint. 

This specific move has no contact in it, so the contact layer never runs. However, if the gripper were lowering a mug onto a table, the last few millimetres would be handed to that layer.

The next section explains that these loops run at different rates. A planner runs once per move and may take a tenth of a second or more, while a controller runs every millisecond. But some sources of targets sit in between those two. A camera-based loop may send a new target thirty times a second, and a learned policy often sends one ten to fifteen times a second.

Because of that gap, something has to fill it between a slow stream of targets and a fast controller. A diagram illustrates this by showing the same ten targets a second sent to the same joint in two different ways. The first way sends each target straight to the controller and holds it there for one hundred milliseconds. Because every new target is a small step, the controller answers each step with a sudden rise in torque, causing the arm to move in small jerks. The second way moves the target smoothly, a thousand times a second, from the previous value to the newest one. This gives a smooth torque, with very little change from one millisecond to the next. But this smooth run has a cost of its own, because it is always one target behind, a tenth of a second late. This trade-off is why learned policies still need the trajectory and controller layers underneath them. The general rule is that every loop should hand the loop below it a signal that changes smoothly at the lower loop's own rate.

Because everything discussed so far turns a decision that has already been made into motor commands, control and motion sits at the end of the chain. The techniques in the other chapters decide what to do and where to go, and this chapter is what makes it actually happen. 

For example, planning and search hands over the path. Trajectory optimisation can already give a path with times, and then the trajectory layer here only checks and smooths it. Numerical inverse kinematics turns a target pose for the gripper into joint angles. Fitting and estimation cleans up the signals the controllers read, like using a Kalman filter to smooth a noisy joint speed or force reading. Geometry and cameras provide the frames that a Cartesian impedance controller works in, because stiffness along the tool only means something once the tool frame is known. Decisions and task logic decide when to switch layers, such as moving fast in free space, then switching to a guarded move, then switching to impedance control to press.

Learned models do parts of this job as well. A movement model decides where the arm should go next from camera pictures, replacing the planner and sometimes the trajectory layer, but its output is still a stream of joint targets that a PID loop must follow. A learned arm model predicts the torque a joint needs, usually correcting the physics model rather than replacing it. A force and slip model reads the touch signals that a force controller could then act on. None of these removes the need for programmed loops, because they sit on top of those loops rather than in place of them.

The final section shows how to put this stack of layers into Python, so you can see which library covers which layer and how they hand values to each other. The code sets up a loop that moves a two-joint arm to a new pair of angles, ticking once every two milliseconds. It uses three libraries, each covering exactly one layer. 

First, it uses a library called Ruckig for trajectory generation. Ruckig takes the target angles and the motors' limits for speed, acceleration, and jerk. It turns the target into a jerk-limited position, speed, and acceleration for every tick. 

Second, it uses a library called Pinocchio for arm dynamics. Pinocchio reads the arm's physical description, like its masses and lengths, and computes the torque that the wanted acceleration needs, so you never have to write the equations of motion yourself. 

Third, it uses a library called simple_pid to create one PID controller per joint. This corrects whatever error is left over, keeping the error history and clamping the output to the limits you provide. 

Inside the main loop, the code asks Ruckig where each joint should be on this tick. It passes that motion to Pinocchio to find the required torque before any error appears. Then it reads the actual joint angles, feeds them to the PID controllers to find the feedback torque, adds the two torques together, clamps them for safety, and sends them to the motors. 

Everything else in that loop is your own code. Reading the joint angles, sending the torques, and clamping to limits depend on your specific arm and driver. You also have to run the loop at a steady rate, handle late ticks, and manage the state machine to start or pause the move. 

The numbers are yours too. Speed and acceleration limits come from the manual, but the jerk limit is usually found by lowering it until the arm stops wobbling. The PID gains come from tuning the real joint, and the physical masses come from the manufacturer or your own measurements. The loop rate must match what your program actually achieves, because a PID tuned at five hundred ticks a second behaves differently at one hundred. 

Finally, the page gives a caution. On most industrial arms, you cannot send torques at all, because the joint loop runs inside the maker's drive. In that case, only the trajectory generation layer stays in your program, and you send positions instead of torques.
