Iterative closest point. 

This page explains iterative closest point, or ICP: a method that moves one set of points onto another set of points that shows the same shape. On a robot arm, the first set is usually a stored model of a part, and the second is a depth scan of the real part. So ICP answers the question "exactly where is the part, and which way is it turned?". This page shows how ICP works, round by round, on a small example with real numbers. It then shows when ICP gives a wrong answer, and how you can tell that it has.

It is written for a reader who has already read the page on nearest-neighbour search, because every round of ICP runs that search. It also helps to know what a rotation and a shift are, from the page on rigid transforms, although this page explains each term where it needs it.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. ICP guesses which scan point each model point belongs to by taking the closest one, moves the model to fit those guesses as well as possible, and repeats until the model stops moving.

The move ICP finds is a rigid transform, which means a rotation plus a shift with no stretching and no bending. That is the right kind of answer, because a part lying on a table cannot change its shape.

Here is an everyday example of the same loop. Suppose you have a paper outline of a key, and you want to lay it over the real key lying on a table. You put the paper down roughly where the key is. Then you see that each edge of the paper sits a little to the left of the matching edge of the key. Because of that, you slide the paper to the right and turn it a little. Now the edges are closer, which means you can see better which edge of the paper goes with which edge of the key, so you slide and turn again. After a few small moves the paper sits on the key, and ICP does the same thing with points instead of edges.

The next section explains how it works, step by step. It walks through that same loop on one small example, one round at a time. So every claim made about it here comes with a number you can check.

The example is an L-shaped metal bracket lying flat on a table, seen from above by a depth camera. It is kept flat only so that the pictures fit on a page. A real scan has three coordinates per point, but every step below works the same way in 3D.

First, the model is the bracket's outline as it was drawn: 120 millimetres long, 80 millimetres high, with arms 30 millimetres wide. It has one point every 10 millimetres round the outline, 40 points in all. Second, the scan is what the camera saw, with one point every 6 millimetres and 66 points in all. Each point is moved by a small random amount, the way a real depth camera's points are. That random amount has a standard deviation of 0.8 millimetres in each direction, and the standard deviation is the typical size of a random error. Third, the real bracket is turned 20 degrees from the model and shifted by 30 millimetres in x and 20 millimetres in y, and ICP is not told any of this, because finding it is its job. Finally, ICP starts from a first guess: the model exactly where it was drawn, not turned and not shifted.

All the numbers on this page come from a diagram script which runs exactly the ICP described here, so you can run that script to print the numbers yourself.

Step one is to pair each model point with its closest scan point. Because ICP does not know which scan point belongs to which model point, it starts by guessing: each model point is paired with the scan point nearest to it. That guess is a nearest-neighbour search run once for every model point, and the pairs it produces are called correspondences.

A diagram here shows the pairs that this first search produces, before the model has moved at all. Many of these pairs are wrong, because the model points along the left edge all reach across to the scan's left edge, which is higher up and turned. Several model points even share one scan point. The mean length of the lines connecting the pairs, called the mean gap on this page, is 15.7 millimetres.

The pairs are wrong, but they are not random, because on the whole they pull the model up, to the right, and anticlockwise. That is already enough, since the next step only needs to move the model in roughly the right direction.

Step two finds the best move for those pairs. Once the pairs exist, ICP works out the one rotation and shift that brings each model point as close as possible to its partner. "As close as possible" means that the sum of the squared distances between partners is as small as it can be. There is an exact method that finds this move, and it takes three steps.

First, find the middle of the model points, and the middle of their partners. The middle is the average of all the x values and of all the y values. It is called the centroid. Second, measure every model point from the model's centroid, and every partner from the partners' centroid. Now both sets are centred on the same spot. Find the turn that lines the first set up with the second as well as it can. A standard piece of linear algebra, the singular value decomposition, or SVD, gives this turn directly. The method is known as the Kabsch method, or the Umeyama method. Third, the shift is whatever then moves the turned model's centroid onto the partners' centroid.

Here is the same method on three points, which is small enough to check by hand. The model is a triangle with corners at zero zero, 40 zero, and zero 20. The partners are that same triangle turned 30 degrees and shifted by 10 and 5, so their corners are at 10 and 5, 44.64 and 25, and zero and 22.32.

First, the model's centroid is at 13.33 and 6.67. The partners' centroid is at 18.21 and 17.44. Second, centred on their centroids, the two triangles differ only by a turn. The SVD finds it: 30.0 degrees. Third, turning the model's centroid by 30 degrees about the origin moves it to 8.21 and 12.44. The shift that takes it to 18.21 and 17.44 is 10 and 5.

The method finds the turn and the shift exactly, because in this small example the pairs are right. In ICP itself the pairs are only guesses, so the move the method finds is only partly right. The model is then moved by it, and ICP goes back to step one.

This repeats until the gap stops shrinking. Because each move brings the model closer to the scan, the pairs found after it are better, which means the next move is better as well. A diagram shows the model at the start, after one round, after four rounds, and at the end. After one round the model has barely turned, after four rounds it is part of the way there, and after 18 rounds it sits on the scan.

A table gives the numbers after some of the rounds. The mean gap falls, while the turn and shift grow toward the real answers of 20 degrees and a shift of 30 and 20 millimetres. At the start, the mean gap is 15.74 millimetres with zero turn and shift. By round 4, the gap has fallen to 7.62 millimetres, and the model has turned 7.77 degrees. By round 18, the gap stops shrinking at 1.70 millimetres, with a turn of 19.18 degrees and a shift of 30.0 and 20.3 millimetres.

ICP also needs a rule for when to stop. The rule here is to stop when the mean gap falls by less than 0.001 millimetres in one round, which happened after round 18. Real libraries use the same kind of rule, and they add a largest number of rounds, such as 30 or 50, so that ICP always ends whatever the scan looks like.

A graph plots the mean gap after each round of this run. The gap falls fast for the first twelve rounds, then hardly changes, and that is when the stop rule ends the loop.

The answer ICP reaches is 19.2 degrees instead of 20 degrees, and 30.0 and 20.3 millimetres instead of 30 and 20 millimetres. As a result, it is close but not exact, and there are two reasons for that. The first reason is that the scan's points carry noise. The second reason is that the model's points and the scan's points do not sit at the same places along each edge, because one set is spaced 10 millimetres apart and the other 6 millimetres. So even at the right pose, each model point's closest scan point is up to 3 millimetres along the edge from it. This is also why the mean gap ends at 1.70 millimetres rather than at the 0.8 millimetres of noise.

The pseudocode for this process starts by setting the pose to the first guess and moving the model points by that pose. It then repeats a loop up to a maximum number of rounds. In step one of the loop, it finds pairs. For each moved model point, it finds the nearest scan point using a k-d tree built once beforehand. If the distance between them is less than a maximum limit, it keeps the pair. It then calculates the mean gap over all pairs. If the gap has shrunk by less than the stop rule since the last round, the loop stops. Otherwise, it moves to step two, finding the best move for these pairs. It finds the centroids of the model points and scan points in the pairs, uses singular value decomposition to find the best rotation, and calculates the shift. It applies this move to the model points and adds it to the total pose. Finally, it returns the pose and the gap.

The scan does not move during the loop, so its k-d tree is built once, before the first round. The maximum pair distance test then throws away pairs that are too far apart to be real. That test matters when the scan also holds other things, such as the table or a neighbouring part.

The version above is called point-to-point ICP, because it measures the gap from each model point to one scan point. But that measure is slow when a surface can slide along itself, which is why the long flat edges of the bracket took many rounds to slide into place in the example.

Point-to-plane ICP is a faster version. It measures the gap from each model point to the flat surface through its partner instead. That surface is set by the partner's normal, which is the direction straight out of the surface. With this measure a model point that slides along the right surface costs nothing. So ICP spends its moves only on the direction that matters, and it usually needs far fewer rounds. However, it needs normals for the scan, which means one more k-nearest search per scan point before ICP starts, and both Open3D and PCL provide the method.

The third section covers where ICP is used on a robot arm. Because ICP sharpens a pose rather than finding one from nothing, it is used wherever the arm already has a rough idea of where something is and needs an exact one. First, it is used for finding the exact pose of a known part. A detector finds roughly where a part lies in a bin, and ICP lines the part's CAD model up with the depth scan to give a pose accurate enough to grip it. Second, it finishes a learned pose. A learned pose model, like those explained in the page on keypoints and object pose, gives a pose that is often a few millimetres or degrees off. A few rounds of ICP fix the last part of the error. Third, it joins several views into one cloud. A camera on the wrist takes pictures from several arm poses, and ICP lines each new cloud up with the ones before it to remove small errors. Fourth, it follows a part that moves a little. When a part is being pushed, its pose in one frame is a good first guess for the next frame. Fifth, it checks a placement. After the arm sets a part down, a scan and ICP against the model say where the part actually ended up. Finally, it checks calibration. If a scan of a known object at a known place has to be moved by several millimetres to line up with its model, the calibration has drifted.

The next section discusses where it is useful, and where it is not. All the jobs just mentioned rely on ICP being precise, which it is whenever it starts close to the answer. The danger is that ICP always returns an answer, even when that answer is wrong. It stops as soon as the gap stops shrinking, and the gap can stop shrinking at a wrong pose.

A diagram shows what happens when ICP starts from a wrong pose. It shows the same bracket scanned twice, once turned 20 degrees and once turned 120 degrees. From 20 degrees away, ICP finds the bracket. From 120 degrees away, however, it stops after 9 rounds with the model turned minus 5.9 degrees and lying across the scan. Its mean gap there is 13.7 millimetres, against 1.7 millimetres for the good result. That difference is the sign to watch for, because a final gap much bigger than the camera's noise is what a wrong pose looks like. Such a stuck answer is called a local minimum, which is a pose from which every small move makes the gap worse, although it is not the best pose overall.

A table lists common problems, the signs you see, and what people use instead. If the first guess is too far off, the final gap is much larger than the camera noise. Instead, people use a global method first, like RANSAC on matched surface features, or run ICP from several starting turns. If the part is symmetric, the gap is small but the turn is random from run to run, so you must accept the unknown turn or use colour. If the scan holds the table or other parts, the model is pulled towards them, so you should cut the part out of the scan first and reject pairs that are too far apart. If only part of the object is visible, the model slides to cover the missing side, so you should use a model of only the side the camera can see. Large flat surfaces cause the model to slide and never settle, which is helped by point-to-plane ICP and using features like edges to fix the position. If the model is in millimetres and the scan in metres, you get a nonsense answer after one round, so check the units. Finally, if ICP is too slow on large clouds, downsample both clouds to a point every few millimetres first.

Section five explains getting a first guess using 3D features and global registration. Since ICP needs a first guess close to the answer, this section shows how a program can get one from the scan alone, with no idea where the part is or which way it is turned. The method is called global registration, which searches every possible pose rather than only the poses near a guess. It does so in four steps. First, work out which way the surface faces at every point, which is the point's normal. Second, describe the shape round every point as a short list of numbers, called a local 3D feature. Third, pair each model point with the scan point whose feature is most alike, and keep the pairs that agree on one rigid move, with RANSAC. Fourth, use that rough move as ICP's first guess.

The first of those four steps is to find surface normals. A single point on its own has no direction, so the program looks at the point's neighbours instead. It finds the point's 5 nearest neighbours and fits the straight line that passes closest to them. Then it takes the direction at right angles to that line. In 3D it fits a flat plane instead of a line. The fit uses principal component analysis, or PCA, which finds the direction in which a set of points is spread out least. A diagram shows the normal at every point of the scan. On the straight edges those normals are neat and parallel, but at the corners they point at an angle, because the neighbours there come from both edges. Real programs turn every normal to face the camera, because a camera can only see surfaces that face it.

The second step is to find local 3D features. A normal only says which way a surface faces, so to tell points apart, the program describes the shape of the whole neighbourhood round each point. The best-known description is the fast point feature histogram, or FPFH. For one point, FPFH looks at every neighbour within a set radius. It measures how the neighbour's normal is turned relative to the point's own normal, and counts how many neighbours fall into each range of angles. Those counts form a histogram. FPFH has 33 bins in all, so two points on similar shapes get similar histograms, while a point on a flat surface gets a very different one.

A diagram shows three points on the bracket and their features. Point C, in the middle of the long edge, has two thirds of its neighbours far away and facing the same way, because the edge there is straight. Point A, at the outer corner, has neighbours on two edges at right angles, so a share of them falls in the far and across bin. Point B, at the tip of the long arm, sees the short end of the arm and the inner edge, and so it has a different mix again. Points along the middle of a straight edge all look alike, which is why the corners and the arm ends are what make the bracket recognisable.

A second well-known kind of feature is the point pair feature, or PPF, which describes two points at a time. For two points with normals it stores the distance between them, the angle between each normal and the line joining them, and the angle between the two normals. A program stores these four numbers for every pair of points on the model. In the scan, each pair of points looks itself up in that table, and every model pair it matches votes for a pose, so the pose with the most votes wins.

The third step is matching features, then RANSAC. The program pairs each model point with the scan point whose feature is most alike. Most of these pairs are wrong, because edge points all look alike. RANSAC does not mind that most pairs are wrong, because it repeats a series of steps many times and keeps only the move that the most pairs agree with. First, it picks two pairs at random, or three in 3D. Second, it checks that the two model points are about as far apart as the two scan points. If not, it skips the try at once. Third, it computes the rigid move that fits the pairs. Fourth, it counts the pairs that the move brings within 5 millimetres of each other. Finally, it remembers the move with the most such pairs.

A diagram shows feature matches between the model and the turned scan. RANSAC keeps the few that agree. In the example, RANSAC found a move that 5 pairs agree with on its 90th try out of 500, and those 5 pairs are shown as green lines. Every one of the 35 red pairs is thrown away. Fast global registration, or FGR, is a common alternative to RANSAC for this step. It fits one move to all the pairs at once, giving less weight to pairs that disagree, and is usually faster.

The final step is finishing with ICP. The move from RANSAC rests on a handful of pairs, so it is only rough, and ICP is what makes it exact. A diagram and table compare three results for the bracket turned 120 degrees. ICP alone gets stuck after 9 rounds with a mean gap of 13.7 millimetres. Features and RANSAC alone give a turn of 118.7 degrees and a gap of 2.6 millimetres. But features and RANSAC followed by 5 rounds of ICP give a turn of 118.9 degrees and a gap of 1.7 millimetres. The feature pose alone is already within about 1.3 degrees of the right turn, so ICP needs only 5 rounds to finish the job.

Everything above works the same way in 3D, with three changes. The normal comes from a plane fitted to the neighbours, RANSAC picks three pairs at a time, and the clouds are first downsampled to one point every few millimetres to save time. On a robot arm, global registration is used for picking a known part that can lie any way up, getting going again after tracking is lost, joining two scans with no idea how the camera moved, and checking an ICP result.

It also fails in predictable ways. If the part has few distinct shapes, there are few RANSAC inliers, so people use colour or a learned pose model. If the normal and feature radius do not suit the scan, features become noise, so the radii must be adjusted. If the part is a small share of a cluttered scan, RANSAC might fit the move to the table, so the part should be cut out first. And if the part is symmetric, the pose flips between look-alike turns.

The sixth section lists libraries that provide ICP. You do not have to write any of this yourself. Open3D provides an ICP registration function, along with point-to-point and point-to-plane variants, as well as global registration with RANSAC on features. Point Cloud Library provides iterative closest point and a generalised version. OpenCV has an ICP class in its surface matching module, paired with a global matcher. The trimesh library in Python offers a simple ICP function, and the Eigen library in C++ provides the Umeyama method for the best-move step. You can read more about where Open3D fits into a robot arm's software on the tools and libraries page.

Section seven asks why use ICP, and what it costs. ICP moves a model point set onto a scanned point set by pairing closest points and fitting a rigid move, over and over. So it turns a rough pose into an exact one, often to within a millimetre or a degree, using only the shape of the part. The obvious alternative is to try every pose, which is millions of poses and far too slow. The second alternative is to match distinctive features and fit the pose with RANSAC. Feature matching does not need a first guess, but it is less exact. That is why the usual answer is to use both: features for the first guess, then ICP to make it exact.

ICP costs you several things. Because it gives a confident wrong answer when the first guess is poor, you must check the final gap. It needs a model of the part, and a clean scan with the table cut away. It needs downsampled clouds to run at camera rate. Finally, it cannot tell apart the poses that a symmetric part makes look the same.

The eighth section covers the learned alternative. The page on keypoints and object pose describes networks that give an object's pose directly from a picture. Models such as MegaPose and FoundationPose cope with clutter and need no first guess, so they can take the place of 3D features and RANSAC. There are also networks that read point clouds, from the page on point cloud models, which can learn their own 3D features. But a pose model needs a graphics processor, and it gives no warning when it is wrong. So ICP is still run after it, and ICP's final gap is the check. ICP alone still wins when a first guess is already at hand.
