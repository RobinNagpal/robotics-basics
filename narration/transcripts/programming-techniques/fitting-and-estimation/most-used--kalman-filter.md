The Kalman filter. This page explains the Kalman filter, which is a way to keep a running estimate of a quantity that is measured again and again with noise, such as the distance to a table or the position of a part on a moving belt. It answers four questions about doing that. What do the two steps, predict and update, do? How much should each new reading be trusted? Where does a robot arm use the filter? And what makes it go wrong? 

The page is for a reader who has read about least-squares fitting, or who knows what an average and a spread of readings are. It starts with a single number and then moves to a position on a table. Every number comes from a real run of a diagram script. 

The filter is named after Rudolf Kálmán, who published it in nineteen sixty, and it is used in nearly every machine that moves and senses, like phones, drones, cars, and robot arms. On an arm, it appears most often in tracking objects the camera sees, and in smoothing sensor readings.

The first section explains what this page answers. Many readings on a robot arm come in a stream, because a camera gives a picture thirty times a second and a force sensor gives a reading a thousand times a second. Each reading is a little wrong, and sometimes a reading is missing, for example when the arm's own body hides an object from the camera. 

The arm wants one steady value at each moment, not a shaking one. It also wants to know how far that value might be off. And when the quantity is changing, such as a part moving on a belt, it wants a value for the part's position now. It also wants a guess for its position a moment from now. 

The Kalman filter gives all three, because it keeps an estimate and a measure of how sure it is, and improves both each time a reading arrives. It can also run without a reading, which lets it carry on through a short gap.

The idea in one sentence is this: before each new reading, predict where the quantity should be from how it was moving; then move the prediction towards the reading by an amount that depends on which of the two you trust more. 

That one sentence describes the whole loop, and an everyday example shows how it feels. You are walking to a friend's house in the dark, and you know you walk about one metre per step. After ten steps you think you are about ten metres along, but you are not quite sure, because each step could have been a little long or short. Then you see a lamp post you know is at eleven metres, but it is dark and you cannot judge exactly how far away it is. You do not throw away your step count, and you do not ignore the lamp post, but settle on something in between, closer to whichever you trust more. Then you carry on counting steps from that new position. 

Counting steps is the predict step, while looking at the lamp post is the update step. The Kalman filter does exactly this, with numbers for how sure it is.

The next part explains how it works, step by step, starting with what the filter keeps. Since the filter has to carry its belief from one reading to the next, it keeps two things at all times. First, the estimate, which is its best value for the quantity. For a part on a belt this could be its position in millimetres. Second, the variance, which is how unsure it is about the estimate. The variance is the square of the spread, also called the standard deviation. A spread of five millimetres means the true value is usually within about five millimetres of the estimate, and gives a variance of twenty-five square millimetres. 

Variances are used inside the filter because they add up simply. When two independent uncertainties combine, their variances add, while their spreads do not. 

The filter also needs two numbers from you, both as variances. First, the sensor noise, written as the letter r, which is how much a single reading scatters. A camera whose positions scatter by four millimetres has an r of sixteen. Second, the process noise, written as the letter q, which is how much the quantity can change between readings in ways the prediction does not know about. A belt that runs at a nearly steady speed has a small q.

Once those four numbers are in place, the predict step moves the filter's estimate forward to the time of the next reading. It does this using what it knows about how the quantity moves. For example, if a belt carries the part five millimetres between pictures, the predicted position is the last estimate plus five millimetres. 

The prediction is less certain than the last estimate, because the belt might have slipped or sped up a little. So the variance grows by the process noise. The predicted estimate equals the last estimate plus the known change, and the predicted variance equals the last variance plus q.

After that prediction, a reading arrives in the update step, and the filter compares it with the prediction. The difference, which is the reading minus the prediction, is called the innovation, because it is the part of the reading the prediction did not expect. 

The filter then moves the prediction part of the way towards the reading. The share it moves is called the Kalman gain, written as a capital K, and it is a number between zero and one. 

The gain K equals the predicted variance divided by the sum of the predicted variance and the sensor noise r. The new estimate then equals the predicted estimate plus K times the innovation. Finally, the new variance equals one minus K, all multiplied by the predicted variance.

The gain reads like this: if the prediction is very unsure compared with the sensor, K is close to one and the new estimate is almost the reading. However, if the sensor is very noisy compared with the prediction, K is close to zero and the reading barely moves the estimate. The new variance is always smaller than both the predicted variance and the sensor noise, because combining two pieces of evidence gives a result more certain than either one alone.

Those two steps are easiest to follow on real numbers, so imagine a block riding on a conveyor belt at fifty millimetres per second. A camera takes ten pictures a second, so the block moves five millimetres between pictures. The camera's position readings scatter by four millimetres, so r is sixteen. The belt is steady but not perfect, so q is one. The filter starts with an estimate of one hundred millimetres and a spread of five millimetres, which is a variance of twenty-five.

Starting from those numbers, the first step goes like this. First, predict. The estimate becomes one hundred plus five, which is one hundred and five millimetres. The variance becomes twenty-five plus one, which is twenty-six, giving a spread of five point one millimetres. Second, the camera reads one hundred and seven millimetres. The innovation is one hundred and seven minus one hundred and five, which is two millimetres. Third, the gain is twenty-six divided by the sum of twenty-six and sixteen, which is zero point six one nine. Fourth, the new estimate is one hundred and five plus zero point six one nine times two, which is one hundred and six point two four millimetres. Finally, the new variance is one minus zero point six one nine, times twenty-six, which is nine point nine zero, or a spread of three point one five millimetres.

The page shows a diagram of this step as bell-shaped curves, where each curve's peak is at the value and its width is the spread. The prediction is the last estimate moved five millimetres to the right and made a little wider. The measurement is narrower than the prediction, because the camera's four millimetre spread is smaller than the prediction's five point one millimetres. That is why the updated estimate lands closer to the measurement, and it is narrower than either.

A table then lists all four steps of the same run, where each row is one picture. In the first step, the gain K is zero point six one nine, and the updated spread is three point one five millimetres. By the fourth step, the gain falls to zero point two seven six, and the updated spread falls to two point one zero millimetres. Notice that the gain falls because as the filter becomes surer, each new reading moves it less. So after four readings the spread is about half the spread of a single camera reading.

The page then describes the steps as pseudocode for the one-number filter used above, where the known change per step can be zero for a quantity that should stay still. The code loops over each reading. In the predict phase, it adds the known change to the estimate, and adds the process noise q to the variance. Then, if a reading arrived, it calculates the gain, updates the estimate by adding the gain times the difference between the reading and the estimate, and shrinks the variance.

For several numbers at once, such as a position and a speed, the same steps use matrices. The estimate becomes a list of numbers called the state, and the variance becomes a table called the covariance, which also says how the errors of the numbers are linked. The predict and update steps follow the exact same pattern, but using matrix multiplication, transposes, and inverses. A table called F moves the state forward one step. A table called H picks out the part of the state that the sensor measures. Q and R are the process and sensor noise as tables. Each line of the matrix math matches a line of the one-number version.

Both versions of the filter need the same two settings. The sensor noise r is the easy one, because you can measure it by holding the sensor still and recording the spread of its readings. However, the process noise q is harder, since it says how much the quantity can change in ways the prediction does not model. It therefore sets how quickly the filter follows real changes.

A diagram shows the difference. In it, a depth camera on the wrist reads the distance to the table, four hundred millimetres, with a four millimetre spread, ten times a second. After three point five seconds the arm lowers the camera by twenty millimetres. Then two filters run on the same readings, one with q set to zero point zero one, and one with q set to four. 

While the distance is steady, the filter with the small q is the smoother of the two. From the eleventh to the thirty-fifth reading its average error is zero point seven seven millimetres, against one point six seven millimetres for the other filter and two point seven six millimetres for the raw readings. But when the camera drops, the small-q filter believes the distance cannot change, so it drifts down very slowly. At the end of the run, two point four seconds later, it still reads eight point seven millimetres too far. However, the filter with q set to four is within five millimetres of the new distance by the second reading after the drop.

Another diagram shows why, by plotting the gain and the spread over time for the steady part. With q at zero point zero one, the gain keeps falling, from zero point five at the first step down to zero point zero two seven at the sixtieth. As a result, the filter trusts its own estimate more and more, and each reading counts less. This is what makes it smooth, and also what makes it slow to follow a real change. With q at four, however, the gain settles at zero point three nine within a few steps, and the spread settles at two point five millimetres. So that filter always gives each reading a fair share. 

There is no single right q, so choose it from how fast the real quantity can change. If you know the arm is about to move the camera, you can also tell the filter so, because the move is a known change, just like the belt's five millimetres per step.

So far the filter has tracked one number at a time, but a part on a belt has a position and a speed. The camera measures only the position, but the filter can estimate the speed too, by putting both in its state. This is called a constant-velocity model. The predict step assumes the part keeps its speed, and adds the speed times the time step to the position. The update step corrects the position from the reading, and the covariance links position and speed, so the speed is corrected too.

A diagram tracks a part moving across a belt at about sixty millimetres per second, with the camera's positions scattering by four millimetres. For zero point eight seconds the arm reaches in and hides the part, so there are eight pictures with no reading. Before the gap, the filter has learned a speed of fifty-eight point seven millimetres per second along the belt and sixteen point eight millimetres per second across it. During the gap it keeps predicting with that speed, and the diagram shows uncertainty ellipses where the filter thinks the part could be. They grow during the gap, because there are no readings to shrink them. The spread along the belt rises from two point zero millimetres to five point zero millimetres. At the end of the gap the estimate is three point six millimetres from the true position, close enough to match the next detection to the same part. Then, once readings return, the ellipses shrink again.

The same growing ellipse tells a tracker how far from the prediction a new detection may be and still count as the same part. This idea is called gating, and is explained more fully on the page about tracking and association.

The Kalman filter as described here assumes the motion and the measurement are straight-line relationships, like position plus speed times time. However, many real problems are not, because an object turning on a turntable, or a camera measuring an angle and a distance, bends the relationship. 

The next section explains what goes wrong when things are not straight lines, and the three filters people use instead: the extended Kalman filter, the unscented Kalman filter, and the particle filter. It uses the same predict and update words, and the numbers come from a real run of a second diagram script.

The Kalman filter describes what it believes as one bell curve, with an estimate and a spread. That works because if you push a bell curve through a straight-line relationship, you get another bell curve. As a result, the filter only has to work out its new centre and width. 

However, push a bell curve through a bent relationship and the result is no longer a bell curve. Angles are the common cause of this on an arm. For example, a sensor may measure a distance and an angle, or a camera may see a turning part side-on. Then the relationship between what it reads and where the thing is becomes bent.

A diagram shows this, using a sensor that says an object is four hundred millimetres away, give or take five millimetres, straight ahead, give or take twenty-five degrees. The diagram plots five thousand possible positions drawn from that belief, and they form a curved band, not an oval. The real average of the dots is three hundred and sixty-four millimetres forward, not four hundred millimetres, because the ends of the band curve back towards the sensor. Their forward spread is forty-nine millimetres, not five millimetres, so a filter that simply converts the best guess gets both of those wrong.

The first way round that problem is the extended Kalman filter, or EKF, which replaces the bent relationship with a straight one that touches it at the current estimate. It is like laying a ruler against a curve at one point. The slope of that ruler is called the Jacobian, which is a table of how much each output changes when each input changes a little. The filter then runs the usual predict and update steps with the ruler in place of the curve. This works well when the bend is gentle over the width of the bell curve, but badly when the bell curve is wide. In the diagram, the EKF's flat ellipse is centred at four hundred millimetres and has a forward spread of only five millimetres. So it is sure of something that is wrong.

The unscented Kalman filter, or UKF, does not straighten anything at all. Instead, it picks a small set of sample points, called sigma points, around the estimate. It takes the estimate itself, plus two points one step out along each direction of the spread. So for two numbers that makes five points, and it pushes each point through the real, bent relationship. Then it works out a new centre and spread from where the points landed, each point with a fixed weight. In the diagram, the UKF's ellipse is centred at three hundred and sixty-four millimetres, the same as the real average, with a forward spread of fifty-two millimetres against the real forty-nine millimetres. It still draws one oval around a curved band, but it puts the oval in the right place and the right size. It needs no Jacobian, so there is no slope to work out by hand. Its cost is running the relationship two N plus one times per step, for N numbers in the state.

Both Kalman filters still hold one bell curve, but sometimes the belief is not one hump at all. A part seen side-on on a turntable, for example, could be at an angle a or at the mirror angle minus a, because both give the same sideways position. The belief has two humps, and one bell curve cannot hold two. 

A particle filter holds the belief as a crowd of guesses instead, and each guess is called a particle. Each step then has three parts. First, move. Push every particle through the motion model, adding a little random jitter for the process noise. Second, weigh. Give each particle a weight for how well it explains the new reading. A particle whose predicted reading is close to the real one gets a large weight. Third, resample. Draw a new crowd of the same size from the old one, where each particle is copied in proportion to its weight. Heavy particles are copied several times. Light ones die out.

A diagram shows one weigh and resample step with only twelve guesses of a part's angle, spaced fourteen degrees apart. The camera read a sideways position of one hundred and twenty-two millimetres. The guesses at minus thirty-five degrees and plus thirty-five degrees explain the reading best, with a high weight. After resampling they have three copies each, while the guesses further out had weights near zero and are gone. Notice that both the positive and the negative angles survive. The particle filter keeps both answers until later readings decide between them.

The pseudocode for one step loops over each particle, moves it with a small random jitter, and weighs it by how likely the reading is if the particle were true. It normalises the weights so they add up to one, then draws a new set of particles based on those weights, and outputs the weighted average of the particles before resampling as the estimate.

To compare the three filters on one problem, a part sits one hundred and fifty millimetres from the centre of a turntable that turns at a known zero point five radians per second. A camera looks along the table and sees only the part's sideways position, with a spread of five millimetres, ten times a second. The motion is a straight line, but the measurement is bent. The true angle starts at thirty-seven degrees. Three filters run on the same forty-five readings, and the EKF and the UKF start from a wrong guess of minus thirty-four degrees, with a spread of twenty-nine degrees. That is the mirror answer, because it explains the first reading just as well. The particle filter starts with a thousand particles spread evenly all round the table, meaning it has no idea where the part is.

A diagram shows the true angle rising, and how the filters respond. The EKF and the UKF both stay on the wrong side. Every reading fits the mirror angle almost as well as the true one, so each update pulls them back to it. After one second they are one hundred and twenty-one degrees off, and over the last three seconds they are one hundred and nineteen degrees off on average. Worse, at the end they report a spread of only one point seven degrees. They are sure, and wrong. Started at the right angle instead, the same EKF tracks the part with an average error of one point two degrees. So the EKF is fine once it is near the answer; it just cannot choose between two answers.

The particle filter finds the right side within three readings. Its first estimate is thirty-nine degrees off, because it averages two humps and lands between them. By the third reading it is one point three degrees off, and over the last three seconds its average error is one point two degrees, the same as the well-started EKF. 

Another diagram shows why, by drawing the particles on the turntable. After the first reading, the weight is split almost evenly between two clusters, on the mirror side and the true side. The table then turns, and on the true side the part's sideways position shrinks as the angle grows, while on the mirror side it would grow. Since the readings shrink, the mirror particles lose their weight, and by the fourth reading all the weight is on the true side.

Once you know how each filter behaves, it is easier to see where the three appear on a robot arm. The EKF is used for tracking an object with a camera that measures pixels, because the pinhole camera model divides by depth, which is a bend. It is also used for joining wheel counts, an inertial measurement unit, and a camera on a mobile base with an arm, where headings are angles. The UKF is used for the same jobs when the bend is sharper, or when working out the Jacobian by hand is error-prone. The particle filter is used for finding where a mobile base is on a known map, where the belief often has several humps. It is also used for finding how a part sits in the hand from a few touch contacts, or deciding which of two symmetric ways round a part is.

Each has its own way to fail. If an EKF has a wide spread across a sharp bend, you will see a small spread but large, one-sided innovations, and the estimate drifts. To fix this, use a UKF, smaller time steps, or better starting guesses. If an EKF or UKF is given a problem where two answers fit the readings, it will confidently estimate the wrong answer. To fix this, use a particle filter at the start, then hand over to a Kalman filter. If a particle filter has too many numbers in its state, it needs far more particles, and most will have near-zero weight. Keep the state small, up to about six numbers, and use a Kalman filter for the rest. If a particle filter has a very precise sensor, all particles collapse onto a few copies, and the crowd stops exploring. Add more jitter in the move step, or more particles. Finally, because a particle filter uses random draws, it gives slightly different answers on every run. Use more particles, or a fixed random seed for testing.

The Python library filterpy has classes for the EKF and UKF, and resampling functions. The ROS 2 package robot localization provides EKF and UKF nodes. The ROS 2 navigation stack includes a particle filter that finds a mobile base on a map. GTSAM handles bent relationships by re-straightening them over a window of past steps rather than only at the latest one. 

The particle filter's cost is speed, because the run above updated a thousand particles every step, where the EKF updated one number and one spread. For a small state that is still fast, but for a large one a Kalman filter that is started near the right answer is the practical choice.

The next section covers where the filter is used on a robot arm. Whichever version of the filter is used, it appears in perception, sensing, and the arm's own state. 

First, tracking objects the camera sees. A tracker runs one filter per object. Each picture, it predicts every object forward, matches detections to the predictions, and updates each filter with its match. 

Second, picking from a conveyor. The filter's speed estimate lets the arm aim for where the part will be when the gripper arrives, not where it was when the picture was taken. 

Third, coasting through a missed detection. When the arm hides a part, or the detector misses it for a frame, the filter keeps predicting. The growing spread tells the program when to give up on the track. 

Fourth, smoothing a depth or force reading. A wrist camera's distance to the table, or a force sensor's reading during a gentle push, is filtered before a controller acts on it. A PID controller that acts on raw readings passes the noise straight to the motors. 

Fifth, estimating a slowly changing constant. A robot that pushes objects can estimate the table's friction from push after push. Another page on contact parameters shows that for a constant, the Kalman filter becomes recursive least squares, which is the least-squares fit updated one reading at a time. 

Sixth, joining sensors. A mobile base with an arm on it combines wheel counts, an inertial measurement unit, and sometimes a camera into one position. 

Finally, estimating joint speed. An arm's encoders measure joint angles. A small filter per joint estimates the speed more smoothly than subtracting two angles and dividing by the time.

In all of those places, the filter works best when you can say how the quantity moves from one moment to the next. It also needs noise that is roughly bell-shaped and does not depend on the last reading, and readings that arrive regularly with known times. 

However, there are ways it goes wrong. If the process noise q is too small, the estimate is smooth but late, and after a real change it trails for seconds. You should raise q, or tell the filter about known changes such as arm moves. If q is too large, the estimate is nearly as noisy as the raw readings. You should lower q, and check with a recording of the sensor held still. If the object stops following the motion model, for example if a person picks it up, the estimate carries on past where the object is, and innovations are suddenly large. You should detect large innovations and restart the track, or use several motion models side by side. If a wrong detection is fed in, you will see a sudden jump that then decays slowly. You should use a gate that refuses readings too far from the prediction. If timing is irregular, the speed estimate jumps. You should use each reading's real timestamp for the time step, not a nominal rate. For strongly bent relationships, the filter becomes over-confident and then drifts. You should use an EKF, UKF, or a particle filter. If readings have rare large errors that are not bell-shaped, one bad reading drags the estimate. You should use a gate, or a robust filter. Finally, if the motion is too complex to write down, like a cloth or a bouncing object, you will see large innovations all the time. You should use a learned motion model instead.

Since the one-number filter is ten lines of code, many projects write it themselves. For more than that, several libraries are well known. OpenCV provides a linear filter with the standard predict and correct steps in C++ and Python. The filterpy library in Python provides readable code for the standard filter, EKF, and UKF, written alongside a free textbook on the subject. The robot localization package in ROS 2 joins wheel, IMU, GPS, and pose readings into one position using C++. GTSAM is a smoother in C++ and Python that corrects past estimates as well as the current one, and is used for camera and robot position. And Eigen provides the matrix types in C++ to write your own filter, as most C++ robotics code does.

The next section explains why to use a Kalman filter, and what it costs. The Kalman filter is a loop that predicts a quantity forward and then corrects it with each new reading, weighting the two by how much each is trusted. It gives the arm a steady value, a measure of how sure that value is, and a prediction for the next moment, even through short gaps.

The obvious alternative is a moving average, which is simpler and needs no model. On the steady part of the depth example, a ten-reading average has an average error close to the Kalman filters. However, it has three drawbacks. First, it always lags. After the twenty millimetre drop it took seven readings to get within five millimetres, against two for the filter with q set to four. Second, it cannot predict ahead, so it cannot help an arm meet a moving part. And third, it gives no spread, so a tracker cannot tell how far to look for the next detection.

A second alternative is to fit a line through the last few positions with least squares and extend it forward. That gives a speed and a prediction, but it forgets everything older than its window, and it does not say how sure it is. The Kalman filter keeps all past readings in two small numbers, the estimate and the covariance, and costs the same on every step however long it runs.

The costs are these. You need a model of how the quantity moves, and the filter is only as good as that model. You must choose the process noise, and a poor choice gives either a laggy or a noisy result without any error message. The filter's confidence is highest just before an object does something new, such as change direction. And its state lives on from step to step, so a wrong reading or a bug can affect many steps after it.

Those costs raise the question of a learned alternative, but learned trackers mostly keep the filter rather than replace it. Methods such as SORT and ByteTrack take boxes from a trained detector and use a Kalman filter to predict where each object should be, and some also compare how the objects look, so that two similar objects are not swapped. When the motion is too complex to write down, a learned dynamics model predicts the next state from recordings of the real arm instead of a formula, but it needs those recordings, and its errors add up over many steps. For touch, force and slip models recognise patterns such as the fast shaking of a slip, which a filter that smooths the reading cannot tell apart from noise. For a steady estimate of a position or a speed, the Kalman filter is still the better choice, because it needs no training data, costs a few lines of arithmetic per reading, and says how sure it is.
