Numerical inverse kinematics. This page explains how a program finds the joint angles that put a gripper at a chosen place, when there is no neat formula to do it. It does that with a guess-and-correct loop, so the page answers five questions about it. How does the guess-and-correct loop work? What is the Jacobian, and why does the loop need it? Why does the plain loop go wild when the arm is nearly straight? How does damped least squares, the method most solvers use, fix that? And what happens when the target cannot be reached at all?

The page is for a reader who has read the earlier material on inverse kinematics, which solves the same small arm with a triangle formula and introduces the guess-and-correct loop. This page picks up where that one stops. It uses the same arm, which has two links lying flat. Link 1 is 3 metres and link 2 is 2 metres. The target is at an x of 2.598 and a y of 3.5, which is where the gripper sits when joint 1 is at 30 degrees and joint 2 is at 60 degrees. 

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. Guess the joint angles, see how far the gripper misses the target, work out which small turn of each joint shrinks that miss, make the turn, and repeat until the miss is tiny. 

Think of parking a car close to a kerb with the help of a mirror. You do not work out the steering angle in advance. Instead you look in the mirror, see that you are 40 centimetres out, turn the wheel a little, roll back, and look again. Each look tells you the miss, and you know from experience which way to turn the wheel to shrink it. A program does the same thing. Forward kinematics is its mirror, because it says where the gripper is for any set of joint angles. The Jacobian is its experience, because it says which way the gripper moves when each joint turns. Inverse kinematics is often shortened to IK, and the page uses that short form from here on.

The next part of the page explains how it works, taking the loop apart step by step. 

Step 1 is to measure the miss. The program starts from a guess for the joint angles, which on a real robot is usually the arm's current angles. Here the guess is joint 1 at 0 degrees and joint 2 at 30 degrees. It runs forward kinematics on the guess to find where the gripper is, and subtracts that position from the target. The result is the miss, which is an arrow from the gripper to the target with an x part and a y part. Its length is how far off the gripper is, and for this first guess, that length is 3.287 metres.

Step 2 introduces the Jacobian. Since step 1 measured the miss, the next question is which joint turn will shrink it, and the Jacobian answers that. It is a small table with one column per joint. Each column says how far the gripper moves in x and in y when that joint turns by one radian. It only holds for small turns, and it changes as the arm moves, so the program works it out again at every step. For this arm at 30 degrees and 60 degrees, the first column is minus 3.500 in x and 2.598 in y. The second column is minus 2.000 in x and 0.000 in y. This means turning joint 1 by a small amount moves the gripper at right angles to the line from the base, because joint 1 swings the whole arm. Joint 2 only swings the last link, so the gripper moves at right angles to that link. 

A diagram shows the two columns of the Jacobian drawn as arrows at the gripper. On the left, with the elbow bent at 60 degrees, the two arrows point in clearly different directions, so together they can move the gripper any way. But on the right, with the elbow nearly straight at 5 degrees, both arrows point almost the same way, meaning neither moves the gripper along the arm.

Step 3 is turning the Jacobian round. The Jacobian tells you where the gripper goes if you turn the joints. However, IK needs the opposite: how much to turn the joints to move the gripper along the miss. So the program solves the equation where the Jacobian multiplied by the joint step equals the miss. With two joints and two numbers in the miss, this is two equations with two unknowns. It has one answer as long as the columns point different ways, and it is solved using the pseudo-inverse. One number tells you how easy the table is to turn round, called its determinant. For this arm it is 6 times the sine of joint 2. At 30 and 60 degrees it is 5.196, but it falls to zero when the elbow is straight or folded back. That pose is a singularity, where the arm cannot move its gripper in some direction, however fast the joints turn.

Step 4 explains why the plain answer goes wild near a straight arm. The trouble starts well before the arm reaches a singularity. As the elbow straightens, both columns of the Jacobian point almost the same way. Neither moves the gripper along the arm towards or away from the base. Asked to move that way, the plain answer asks for a huge turn. 

A diagram and a table show the joint step each method asks for to pull the gripper in by 10 centimetres as the arm straightens. The plain inverse step grows rapidly. At an elbow angle of 30 degrees, it asks for a 9.6 degree step. But at an elbow angle of 0.1 degrees, it asks for a massive 2946.4 degree step, which is eight full turns of the joints in one step. In contrast, damped methods shrink the step size as the arm straightens, keeping it under a fraction of a degree. A simple cap on the step size does work, but it is a blunt tool because it cuts the step to the same size whether the arm is near a singularity or far from it.

Step 5 introduces damped least squares. Because a cap is so blunt, damped least squares asks a slightly different question. It asks which step makes the squared miss left over, plus a penalty for the size of the step, as small as possible. The penalty is set by a number called lambda, which is the damping. When the arm is well bent, a small turn removes a lot of miss, so the penalty hardly matters and the step is close to the plain answer. When the arm is nearly straight, removing the miss along the arm would need a huge turn. The penalty wins, and the step stays small. The method gives up on the direction the arm cannot move in, and moves in the directions it can. The formula for this step multiplies the transposed Jacobian by the inverse of a matrix. That matrix is the Jacobian times its transpose, plus lambda squared times the identity matrix. The result is then multiplied by the miss. When the program also changes lambda as it goes, it is called the Levenberg-Marquardt method.

Step 6 shows a worked run of damped least squares with lambda set to 1.0 and no step cap. A diagram traces the arm at every step from the first guess to the target. The first two steps do most of the work, and it finds the elbow-down answer in 17 steps. For comparison, the plain pseudo-inverse from the same guess folds the elbow almost flat on its first step, making the miss bigger, but it recovers and finishes sooner, in 7 steps. Near the answer, the plain method is the fastest there is. Damped least squares gives up some of that speed to keep every step safe. Another diagram compares the miss at every step. On a reachable target, both methods reach a miss below a micrometre. But on a target that is out of reach, the damped method settles at the best pose, while the pseudo-inverse jumps about wildly.

Step 7 explores a target out of reach and choosing the damping. If you ask for a target 6 metres away when the arm is only 5 metres long, no answer exists. The best the arm can do is point straight at it with a miss of 1 metre. Damped least squares with a lambda of 1.0 gets there and stays there. The plain pseudo-inverse never settles, winding its joints up to hundreds of degrees. A table shows the trade-off for different damping values. Small damping, like 0.05, is fast on easy targets but jumps about on hard ones. Large damping, like 2.0, is safe but slow. A damping of 0.5 is interesting because it swaps every step between two mirror poses with the same miss. A program might see a steady miss and not notice the arm is being told to swing back and forth. The usual answer is Levenberg-Marquardt, which halves the damping after a good step and doubles it after a bad one. This reaches the target quickly and settles safely when out of reach.

Step 8 covers having more joints than the task needs. If you add a third link but only ask for the gripper's position, the arm is redundant and has endless answers. Damped least squares will still reach the point, but from different guesses it will choose very different poses and gripper angles. It just picks an answer near the guess with small joint turns. If the gripper angle matters, you must add it to the miss, and a real six-joint arm works exactly this way, using a miss with six numbers for position and rotation.

The section ends with pseudocode for the full loop. It starts with a guess, measures the miss, and repeats until the miss is tiny or it runs out of time. Inside the loop, it finds the Jacobian, calculates the damped step, and clamps the new joint angles to their limits. If the new miss is smaller, it keeps the step and halves the damping. If it is larger, it throws the step away and doubles the damping. It finally returns the joint angles, or reports failure along with the final miss, because "not found" is not the same as "no answer exists".

The third section lists where this loop is used on a real robot arm. First, it turns a grasp into joint angles by moving a gripper pose from the camera frame to the arm's frame. Second, it checks many candidate grasps before moving, throwing away unreachable ones. Third, it calculates straight-line moves by splitting the line into small steps and calling IK at each one. Fourth, it handles jogging and following, running one step of the loop every control cycle to turn a wanted velocity into joint speeds. Fifth, it points a wrist camera at a target, which leaves spare joints for the loop to pick a pose near the current one. Sixth, it finishes a learned guess by taking a close answer from a neural network and making it exact. Finally, the same Jacobian math is used inside trajectory optimisation.

The fourth section lists where the loop works and where it does not. If the arm is near a singularity with no damping, a joint might jump hundreds of degrees, which is why people use damped least squares. If the target is out of reach, the loop uses all its steps, so you should check reach first and report the final miss. If damping is too low, the miss jumps up and down; if it is too high, the solver times out. If a joint limit is in the way or the guess is on the wrong side, the standard fix is to restart from other random guesses. Finally, a time limit might be too short. MoveIt's default solver stops after 0.05 seconds, so it might fail simply because it ran out of time.

The fifth section lists libraries that provide these solvers. For C++ and Python, the Orocos Kinematics and Dynamics Library, or KDL, provides several solvers including Levenberg-Marquardt, and is the default in MoveIt 2. TRAC-IK and pick_ik are modern alternatives for MoveIt. Libraries like Pinocchio and Drake give you the pieces to write the loop yourself. In Python, the Robotics Toolbox, ikpy, and cuRobo offer various solvers, and you can write it from scratch using NumPy or Eigen. For a ROS 2 arm, start with the default MoveIt solver, switch to TRAC-IK or pick_ik if it fails, and only write your own loop if you need it inside a fast control cycle or for a custom task.

The sixth section explains why you would choose numerical inverse kinematics and what it costs. It gives you joint angles for any arm that has forward kinematics, whatever its shape. The alternative is an analytic solver, which is a neat formula. Formulas are hundreds of times faster, list every answer, and know for certain if none exists. But many arms have no formula, like seven-joint arms or arms with offset wrists. The numerical loop works for all of them. The costs are that it only finds one answer nearest the guess, it is slower, its time is not fixed, and it cannot tell if a target is truly unreachable. You also have to tune settings like damping and time limits.

The seventh section looks at a third option: a learned inverse kinematics solver. This is a neural network trained on many pairs of joint angles and gripper poses. It answers in one pass and can give many different answers at once. However, its answer is only close, not exact. Because of this, it is usually used as the starting guess for the numerical loop, which finishes the job in a step or two. For one target at a time, the numerical loop alone remains the usual choice because it is exact, needs no training, and works immediately on new arms.

The final section shows how to use this in Python, specifically using the Pinocchio library. The provided script builds the robot model from a URDF file and sets a target pose and a starting guess. It then enters a loop for a set number of steps. Inside the loop, it runs forward kinematics and calculates the miss as a six-number twist, which includes three distances and three rotations. If the miss is tiny, the loop breaks. Otherwise, it asks Pinocchio to compute the Jacobian for the current pose. It then calculates the damped least squares step using a matrix solver, and integrates that step into the current joint angles, making sure to respect joint types like revolute joints that wrap around.

On a sample six-joint arm, this loop converges in just 7 steps to an error far below any real sensor's accuracy. Pinocchio handles the hard math of building the Jacobian and calculating the six-number twist. You write the loop itself, which lets you change how the miss is measured, set the stopping test, and clamp the joints to their limits. You also have to choose the damping and the stopping tolerance. A damping value like zero point zero one is a compromise between stability and speed, and you might adjust it dynamically. The tolerance should be set based on the real accuracy you need. For a ROS 2 arm, you should first try the solver MoveIt already uses, and write this loop only when you need it inside a control cycle or when the miss is not a standard pose.
