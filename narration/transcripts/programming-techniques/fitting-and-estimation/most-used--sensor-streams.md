Sensor streams: time stamps, filters and thresholds.

This page explains how to work with a stream of sensor readings. This means readings that arrive one after another, many times a second, for as long as the arm runs. It answers the five questions that come up most often once you write a program against such a stream. How do you pair a camera picture with the joint angles of the same moment? What goes wrong when two devices keep their own clocks? How do you smooth a noisy reading, and what does smoothing cost? How do you get a rate of change, such as how fast a force is rising, out of noisy readings? And how do you turn a reading into a yes-or-no flag, such as "the force is too high", without the flag flickering on and off?

It assumes you know what a joint angle, a wrist camera, and a force sensor are. It also helps to have read the page on the Kalman filter, because that page handles one stream in a more careful way than anything here does. Every number on this page comes from a real run of a diagram script, rather than from an estimate.

Two running examples are used throughout, and both come from an ordinary arm. The first is a wrist camera, which is a camera fixed to the arm near the gripper, so that it moves whenever the arm moves. The second is a force-torque trace, which is the stream of readings from a force-torque sensor at the wrist. That means a stream of numbers saying how hard the tool is pushing on something.

The first section covers what this page answers. A robot arm has many sensors, and each one sends its own stream at its own rate. The joint encoders report the joint angles perhaps 100 or 1,000 times a second, and an encoder is the sensor inside a joint that measures its angle. A wrist camera is much slower, because it sends only 30 pictures a second, while a force-torque sensor is faster again at 500 or 1,000 readings a second.

Because those rates differ, and because no reading is perfect, these streams cause four everyday problems. First, the streams do not line up, because a picture is taken between two joint readings rather than at the same instant as one. Second, every reading is late, so a picture arrives at the computer some tens of milliseconds after it was taken. Third, every reading is noisy, since a force sensor's reading shakes a little even when nothing touches it, and now and then it jumps for no real reason. Finally, decisions made from noisy readings flicker, so a check such as "is the force above 10 newtons?" can switch between yes and no hundreds of times while the real force sits near 10.

The techniques on this page are small, and none of them needs a model of the arm, but almost every arm program uses all of them. Getting them wrong causes some of the hardest bugs to find, because nothing crashes. Instead, the arm just places things a few millimetres off, or stops for no visible reason.

The next part gives the core idea in one sentence. Since the four problems just mentioned all come from time, the answer is stated in terms of time as well. Treat each reading as a value together with the moment it was true, combine readings by their moments rather than by when they arrived, and smooth and threshold them in ways whose delay you have measured.

Here is an everyday example of the same rule, taken from outside robotics. You take a photo from a moving train, while a friend on the train writes down where the train is every second. Later you want to know where each photo was taken, so you must match each photo to one of the positions your friend wrote down. You do not use the position written down at the moment you looked at the photo, because that moment has nothing to do with the photo. Instead, you use the position at the time printed on the photo. So if the photo says ten fifteen and three and a half seconds, you take the positions at ten fifteen and three seconds and ten fifteen and four seconds, and pick the point halfway between them. And if your camera's clock is a minute fast, you must correct for that first, or every photo is placed a minute's travel down the line.

The page then explains how it works, step by step.

The rule starts from the moment a reading was true, so the first thing every stream needs is a way of writing that moment down. A time stamp is the moment a reading was true, recorded together with the reading itself. In the Robot Operating System, or ROS, most messages carry a header. This header holds the time stamp and the name of the frame the numbers are measured in.

The rule about stamps is short, and it decides everything that follows. The time stamp should say when the reading was true, not when it arrived. For a picture, that is the moment the camera's sensor was exposed to light, and for a force reading, it is the moment the sensor sampled. So a program that uses the time its callback ran, instead of the stamp, is using the arrival time. Here the callback is the function that runs when a message arrives.

Once every reading carries a stamp, two streams can be paired by those stamps. The page has a diagram showing the two streams of the running example. The top part shows joint readings arriving every 10 milliseconds and camera pictures every 33 milliseconds on one time line. The bottom part shows the tool's position. The arm moves the tool 100 millimetres in 0.6 seconds along a smooth path. As a result, at the moment of the marked picture, the tool is moving at 261 millimetres per second.

The picture has the time stamp 0.2833 seconds, which falls between the joint readings at 0.28 seconds and 0.29 seconds. So there are three ways to get the arm's pose for it.

First, you could read the pose "now", when the picture arrives. The picture arrives 45 milliseconds after it was taken, so by then the tool is at 57.4 millimetres although it was really at 45.6 millimetres. That means the pose is 11.7 millimetres wrong, and this is the most common mistake of the three.

Second, you could take the nearest joint reading. The nearest one is at 0.28 seconds, which is 3.3 milliseconds before the picture, and it gives 44.8 millimetres, so the error is 0.9 millimetres. At 100 readings a second, the nearest reading is never more than 5 milliseconds away, so at this speed the error is never more than about 1.3 millimetres.

Third, you could interpolate at the picture's stamp. Interpolate means working out a value between two known ones, here by drawing a straight line between the readings at 0.28 seconds and 0.29 seconds. It gives 45.6 millimetres, which is the same as the true position to one decimal place.

The second method is called approximate-time matching, because it pairs each message with the message from the other stream whose stamp is closest. ROS provides it as Approximate Time Synchronizer in the message filters package. You give it a slop, which is the largest difference between two stamps that still counts as a pair. That is why the method suits two streams at similar rates, such as a colour picture and a depth picture.

The third method suits a slow stream against a fast one instead, such as pictures against joint angles. You keep the last second or so of joint readings in a buffer. Then, when a picture arrives, you look up the two readings either side of its stamp and interpolate between them. This is exactly what the ROS transform library, tf2, does when you ask it to look up a transform for the pose at a given time. The rule is that the pose must belong to the frame, not to now.

Pairing by stamps only works when the stamps themselves are right, and two different delays hide in a stream, so each one needs its own fix.

Latency is the time between a reading being true and it arriving, and for the camera above it is 45 milliseconds. It comes from the exposure, the transfer over the cable, and the driver's work. Latency on its own is harmless if the stamp is right, because you look up the pose at the stamp. So it only matters when you act on the result, since an arm steering by a picture is always steering by the past.

A clock offset is a difference between two clocks. Such an offset appears because the camera may stamp its pictures with its own internal clock, while the joint readings are stamped with the computer's clock. If the camera's clock runs 12 milliseconds ahead, every picture looks 12 milliseconds later than it was. So in the example, pairing by the uncorrected stamp gives the pose at 0.2713 seconds instead of 0.2833 seconds. That is 42.5 millimetres instead of 45.6 millimetres, which is 3.1 millimetres wrong. Nothing warns you, because the pairs look perfectly matched.

Clocks also drift, which means that two clocks agreeing at start-up slowly move apart, because no two crystals tick at exactly the same rate. So an offset measured once is not enough for a long run.

Three fixes are used against these clock problems, and the most reliable comes first. First, put every device on one clock. Computers are kept in step with the Precision Time Protocol, which some cameras and arm controllers also support, or with the Network Time Protocol through a program such as chrony. Second, let the driver translate, because some camera drivers convert the camera's own time into the computer's time. The Intel RealSense library, for example, has a global time setting that does this. Finally, measure the offset yourself by moving the arm at a steady speed past a marker the camera can see. Then find the time shift that makes the marker's position in the pictures agree best with the arm's recorded position. That shift is the offset, together with any stamping delay in the driver.

Correct stamps put every reading at the right moment, but they do nothing about noise, so the next step is to smooth each stream. A filter here is a small calculation that takes the stream of raw readings and gives back a smoother stream. Filters that let slow changes through and damp fast ones are called low-pass filters.

The page shows a diagram of a noisy force trace with three spikes, and three filtered versions of it. The sensor sends 500 readings a second with 0.25 newtons of noise. The tool moves freely until 0.5 seconds and then touches a surface, so the force rises at 20 newtons per second to 6 newtons and stays there. Three single-reading spikes of plus 4 newtons stand for electrical glitches, and three filters run on the same raw readings.

First is a moving average, which reports the average of the last n readings. Here n is 25, which is 50 milliseconds of readings. On the quiet part before contact, it cuts the spread of the readings from 0.25 newtons to 0.04 newtons. A spike of 4 newtons becomes a bump of about 0.3 newtons, because the spike is shared among 25 readings.

Second is an exponential filter. It keeps one number, the estimate, and each new reading moves the estimate a fixed fraction of the way towards itself. The new estimate is the old estimate plus a fraction, called alpha, times the difference between the reading and the old estimate. Here alpha is 0.077, which is the usual match for a 25-reading average. That is why it behaves much like the moving average, with a spread of 0.055 newtons and a spike bump of about 0.4 newtons. It needs no buffer of past readings, which is why it is popular on small processors.

Third is a median filter, which reports the middle value of the last n readings once they are sorted. It has a spread of 0.04 newtons, and the spikes almost vanish, because the worst bump is 0.2 newtons, which is the noise itself. A single wild reading only moves the median by one place in the sorted list.

Every filter is late, however. The diagram shows a close-up of the rise, where every filter crosses a 3-newton threshold later than the true force. The true force crosses at 0.650 seconds, while the moving average and the exponential filter cross at 0.676 seconds, 26 milliseconds late. The median crosses at 0.680 seconds, which is 30 milliseconds late. A causal moving average of n readings is late by about half its window, here 12 readings or 24 milliseconds. The word causal means that it uses only readings that have already arrived, as any filter on a running robot must.

The raw readings are not late, but they cannot be used either. A 3-newton threshold on the raw readings would have fired at 0.20 seconds on the first spike, while the tool was still in free air. This is the trade that runs through every filter, since a longer window gives a smoother signal and a later one. Choose the window from the noise you must remove and the delay you can afford, and measure both.

Smoothing gives a steadier value, but often the useful number is how fast a reading changes rather than the reading itself. How fast the force rises tells you how stiff the surface is, and how fast a joint angle changes is the joint's speed. This rate of change is called the derivative, or the slope.

The obvious way is to subtract neighbouring readings and divide by the time between them, but a diagram shows why that fails. It uses the rising part of the force trace, where the true slope is 20 newtons per second. The diagram shows that slopes from neighbouring readings swing wildly between about minus 400 and plus 450 newtons per second. The readings are only 2 milliseconds apart, so noise of 0.25 newtons divided by 0.002 seconds is already 125 newtons per second. As a result, the neighbour differences have a spread of 164 newtons per second around a true value of 20 newtons per second. Their average is right at 20.2 newtons per second, but no single value is any use.

Two better ways use a window of 25 readings, which is 50 milliseconds long. The first is the difference across the window. This subtracts the reading 25 places back, so the noise is the same while the time between the two readings is 25 times longer. That brings its spread down to 7.1 newtons per second. The second is a least-squares line through all 25 readings. This uses every reading in the window rather than just the two ends. Its slope has a spread of 2.7 newtons per second, and its average is 19.8 newtons per second.

The diagram shows that slopes over a 25-reading window stay near 20 newtons per second, and the least-squares line is steadier than a difference across the window. Both are late by about half the window, 24 milliseconds, for the same reason as the filters. A Kalman filter that tracks a value and its speed together is the next step up. The Savitzky–Golay filter in the SciPy library fits a small curve in a sliding window and can return its slope, which is the same idea as the least-squares line.

Once a value is smooth and its slope is known, the last step is to turn it into a decision. A threshold does that by turning a reading into a flag, so that "the force is above 10 newtons" is either true or false. The trouble comes when the real value sits near the threshold, because noise then pushes each reading to one side or the other at random.

The page shows a diagram of a force hovering at a 10-newton limit. The force climbs to 9.8 newtons, creeps up to 10 newtons, rises to 11.5 newtons, then falls back to 6.5 newtons. In that run, the noise is 0.3 newtons and the limit is 10 newtons.

With one threshold, the flag is on whenever the reading is above 10 newtons, and it changes 142 times in two seconds. So a program that stops the arm when the flag goes on, and restarts it when the flag goes off, would jerk the arm about.

The first fix is hysteresis. The flag turns on above 10 newtons but only turns off below 9 newtons. Hysteresis means that the switch-on level and the switch-off level are different, so the flag remembers which side it came from. The gap between the two levels must be wider than the noise. Here it changes 4 times, since the gap of 1 newton is only about three times the noise, so one dip early on still gets through. A wider gap between the two levels would remove that dip.

The second fix is debouncing. The flag only changes after 10 readings in a row, or 20 milliseconds, agree with the new state. The word comes from mechanical switches, whose contacts bounce open and shut for a moment when pressed. Here the debounced flag changes twice, on at 1.044 seconds and off at 1.602 seconds. That is because it ignored the early touches of 10 newtons, when the real force was still 9.8 newtons, and waited until the real force was clearly above the limit.

The two tricks do different jobs, and they are often used together. Hysteresis stops the flag chattering once it has changed, while debouncing stops a brief excursion from changing it at all. But debouncing adds a delay, because here the flag waits 20 milliseconds before acting. For a safety stop that delay must be counted, and it should be short. Instead, for a slow decision such as "the part is seated", a longer wait is fine.

All five steps sit in one program, so the page provides pseudocode that puts them in the order they run. That order is to pair the streams, smooth them, take a slope, and then set the flag. First, it pairs a slow stream with a fast one. It keeps a ring buffer of the last one second of joint angles and their time stamps. When a picture arrives, it subtracts any camera clock offset from the picture's time stamp. Then it looks up the readings either side of that stamp in the pose buffer. If there are none, or the gap between them is too large, it drops the picture and counts the drop, never falling back to reading the pose "now". Otherwise, it interpolates the joint angles between the two readings based on the time stamp, and uses the image and the interpolated angles together. Second, it smooths the readings over a window of the last n readings, using a median, mean, or exponential filter. Third, it calculates the slope of the least-squares line through the time stamps and the window of readings. Finally, it sets a flag with hysteresis and debouncing. It counts up how many readings in a row are above the switch-on level, and counts down how many are below the switch-off level. Only when the count reaches a required number of consecutive readings does the flag actually turn on or off.

The next section describes where this is used on a robot arm. That short program is not a special case, because almost every program on an arm reads a stream, so these techniques turn up everywhere. First, placing what the wrist camera sees. A detection in a wrist-camera picture becomes a point in the room only through the arm's pose at the picture's time. Second, stitching several views. A depth camera on the wrist, swept over a table, gives many point clouds, and each must be placed with its own pose. A wrong pairing shows as a surface that is doubled or smeared. Third, guarded moves. An arm creeps towards a surface and stops when the force passes a threshold, so the filter decides how late the stop is. Fourth, weighing a held object. A long median over a still arm gives the weight, because a short filter would measure the arm's own shaking instead. Fifth, force limits. A "force too high" flag with hysteresis and a short debounce stops a press or an insertion without flickering. Sixth, joint speed from encoders. A controller that needs speed gets it as a slope from the angles, filtered. Seventh, recording demonstrations for learning. A learned policy is trained on pictures, joint angles and commands recorded together, so if the streams are paired wrongly, the policy learns that the arm reacts before it sees. Finally, gripper checks. A check that the fingers stopped closing is just a slope near zero on the finger position, debounced so that one still reading does not count.

The next part explains where these techniques are useful, and where they are not. They are the right tool whenever one stream must be read at another stream's times, or whenever a noisy stream must be turned into a steady number or a clean flag. They are cheap, they need no model of the arm, and they are easy to test on a recording.

They still fail in a handful of ways, and the page provides a table of common problems, the signs you would see, and what to use instead. If the pose is read when the picture arrives, objects land a few millimetres off, more when the arm moves fast, and are right when it is still. The fix is to look up the pose at the picture's stamp. If the driver stamps pictures on arrival instead of on exposure, you see the same error even though the code uses stamps. You must measure the stamping delay with a moving marker and subtract it. If two clocks disagree or drift apart, you see a constant offset in moving views that slowly grows. The solution is to use one clock for everything, such as the Precision Time Protocol, or use the driver's clock translation. If there is no pose within the slop, pairs are dropped, leading to fewer detections than pictures or gaps when the computer is busy. You should use a longer pose buffer, raise the joint rate, and count and report the drops. If the filter window is too long, contact is detected late and the force overshoots, or a threshold never fires during a short event. You should use a shorter window for detection, and a long one only for measurement. If a mean is used where readings spike, you get a jump the size of the spike divided by the window. Use a median instead, or a gate that discards readings far from the rest. If you take a slope from neighbouring readings, the speed or force rate swings wildly. Use a least-squares slope over a window, or a Kalman filter. If you use a single threshold near the working value, a flag or a stop chatters. Use hysteresis, debouncing, or both. Finally, if the value really changes in steps, but the filter smooths them, a step becomes a ramp, giving an estimate that is wrong in a believable way. Instead, use a Kalman filter that is told when the arm moves, or reset the filter at known events.

The most useful single test for pairing errors is this: if the result is right while the arm is still and wrong while it moves, then the problem is timing.

The next section lists libraries that provide these tools, so you rarely have to write them yourself. For pairing messages with equal or close stamps, or keeping a short history of one stream, ROS 2 provides Time Synchronizer, Approximate Time Synchronizer, and Cache in the message filters package. To get the pose of any frame at a given time, interpolated from published transforms, ROS 2 provides the lookup transform function in the tf2 buffer. For interpolation, moving averages, medians, and least-squares slopes on a buffer, Python's NumPy library has functions like interp, convolve, median, and polyfit. For proper low-pass filter design, or smoothing and slopes on a recorded trace, Python's SciPy library provides signal filtering functions. For configurable filter chains for ROS nodes, the ROS filters package provides mean, median, and transfer-function plugins. For a first-order low-pass filter used on wrenches and other controller signals in C++, ros-controls provides a Low Pass Filter in the control toolbox. And to keep computers' clocks and capable devices in step, command-line tools like ptp4l and chronyd are used. For a quick look at a recorded trace, NumPy is enough. But for a filter that runs in a ROS 2 control loop, use one from the control toolbox or the filters package, so that it runs in the same place as the controller.

The next part explains why these techniques are used, and what they cost. Whichever libraries you use, they all follow the same small set of rules for handling readings that arrive over time. Those rules are to pair readings by their stamps, correct the clocks, smooth them with a known delay, take slopes over a window, and put hysteresis and debouncing on every threshold. They give the arm poses that belong to the right picture, forces it can trust, and flags that change once.

The obvious alternative for pairing is to use the latest pose, which is simpler and needs no buffer. In the running example, though, it put the tool 11.7 millimetres from where it was, which is larger than most grasps can tolerate. And because that error only appears when the arm moves, it survives every still test.

The obvious alternative for smoothing is a Kalman filter, which gives a smoother value, a speed, and a measure of how sure it is. But it needs a model of how the value changes, and its settings are harder to choose. A moving average or a median has one setting, the window, and its delay is easy to state. That is why most arms use the simple filters for force and joint signals, and a Kalman filter for tracking things the camera sees.

The obvious alternative for a flag is a single threshold, which works only when the value passes through the threshold quickly. So when the value can linger near it, as a pressing force does, it chatters.

These techniques carry costs of their own, and the first is delay. Every filter and every slope window adds about half its window, so that delay moves every threshold later. A pose buffer uses memory and must be long enough for the slowest stream, while clock synchronisation is work to set up and must be checked again after hardware changes. Debouncing trades chatter for a fixed wait, and that wait must be counted like any other delay. And none of these tricks can fix a stream that is wrong in a steady way, because the median of a drifting sensor drifts with it.

The next section looks at the learned alternative. Since those costs are the price of rules written by hand, it is worth asking what a learned model would do instead. There is no learned model that replaces time stamps and clock correction, because pairing readings by time is bookkeeping with one right answer. For thresholds, though, there are learned models that read a short window of readings, as the filters on this page do. A learned collision detector tells a gentle bump from a fast movement by the shape of the torque gap over time, which one fixed stop line cannot. A slip model calls a slip from the pattern of the force signal, where a fixed rule needs a friction number that changes when the surface is wet. Both need recorded examples, including collisions or slips caused on purpose, and retraining when the gripper or payload changes. A learned detector adds to the certified safety function and never replaces it. So the filters, hysteresis and debouncing on this page stay underneath as the check that always works. When the job is to name the contact state, such as free, touching, pressing or slipping, from a few numbers per window, a decision tree or forest learns the thresholds from labelled windows. Then a hidden Markov model follows the states over time, so a single noisy reading does not flip the answer.
