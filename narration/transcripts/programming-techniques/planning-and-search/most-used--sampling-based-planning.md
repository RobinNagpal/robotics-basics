Sampling-based planning. 

This page explains sampling-based planning, which means finding a route for the arm by trying random joint angles, keeping the ones that hit nothing, and joining them up. It covers the three methods you will meet most, which are the rapidly-exploring random tree, or RRT, RRT-Connect, and the probabilistic roadmap, or PRM. It also explains the two ideas those three rest on, which are the configuration space and collision checking. Two further sections then open up the collision checker, and show how to plan when a rule, such as keeping a cup upright, must hold all along the path.

It is for a listener who has already covered the chapter overview and the page on graph search. This is because the page uses graph search's words, such as node, edge and path, without explaining them again.

Sampling-based planning is what most arm software uses to find a route. For example, MoveIt 2 calls the Open Motion Planning Library, or OMPL, and when no other planner is set it uses RRT-Connect. The page on planning a path covers that in practice, whereas this page explains how the methods work inside. Every number and picture described here comes from a real run of the planners on a two-joint arm.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. A sampling-based planner picks random sets of joint angles, throws away the ones where the arm would hit something, and joins the rest into a tree or a network until one of its routes links the start to the goal. 

Here is an everyday example of that idea. You are in a dark, cluttered garage with a torch that only lights a small spot, and you want to reach the door on the far side. Because you cannot see the whole garage, you point the torch at a random spot, and if it shows clear floor you step a little way towards it. Then you pick another random spot and step towards it from whichever point on your trail is closest. So your trail spreads out across the clear floor, until sooner or later a step lands next to the door. A sampling planner does the same thing, with joint angles in place of floor and a collision checker in place of the torch.

The next part of the page explains the configuration space, drawn for a real arm. The random spots in the garage example were places on a floor, but a planner does not search the room at all. Instead it searches the space of joint angles, which is called the configuration space. One set of joint angles is one point in that space, and such a point is called a configuration. 

The arm used here is a two-joint arm, whose first link is 3 metres long and whose second link is 2 metres long. Joint 1, the shoulder, can turn from minus 180 degrees to 180 degrees, and joint 2, the elbow, can turn from minus 160 degrees to 160 degrees. Three objects stand on the table, a box, a post and a jar. All three of them are more than 3 metres from the base, so only link 2 and the gripper can touch them. The start puts the gripper at one side of the table, and the goal puts it at the other. 

The diagram shows the table from above, and next to it, the same scene drawn as a configuration space. The horizontal axis is joint 1's angle, and the vertical axis is joint 2's angle. Each coloured band in this space is the set of configurations where the arm touches the object of the same colour. The bands cover about nine percent of the space. 

Three things in the picture are worth noticing, because each one explains why a planner has to work the way it does. First, a square box on the table becomes a curved band, and there is no simple formula for that band's shape. Planners do not describe the free space, they probe it. Second, the straight line from start to goal crosses two bands, so turning both joints at a steady rate would hit the post and then the box. Finally, the bands stop when the elbow is bent near 95 degrees or minus 95 degrees, because with the elbow bent more than that, link 2 folds back towards the base and passes inside the objects. So a free route does exist, and it is to bend the elbow sharply, swing round, and straighten it again. 

For a six-joint arm the configuration space has six axes, so nobody can draw it, and checking every cell would take billions of checks. So a sampler checks only a few thousand of them instead.

The next section covers collision checking, which is the only question a sampler asks. A planner never sees those bands, because it only ever asks about one configuration at a time. A collision checker is the program that answers that question, since it takes one configuration and replies yes, the arm hits something, or no, it is clear. It does that by placing the arm's shapes where the joint angles put them, and then testing those shapes against the shapes of the obstacles. Real checkers test meshes and simple shapes for overlap, and they are much faster than the planner around them. 

A sampler asks the checker two kinds of question, and the second kind is where most of the checking work goes. First, is this one configuration clear? This question is asked once for each random sample. Second, is this whole straight move between two configurations clear? This question is asked for each new edge, and because the checker cannot test every point on a line, it tests points along the line at a fixed spacing. 

The spacing in that second question is a real risk, because anything thinner than the spacing can slip between two checks unseen. The page shows a diagram of one move of the arm, where joint 1 turns 60 degrees. A rod 12 centimetres across stands on the gripper's path. When the move is checked every 10 degrees, the gripper moves 84 centimetres between one check and the next. Because the rod sits between the 40 degree check and the 50 degree check, no check touches it, the edge passes, and the arm would hit the rod. Instead, when the move is checked every 1 degree, the check at 45 degrees hits the rod, so the edge is correctly rejected. 

This is the problem described on the page about planning a path, where collisions are checked at sampled points, not continuously. OMPL sets the spacing as a fraction of the size of the whole joint space. So on an arm with wide joint limits the default comes to the order of ten degrees. Checking every 1 degree is safe but costs time. However, a continuous check removes the risk altogether.

The page then explains how a collision checker answers. A planner may ask it tens of thousands of questions for one move. So the checker is built to answer most of them without ever doing the hard sum. It works in three stages, which are a cheap test with simple wrappers, a tree of wrappers for detailed shapes, and an exact test only where the cheap ones cannot decide. It can also answer two harder questions, namely how far apart two shapes are and whether a whole motion is clear.

The first of those three stages uses a bounding volume, which is a simple shape that completely contains a detailed one. If two bounding volumes do not touch, then the shapes inside them cannot touch either. Because testing two simple shapes takes only a few comparisons, this rules out most pairs almost for free. 

Three kinds of bounding volume are common, and they trade how tightly they fit against how much the test costs. First, an axis-aligned bounding box is a box whose sides run along the x, y and z axes, and two such boxes overlap only if their ranges overlap on every axis. Second, an oriented bounding box is a box turned to fit the shape, so it fits much more tightly, although the test is a little more work. Third, a set of spheres, where two spheres touch when the distance between their centres is less than the sum of their radii, which is why many arm planners describe each link as a row of spheres. 

The diagram shows link 2 wrapped in an axis-aligned box and in four spheres. The axis-aligned box is more than four times bigger than the link itself, because the link lies at a slant. Four spheres cover it more tightly, and an oriented box would fit it exactly. When this first stage is run on the whole scene, most pairs of boxes do not even overlap, so those pairs are clear. Only the pairs with overlapping boxes go on to the exact test. So this first stage is called the broad phase, and the exact test that follows it is the narrow phase.

The broad phase puts one wrapper around a whole object, but a real object is often a mesh, which is a surface made of thousands of small flat triangles. Because testing a fingertip against every triangle would be slow, the checker builds a bounding-volume tree once, at the moment the object is loaded. First, it puts one box around the whole mesh, and that box is the top of the tree. Second, it splits the triangles into two halves along the longest side of the box, and puts a box around each half, so that those two boxes become its children. Finally, it repeats this for each half until each box holds only one or two triangles. 

To test something against the mesh you start at the top, and if it misses a box you can skip everything inside that box. If it touches a box instead, you look at that box's two children, and only at the bottom of the tree does the checker test real triangles. The diagram shows a mug outline inside a tree of boxes. A fingertip near the mug needs several box tests but no edge tests at all to prove it is clear. A fingertip touching the handle needs more box tests and a few edge tests to find the contact. Testing every edge would have taken many more tests, and the saving grows with the size of the mesh.

The two stages so far answer yes or no, but a planner often wants more than that. A trajectory optimiser needs the distance between the arm and each object, so that it can push the path away. In the same way, a safety check wants to know how close the arm came. A convex shape is a shape with no dents or holes, such as a box, a sphere or a cylinder. For shapes like those, the standard method for the distance is GJK, named after its inventors Gilbert, Johnson and Keerthi. 

GJK rests on one idea, which is to take every point of shape A and subtract every point of shape B. Then the result is a new shape, called the difference shape. If A and B overlap, then some point of A equals some point of B, so the difference shape contains the zero point, which is called the origin. If they do not overlap, then the distance between A and B is exactly the distance from the origin to the difference shape. 

GJK never builds the whole difference shape, because it only asks each shape one question, namely which of that shape's points is furthest in a given direction. From the answers it keeps up to three corners of the difference shape. Then it finds the point among them nearest the origin, and asks again in the direction of the origin. It stops once a new answer gets no closer, and for shapes like these it takes only a handful of rounds. 

The diagram shows a finger and a jar one and a quarter centimetres apart, and their difference shape, whose nearest point to the origin is exactly that distance away. When the shapes overlap the distance is zero, so a different number becomes useful. That number is the penetration depth, which is the shortest move that would separate the shapes. It is the distance from the origin, now inside the difference shape, to the nearest edge of that shape. A second method, the expanding polytope algorithm, or EPA, finds it by growing GJK's last triangle outwards until it reaches that edge. A shape with dents, such as a mug with a handle, is split into convex pieces first. Then the tree of boxes finds the pieces that might touch, and GJK runs on only those pieces.

The page then explains checking a whole motion, not just its ends. Continuous collision checking gets round the problem of thin objects slipping between fixed checks by answering a different question: does the arm touch anything at any moment during the move? One way to answer it is called conservative advancement, and it uses the distance that GJK gives. 

First, measure the distance from the arm to the nearest object. Second, work out how far any point of the arm can travel for a small turn of the joints. Third, turn the joint by an amount that guarantees no point of the arm can have reached the object yet. Finally, repeat until the distance is almost zero, which is a contact, or the move ends, which proves it clear. 

The diagram shows this in action. When the arm moves towards a rod, three distance queries with shrinking steps find the exact point of contact. When the rod is moved slightly out of reach, eleven queries with shrinking and then growing steps prove the whole move clear. Unlike fixed spacing, this cannot miss the rod, because every step is proved safe before it is taken. Other checkers sweep each link's shape along the move and test the swept shape. Either way, a continuous check costs more per edge than a single point check, and it is worth that cost for thin objects and fast moves.

Because a planner believes whatever the checker tells it, the problems of a collision checker are the ones the planner inherits. A mesh with holes or flipped triangles gives wrong answers, so people repair the mesh or replace it with a few simple shapes. A shape with dents, treated as convex, fills its own dents, so people split the shape into convex pieces. A very detailed mesh makes every check slow, so people use a simplified mesh for checking and the detailed one for drawing. And a point check at a fixed spacing misses thin objects, so people use a finer spacing, padding or a continuous check. 

The page lists three libraries that do this work. The Flexible Collision Library, or FCL, provides bounding-volume trees, GJK and continuous checks, and is MoveIt's default checker. Coal is a faster fork of FCL, used by Pinocchio. And Bullet is a physics engine whose collision code is also used on its own.

The next section explains how the planners work. All three use the same pieces, which are a sampler, a nearest-neighbour search, a step and the two collision questions. But they differ mainly in how they grow their structure.

The rapidly-exploring random tree, or RRT, grows a tree of free configurations out from the start. A tree here is a graph in which each node has exactly one parent, except for the very first node. So from any node you can follow parents back to the start. 

First, put the start in the tree. Second, pick a random configuration inside the joint limits, but now and then pick the goal itself instead. This is called goal bias, and it pulls the tree towards the goal. Third, find the node in the tree that is closest to the random configuration. This is a nearest-neighbour search. Fourth, take one short step from that node towards the random configuration. Fifth, ask the checker whether the new configuration is clear, and whether the move to it is clear. If both are, add it to the tree, with the nearest node as its parent. If not, throw it away. Sixth, if the new node is within one step of the goal, and the move to the goal is clear, add the goal and stop, then follow parents back from the goal, because that chain of parents is the path. Otherwise, go back to the second step and pick another random configuration. 

The name comes from the fact that a random configuration in a large empty region is most likely to be closest to a node on the edge of the tree. So the tree grows fastest into the space it has not yet covered. 

The diagram shows an RRT run. After 40 tries, the tree has 28 points, because the other tries landed in a collision band. At success, after 504 tries, the path goes over the top of the bands. The page gives a worked example of the first three tries. The first two tries pick random points, take a step towards them, and add the new clear points to the tree. The third try aims at the goal, but the step towards it lands in an obstacle band, so it is thrown away. This shows why a planner that only heads for the goal gets stuck, and why the random tries are what carry the tree around the obstacles.

RRT-Connect grows two trees instead of one, with the first from the start and the second from the goal, and it takes turns between them. First, grow one tree by one step towards a random configuration, as in RRT. Second, if that worked, take the new node and try to reach it from the other tree. Keep stepping from the other tree's nearest node towards it until you reach it or hit something. This greedy stepping is the connect in the name. Third, if the other tree reached the new node, then the two trees are joined, and the path is the start tree's branch followed by the goal tree's branch. Otherwise, swap the roles of the two trees and go back to the first step. Over 100 runs, RRT-Connect needed about a third fewer collision checks than RRT. In larger spaces with more joints the gap is usually much wider, which is why MoveIt uses RRT-Connect by default.

The probabilistic roadmap, or PRM, splits the work in two, so that the expensive half of it is done only once. The first part builds a roadmap, and it runs only once for a given scene. It picks many random configurations, throws away the ones in collision, and for each one that is left, finds its nearest few neighbours, adding an edge to each neighbour where the straight move is clear. The second part answers a query, and it can run as many times as you like. It joins the start and the goal to their nearest roadmap nodes, and runs Dijkstra's algorithm or A-star on the roadmap from the start to the goal. The build cost is high, but it is paid only once, so when the scene does not change every later query is cheap. When the scene does change, however, edges that pass through the new object are wrong, and the whole roadmap must be checked again. That is why RRT-Connect, which builds nothing in advance, is the usual choice for an arm whose scene changes every time.

The page describes the algorithm as a loop. It starts with a tree holding just the start position. It repeats up to a maximum number of tries, picking a target that is either the goal or a random configuration. It finds the nearest node in the tree, takes a step towards the target, and if the new configuration and the move to it are clear, it adds it to the tree. If it reaches the goal, it follows the parents back to get the path. Notice that if it stops, it does not say no path exists, it says that none was found in the time allowed. A sampler cannot tell those two cases apart. 

The planners are random on purpose, so the same problem gives a different answer each run. A table shows the spread over 100 runs. For RRT, the fastest run took 170 tries, the median took 408, and the slowest took 964 tries. The lengths of the paths vary just as much. This spread is the main reason samplers do not suit a cell that must do the same thing every time. RRT-Connect found paths with fewer checks, but its paths were longer on the median, because joining two trees greedily finds a path fast rather than a short one.

A sampler's path has detours, because each step went towards a random point, and two fixes for that are common. The first is shortcutting, in which you pick two points on the path at random. If the straight move between them is clear, you cut out everything between them, and you repeat that a few hundred times. The second is to hand the path to a trajectory optimiser, which makes it smooth and pushes it away from obstacles as well as shortening it. There are also planners that keep improving their own path, such as RRT-star and PRM-star, which reconnect nodes whenever a shorter route through them appears. Given more time their paths approach the shortest possible, but the cost is more time per try.

The next section is about planning with rules on the way, which is called constrained planning. In every section so far a path only had to avoid collisions, but many tasks add a rule that must hold at every moment of the move. For example, an arm carrying a cup of water must keep the cup upright. A rule like this is called a path constraint. 

The page gives an example of an arm moving a cup over a box. An ordinary RRT-Connect path lifts the cup over the box, tilting the cup nearly upside down on the way. Nothing in the planner cares about the cup, so nothing keeps it level. The obvious fix is to throw away every sample that breaks the rule, which is called rejection sampling, but it fails because almost every sample breaks the rule. A histogram of 10,000 random joint samples shows that only about 1 in 100 fall within 2 degrees of upright. For a real six-joint arm, roughly 1 in 10,000 samples would pass. A rule that must hold exactly is passed by no random sample at all. The reason is geometric, because the poses that satisfy the rule form a thin surface inside the configuration space, in the same way a line is thin inside a page. So a random point almost never lands on a line.

Projection is the fix that does work, because it takes a random sample and moves it, by the smallest change it can find, until the sample satisfies the rule. It uses the same idea as numerical inverse kinematics. First, measure how badly the sample breaks the rule, and call that number the error. Second, work out how the error changes when each joint moves a little. This is the rule's gradient. Third, move the joints against the gradient by exactly enough to make the error zero, as it would be if the rule were a straight line, and that is one Newton step. Finally, repeat until the error is below the tolerance. Throw the sample away if it takes too many steps or leaves the joint limits. 

The diagram shows random samples being pulled onto a curved rule in two or three Newton steps. A constrained sampler uses projection in three separate places. First, each random sample is projected before the planner uses it. Second, each short step towards a sample is projected, so the tree grows along the rule. Third, each point checked along an edge is projected, because the straight joint move between two poses on a curved rule leaves the rule in the middle. With these changes, the cup stays upright the whole way. OMPL has two further methods, called an atlas and a tangent bundle, which cover the rule's surface with small flat patches and give more even coverage than projection does.

Constrained planning is used for carrying liquid, pointing a camera, staying in contact with a surface, keeping two hands on one object, or opening a door. It brings problems of its own. If projection cannot find the rule, samples jump to far-off poses. If the rule is too tight with obstacles, the planner times out. If the rule is only checked at waypoints, the move between them can break the rule. And if the rule is to follow an exact straight line, a sampler is the wrong tool, and Cartesian paths should be used instead. MoveIt 2 handles constraints through its constraint messages, and switching OMPL to its projection-based planning works well for tight tolerances. OMPL provides the underlying constraint and state space classes, and TrajOpt can treat the rule as a hard constraint during trajectory optimisation.

The next part lists where sampling-based planning is actually used on a robot arm. It is the default way to move an arm through free space when the scene is not fixed. Concrete examples include moving from a camera pose to above a detected object, reaching into a bin or shelf where the free space is awkward, and carrying a held object, which becomes part of the arm for collision checking. It is also used for recovering after a fault, planning many queries in a fixed cell using a PRM, feeding an optimiser to make the route smooth, and checking whether a grasp is usable at all before committing to it.

The eighth section sets out the limits of a sampler. A sampling planner is probabilistically complete. This means that if a free path exists, the chance that the planner finds one grows towards certainty as it runs longer. It does not mean that the planner will find one in the time you give it. 

The page lists common problems and what people use instead. For a narrow gap, where few random samples land inside it, people use samplers that aim near obstacles, a learned sampler, or a longer time limit. If the same move must be identical every run, people use graph search on taught poses or an optimiser. For straight-line tool motion, they use a Cartesian path. If a rule must hold all the way, they use constrained planning with projection. If planning time must have a hard limit, they use an optimiser warm-started from the last answer. For collision checks that are too far apart, they use a smaller spacing or a continuous check. If the goal cannot be reached at all, which looks like a timeout, they check reachability first. If the path is ugly, they use shortcutting and a trajectory optimiser. And if the scene changes after a PRM roadmap is built, they rebuild it or use RRT-Connect. The narrow gap problem is severe because a gap that is one percent as wide as the space, in each of six joints, holds a tiny fraction of the random samples.

The next section lists the libraries that provide these planners, because almost nobody writes a production sampler from scratch. OMPL is the reference collection of samplers, providing RRT, PRM, and their variants, but it brings no collision checker of its own. MoveIt 2 connects OMPL to the arm model, the planning scene and the collision checker. FCL is MoveIt's usual collision checker. Coal is used by Pinocchio, which combines arm kinematics and collision checks in one library. And PyBullet is a simulator whose collision queries are handy for quick experiments. To try the ideas in Python, OMPL's Python bindings are the most direct route.

The tenth section answers why you would choose sampling and what it costs. It is a family of planners that probe the configuration space with random samples and join the clear ones into a tree or a roadmap. It finds a collision-free route for an arm with any number of joints, without anyone having to describe the free space first. 

The obvious alternative is graph search on a grid of the joint space, which gives the shortest path and the same answer every time. But a six-joint arm would need over two billion cells, whereas a sampler needs only a few thousand collision checks. So choose a sampler when the arm has more than about three joints and the scene changes. The second alternative is a trajectory optimiser, which gives a smooth path and the same answer every time, but can get stuck on the wrong side of an obstacle. A sampler does not get stuck that way, because it keeps exploring, which is why the two are often used together. 

Against all of that, sampling brings costs. The answer differs every run, the path has detours and needs cleaning, and the time to find a path has no fixed limit. A failure tells you nothing about why it failed. And the planner is only as good as its collision checker and its planning scene.

The next section covers the learned alternative, which is networks that either help this planner or stand in for it. A learned sampler picks its samples where routes usually pass, such as the front of a gap between shelves, which fixes the narrow gap problem. A learned route planner gives a whole route from a point cloud in about the same short time every run. A learned collision checker gives a quick guess for the thousands of checks a search makes. These win when planning is usually fast but sometimes far too slow, in a cell with a strict cycle time. In open space the ordinary planner still wins, because it is fast enough, free, and checks every move. Even beside a learned helper it stays in the system, because a network gives no guarantee and the exact checker must test the final route.
