This chapter is about language models. It answers three questions: what a model that reads words does for a robot arm, how words get into a model at all, and which of the three kinds of language model in this chapter you should read about first. This assumes you already know what a model is, which is a program that turns numbers in into numbers out, and that it learns this from examples. However, you do not need to know anything about language models yet, because every new word is explained where it first appears.

The first section covers what language models are for. They are the fifth of the seven families in the map of models, and their job is to understand words, and connect words to pictures and actions. A language model reads text and writes text. It was trained on a very large amount of written text, mostly from the public internet. During that training, it did one job over and over again: it saw the start of a sentence and guessed the next word. After enough practice, it guesses well, and to do that, it had to pick up a great deal about the world. For example, it knows that a spill is cleaned with a sponge, and that cups go in a cupboard. 

The very big language models are called large language models, or LLMs. Large means the model has a very large number of adjustable numbers inside it, usually billions. You may know some of them by name, such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. 

But a robot arm does not need a model to write essays. It needs three other things that words can give it instead. First, a person can tell the robot what to do in ordinary words, instead of writing a program. Second, the robot can use what the language model knows about everyday objects, such as which object is a sponge and what a sponge is for. And third, the robot can check its own work by asking a question about a camera picture, such as whether the mug is in the bowl.

The next part explains the question they answer for a robot arm. Every family of models answers one question, and for this family, the question is: what did the person ask for, and what does that mean here, in front of this camera? The first half of that question is about words, while the second half is about pictures and movement. A plain language model can only answer the first half, because it never sees the table. So, the models later in this chapter add a camera picture, and then movement, in order to answer the second half too.

The third section explains how words become numbers. A model only works with numbers, so before a model can read a sentence, the sentence has to be turned into numbers. This is done in two steps, one after the other. The page shows a diagram of the sentence "put the red mug in the sink" going through both steps, being cut into pieces and turned into lists of numbers. 

In the first step, the sentence is cut into small pieces called tokens. A token is often a whole word, but a long or rare word is cut into two or three tokens. For example, "screwdriver" might become "screw" and "driver". The model has a fixed list of all the tokens it knows, typically containing tens of thousands of entries. 

In the second step, each token is looked up in a table, and the table gives a list of numbers for every token. The same token always gets the same list of numbers. The model learned the numbers in this table during training, so tokens with similar meanings end up with similar lists, meaning words like "mug" and "cup" get lists that are close to each other. 

After these two steps, the sentence is a row of number lists. The model reads the whole row and works out what each token means in this sentence. The kind of network that does this is called a transformer. A transformer lets every token look at every other token in the row, which is how the model knows that "red" belongs to "mug" and not to "sink". 

This leads to an idea that matters for the whole chapter. A picture can also be cut into pieces and turned into lists of numbers, and so can a movement of the arm. Once everything is a row of number lists, one transformer can read words, pictures, and movements together.

The fourth section introduces the three kinds of models in this chapter. Because words, pictures, and movements all become the same kind of numbers, they can be put together in stages. A diagram shows these three kinds side by side, all dealing with the same mug, bowl, and instruction. 

First are language models as planners. A language model reads an instruction and writes a list of steps, chosen from skills the robot already has, but it never sees a picture itself. 

Second are vision-language models, or VLMs. A vision-language model takes a picture and a question in words, and answers in words. A robot uses it to find things, to describe a scene, and to check whether a step worked. 

Third are vision-language-action models, or VLAs. A vision-language-action model takes a picture and an instruction, and outputs the movement of the arm directly. It is one model that does the seeing, the understanding, and the moving. 

In terms of usage, vision-language-action models and vision-language models are where most of the work on language and robots happens as of 2026. Planning with a plain language model came first, and is still used to split a long instruction into steps, but this is now often done by a vision-language model instead.

The fifth section compares how these three kinds of models work. The page has a table showing that as you move from a planner, to a vision-language model, to a vision-language-action model, the model does more of the job by itself. A planner only takes in words and outputs a list of steps. It does not see the table or move the arm. A vision-language model takes in a picture and words, and outputs words or a point on the picture. It sees the table, but still does not move the arm. Finally, a vision-language-action model takes in a picture, words, and the arm's joint readings, and outputs the next movements of the arm directly. While the models that do more of the job are more capable, they also need more special training data. More of that data has to come from real robots, and because recordings from real robots are slow and expensive to collect, the vision-language-action model is the hardest to build.

The sixth section explains how this chapter connects to the others. Language models rarely work alone on a robot arm. First, a planner chooses the steps, but each step is then done by other models, such as a grasp model to decide where to hold an object, and a movement model to move the arm. Second, a vision-language model often does the same job as an open-vocabulary model from the seeing chapter, because both find objects from a name in words, but the vision-language model can also answer questions and explain what it sees. Third, a vision-language-action model is a movement model. It works in the same way as the models in the movement chapter, using the same ideas of action chunks and flow policies, but adds an understanding of words and pictures from the internet. Finally, some of the newest robot models also predict what the camera will see next, which connects to the next chapter on world models.

Nearly everything in this code was made by someone else. The pretrained model brings the list of tokens it knows, the rule for cutting a sentence into them, and the lookup table whose numbers were set during its training. You never write any of those yourself. What you write is the sentence that goes in, and the code that uses what comes out. On a robot, that means building the text you send and reading the answer you get back. Your main decision is which model to use, because that choice fixes everything else. A small model runs on your local computer but understands less, while a large model understands more but needs either a graphics card or a paid online service. Each of the three pages after this one shows that decision being made in a different way.
