Edges and contours. This page explains how a program finds the outline of an object in a picture, and what it can learn from that outline once it has traced it. Because a program does that work in a fixed order, the page follows the same order through five questions. What is an edge, and how is it computed from the raw brightness numbers? How does the Canny edge detector turn a noisy picture into thin, clean lines? How does a program walk round the outline of a blob and store it as a list of points? How does it then turn that list into a polygon with a few corners? And how does it count those corners, or fit a known shape, so that it can say "this is a triangle" or "this is a circle"? Because real outlines are often broken, a last section adds a sixth question: how does a program find a straight line or a circle when the outline comes in pieces?

It is for a reader who knows what a pixel and a grey picture are, and who has already read the page on thresholding and colour masks. That page explains how to turn a picture into a mask, which is a picture where each pixel is either "object" or "not object". This page often starts from such a mask rather than from the raw picture. So the mask has to be tidy before its outline is worth tracing, and this is explained more fully on the page about morphology and the distance transform.

On a robot arm, edges and outlines answer questions like "where exactly is the border of this part?", "which way is this block turned?" and "is this block a hexagon or a circle?". They answer them cheaply, because they cost a few milliseconds and need no training data at all.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. An edge is a place in a picture where the brightness changes sharply, and a contour is the closed line you get by following the edge all the way round one object.

For example, put a white plate on a dark wooden table and look at it. Because one side of the rim is dark and the other side is bright, your eye finds the plate's rim at once. If you traced that rim with a pencil on a photo, the line you drew would be its contour. If someone then asked whether the plate was round or square, you would look at the traced line and count its corners. This means a program does the same three things, in the same order, and the rest of this page is how it does each one.

The next part of the page explains how it works. The three things your eye did become five steps in a program. While the first two find edges in a grey picture, the last three start from a mask, trace its outline and describe its shape. Because it depends on what a program already has, a real program often uses only some of the five. For example, a program that already has a clean mask from a colour threshold skips steps one and two and begins at step three.

Step one computes the gradient of a picture. The gradient of a picture at a pixel is an arrow that points in the direction in which the brightness rises fastest. Because its length says how fast the brightness rises, the arrow has length zero in a flat area and is long on an edge.

Since a program cannot measure a slope at a single pixel, it has to compare that pixel with its neighbours. The most common way of doing so is the Sobel operator, which looks at the three by three patch of pixels round the pixel it is working on. It multiplies that patch by two small tables of weights, one for the change from left to right and one for the change from top to bottom. So between them the two tables cover both directions.

The weights for the change from left to right are minus one, zero, and plus one on the top and bottom rows, and minus two, zero, and plus two on the middle row. The weights for the change from top to bottom are similar, but rotated to measure vertical change. To get the left-to-right change, called gx, you multiply each weight by the pixel under it and add the nine results together. Then you do the same with the second table of weights to get the top-to-bottom change, called gy. The length of the arrow is the square root of gx squared plus gy squared, and its direction is the angle of the point formed by gx and gy. Because the pixels right next to the centre are the best evidence of what happens at the centre, the middle row and column carry a weight of two.

The page shows a diagram of an eight by eight picture where a bright square with brightness 200 sits on a dark table with brightness 20. The left panel shows the brightness numbers, and the right panel shows the gradient that the Sobel operator computes at every pixel.

Take the pixel in row three, column one, which is dark while its right-hand neighbour is bright. Its three by three patch reads 20, 20, 200 in each row. For gx, each row gives minus 20 plus zero plus 200, which is 180, and the middle row counts twice. So gx is 180 plus two times 180 plus 180, which equals 720. Because the top row and the bottom row are the same, they cancel, so gy comes out as zero. This means the arrow has length 720 and points straight to the right, towards the bright square.

The pixel in row three, column three sits inside the square, where the whole patch reads 200. So both sums come out as zero, and that pixel gets no arrow at all. The pixel on the square's top-left corner gets a gx of 540 and a gy of 540. This means its arrow has length 763.7 and points diagonally down and to the right, into the square. All of these numbers come from running the Sobel operator in NumPy on this exact picture.

Two things in the right panel matter for the step that follows. First, the arrows sit on both sides of the real border. This means a raw gradient gives an edge two pixels thick rather than one. Second, the arrows always point across the edge and never along it. That is why the Canny detector, which is the next step, can use both of those facts.

Step two is the Canny edge detector. Because the gradient from step one is both thick and noisy, something has to thin it down. The Canny edge detector, published by John Canny in 1986, is the standard way to turn a gradient into thin edge lines one pixel wide, and it does that in four steps.

First, it blurs the picture a little with a Gaussian blur. A Gaussian blur replaces each pixel by a weighted average of the pixels round it, with the nearest pixels weighted most. This removes single-pixel noise, which would otherwise give strong gradients everywhere. Second, it computes the gradient at every pixel with the Sobel operator. Third, it thins the edges. It looks at each pixel's two neighbours across the edge, in the direction of its gradient arrow. It keeps the pixel only if its gradient is at least as long as both of theirs. Otherwise it sets it to zero. This step is called non-maximum suppression, and it leaves only the ridge of each thick band. Finally, it keeps strong edges, and weak edges that touch them. It uses two thresholds, a high one and a low one. A pixel above the high threshold is a strong edge and is kept. A pixel between the two is a weak edge. It is kept only if it is connected, through other kept pixels, to a strong edge. Everything below the low threshold is dropped. This step is called hysteresis thresholding.

The two thresholds in the fourth step are the reason Canny works so well. For example, a real edge often has a strong part and a faint part, where a shadow falls across it. So a single high threshold would break the edge at the faint part, while a single low threshold would keep every patch of noise as well. The two thresholds together keep the whole of a real edge and still drop the noise that stands alone.

A diagram shows the four steps on a 64 by 96 picture that has a lot of noise in it. Because there is so much noise, the two thresholds really matter here. A dim triangle and a bright turned square sit on a darker table. Each panel is the real output of that step, computed in NumPy. The Gaussian blur has a spread of one pixel, the low threshold is 80 and the high threshold is 200.

The numbers tell the story of what each step takes away. A single threshold of 80 on the gradient keeps 1,185 pixels, which form thick bands round both shapes as well as many specks of noise. After thinning, 515 pixels above 80 remain, of which 163 are strong and the other 352 are weak. Hysteresis then keeps 76 of the weak pixels, because they touch a strong chain, and it drops the other 276. So the final result has 239 edge pixels in all. In the last panel the kept weak pixels are drawn in blue, and they lie almost all on the dim triangle, whose edge is too faint in places to be strong. Because the dropped weak pixels are scattered noise that touches no strong chain, they are drawn in light grey.

The whole of Canny comes to only a few lines of code, because each of its steps is a simple pass over the pixels. The code smooths the image, calculates the gradient size and direction for each pixel, and thins the edges by comparing each pixel to its neighbours along the gradient direction. It then finds all strong edge pixels, puts them in a queue, and grows the edges by adding any neighbouring weak pixels until the queue is empty.

Step three is tracing a contour. Canny gives back loose edge pixels rather than whole objects. So a program that wants to describe an object still needs its outline as one list of points in order, and that ordered list is the contour.

Because a mask's outline is always closed while an edge line can have gaps in it, contours are usually traced from a mask rather than from Canny edges. The usual method is border following, also called Moore-neighbour tracing. It works like a person walking round a pond while keeping one hand on the fence. First, scan the mask row by row from the top. The first object pixel you meet is on the outline. Call it the start. Second, stand on the current pixel. Look at its eight neighbours one by one, turning clockwise, beginning just after the pixel you came from. Third, the first object pixel you meet is the next outline pixel. Step onto it. Finally, repeat until you come back to the start pixel, arriving the same way as the first time.

The result is a list of pixel positions in walking order. For example, a diagram shows that list for a mask of a hexagonal block. The middle panel shows the 130 outline points the trace found, and the right panel shows the 6 corners that the next step keeps from them.

A contour gives several useful numbers straight away, before anything is simplified. Its area is the number of pixels inside it, which here is 1,624. Its perimeter is the length of the line through the outline points, which here is 154.0 pixels. Its centroid is the average position of the pixels inside. That is why it is a good point to aim the gripper at when the object is compact.

A mask can hold several objects, and an object can have a hole in it, such as a washer. This is why real contour functions handle both cases: they return one contour per outline, and they also record which contour lies inside which. Then, when you only want the outer outline of each object, you ask the function for the outer contours only.

Step four is simplifying the contour to a polygon. Because the traced outline follows every pixel step, it holds far more points than the shape itself needs. For example, a hexagon's outline has 130 points, while a person would describe the same shape with 6 corners. The Ramer-Douglas-Peucker algorithm removes the points that are not needed. It keeps a point only if leaving that point out would move the line by more than a chosen tolerance.

For an open line, which has a first point and a last point to work from, the method goes like this. First, draw a straight line from the first point to the last point. Second, find the point that lies farthest from that line. Third, if that distance is less than the tolerance, the straight line is good enough, so keep only the two end points. Otherwise, keep the farthest point, and repeat the whole method on the part before it and the part after it.

A contour is closed, however, so it has no first point and no last point to start from. The usual fix is to cut the loop in two at two points that lie far apart. Then simplify each half on its own, and join the two halves back together. Because the outline point farthest from the centroid is almost always a real corner, the code behind the pictures on this page starts there.

Since the tolerance is the one setting that matters here, it is worth choosing with care. For example, a common choice is two percent of the contour's perimeter, so that the setting grows with the object's size in the picture. For the hexagon that works out as 3.08 pixels, and it gives 6 corners.

A table shows how the number of corners changes with the tolerance, for the hexagon and for a circle of about the same size. The hexagon gives 6 corners over a wide range of tolerances, from one percent to five percent. This means that a wide flat range is the sign of a real corner count. The circle has no real corners, so its count keeps falling as the tolerance grows. It starts at 16 corners for a half-percent tolerance, and drops to 4 corners at a ten percent tolerance. For example, at a tolerance of five percent the circle would even pass for a square. This means that counting corners on its own cannot tell a circle from a polygon, so the next step adds a second measure.

Step five is counting corners and fitting shapes. Once a program has the polygon from step four, naming the shape is simple for flat parts with straight sides. Three corners means a triangle, and six corners means a hexagon. But four corners means either a square or a rectangle, and the side lengths and angles tell you which.

Round shapes have no corners, so they need a different test. The usual one is roundness, also called circularity. It is calculated as four times pi times the area, divided by the perimeter squared. It is one for a perfect circle and smaller for every other shape. For example, a perfect square gives 0.79 and a perfect equilateral triangle gives 0.60.

A diagram runs steps three to five on four masks of the same size, so the corner count and the roundness can be compared side by side. The measured roundness values are 0.56 for the triangle, 0.69 for the square, 0.83 for the hexagon, and 0.90 for the circle, which are all lower than the perfect values. They come out low because a traced outline steps from pixel to pixel, and so it is a little longer than the true smooth outline. The order is still right, however, and the circle stands clearly apart from the other three. This means a rule such as "roundness above 0.87 means round, otherwise count the corners" sorts all four shapes correctly here. But on your own camera you would measure the values on real parts before choosing that number.

For a round object you usually want its centre and its radius rather than a corner count. A least-squares circle fit finds the circle that passes closest to all the outline points at once. That fit is explained more fully on the page about least-squares fitting. Run on the circle's 128 outline points, it gives a centre of 30.0, 30.0 and a radius of 22.56 pixels. The mask itself was drawn with its centre at 30, 30 and a radius of 23 pixels. So the fit is half a pixel small, because the traced points are the centres of the outer ring of pixels, and those centres lie half a pixel inside the true edge. This is a real effect rather than a mistake, so it is worth knowing about when you turn pixels into millimetres.

Other fits work in the same way, and each one answers a different question about the part. For example, the smallest turned rectangle round a contour gives the part's length, width and angle, while a fitted ellipse gives the tilt of a round rim. A program can also compute the convex hull, which is the outline you get by stretching a rubber band round the contour. Then comparing the hull's area with the contour's area finds dents and notches.

The next section explains finding lines and circles by voting, using the Hough transform. Steps three to five all start from a clean mask with one closed outline. But real edges are often not like that at all. A cable lies across the edge of the table, a shadow hides part of a box, or a gripper finger covers a quarter of a cup's rim. In each of those cases Canny gives back several short pieces of edge, plus specks of texture. So in those cases no single contour is the whole object.

The Hough transform finds straight lines and circles in exactly those broken edges. Its idea fits into one sentence: every edge pixel votes for every shape that could pass through it, and the shape with the most votes wins.

For example, ask a room of people, each standing somewhere on a large floor, to name every straight path across the room that passes through their own spot. Most paths get one or two names, but if ten people happen to stand in a row, the path along that row is named ten times. This means you find the row by counting names, without ever looking for it directly. It does not matter if there are gaps in the row, or if other people stand around at random.

For lines, every point votes for every line through it. A straight line in a picture can be described by just two numbers. In other words, a vote for a line is really a vote for a pair of numbers. The first number is the line's angle, and the second is its distance from the picture's top-left corner, measured straight across to the line. With those two numbers, a pixel at coordinates x and y lies on the line when the distance equals x times the cosine of the angle, plus y times the sine of the angle.

The Hough transform keeps a vote table, also called an accumulator. It has one row for each possible distance and one column for each possible angle, and every cell starts at zero. Then, for each edge pixel, the program tries every angle, works out the distance of the line through that pixel at that angle, and adds one vote to the matching cell. This means each pixel draws a wavy curve of votes across the table. Because pixels that lie on one line all vote for the same cell, their curves cross at that cell.

A diagram shows lines through one point, and the curves of four points in the vote table. In the left panel, pale blue lines show some of the lines through a single pixel, and each one of them is a single vote. In the right panel each pixel's votes form one curve. Because three of the pixels lie on one line, their curves cross at one spot: an angle of 64 degrees and a distance of 40 pixels. At that angle the formula gives distances that all round to 40 and land in the same cell, which therefore gets three votes. A fourth pixel is not on that line, so its curve misses that cell altogether.

The method is short in code, because it is two loops followed by a search for the fullest cells. It creates a table of zeros, then loops through each edge pixel. For each pixel, it loops through angles from zero to 180 degrees, calculates the distance, and adds a vote to that cell in the table. Finally, it repeatedly finds the cell with the most votes, adds that line to its results, and clears the cells around it so the same line is not reported twice. Because a line at 190 degrees is the same line as one at 10 degrees with the sign of the distance flipped, only angles from zero to 180 degrees are needed.

A worked example shows a broken table edge. A diagram puts the vote table to work, starting from 167 edge pixels. One hundred of them lie on the front edge of a table, which has two gaps where a cable and a shadow cross it. Twenty-seven lie on one side of a box, which has one gap. But the other 40 are specks of table texture, and they belong to no line at all.

The vote table has over 54,000 cells in all. Each bright band in it is one pixel's curve, and two bright peaks stand out. The table edge gets 88 votes, at an angle of 76 degrees and a distance of 68 pixels. The other 12 of its 100 pixels round into the next row, at a distance of 67. This is why the program clears the cells round a peak once it has taken that peak, because otherwise it would report the same line twice. The box side gets 28 votes, at an angle of zero degrees and a distance of 88. That is its own 27 pixels plus one pixel of the table edge, which crosses the box's line. Because specks do not line up with each other, no speck gets more than a few votes. The two lines are found, and each one runs straight across the gaps. Because a gap only means a few missing votes, the line is found all the same.

For circles, such as the rim of a cup seen from above, the Hough transform uses the same voting idea with a different shape of vote. A circle needs three numbers rather than two: the x and y of its centre, and its radius. First fix the radius, say 20 pixels, so that only the centre is left to find. Then every point at a distance of 20 from an edge pixel could be the centre. So each edge pixel votes for every cell on a circle of radius 20 round itself. Because the real centre lies at 20 pixels from every rim pixel, all of those circles of votes pass through it.

A diagram shows 134 edge pixels, of which 109 lie on the rim of a cup. A gripper finger hides a quarter of the rim, and the other 25 pixels are specks. The votes of the rim pixels are circles that meet at the centre. The vote table has one cell for each possible centre. Here the highest cell is exactly at the true centre, with 69 votes, while the highest cell anywhere else has only 12. Because each rim pixel sits at a whole-pixel position, the peak does not get all 109 votes. Its true distance from the centre is a little more or a little less than 20, so its circle of votes can pass through a neighbouring cell instead. The peak is still clear, however, even with a quarter of the rim missing.

When the radius is not known, the program tries a range of radii and keeps the one whose best cell has the most votes. A diagram shows the number of votes at the best centre for each radius tried, from 10 to 30 pixels. Radius 20 gets 69 votes, while radius 19 gets 34 and radius 21 gets 32. Every other radius gets 26 or fewer, so the radius is found as well as the centre. This means that neither the centre nor the radius has to be known beforehand.

However, trying every radius costs a lot of time, because the vote table becomes three-dimensional. So OpenCV's HoughCircles function avoids that cost with a shortcut, using the direction of each pixel's gradient from step one. On a circle the gradient points straight towards or away from the centre. This means each pixel only has to vote along one short line instead of round a whole circle.

The Hough transform earns its place on a robot arm because it finds lines or circles hidden inside broken edges. First, it finds the rim of a cup, bowl or bottle seen from above, giving the centre and radius even when a finger or a handle hides part of the rim. Second, it finds round parts on a tray, like washers or coins, including ones that overlap a little, because each circle keeps its own peak. Third, it finds straight edges for alignment, like the edge of a table or the long side of a box, whose angle tells the arm how the object is turned. Finally, it finds holes and pegs, letting a camera on the wrist look down at a hole before an insertion.

But it fails in four ways as well, and each failure has a sign you can watch for. First, too many edges. On a patterned or textured surface, random pixels line up by chance and give false peaks, so you would see lines or circles reported where there is nothing. The fix is to raise the vote threshold, blur more before Canny, or work from a colour or depth mask instead. Second, a slanted view. A circle seen at a slant is an ellipse, so the circle vote smears out and you would see a weak peak, or two circles on one rim. The fix is to look from straight above, or fit an ellipse to the contour. Third, coarse cells or a wrong radius range. Cells that are too big merge two nearby lines, while cells that are too small split one line's votes over several cells. A radius range that leaves out the real radius finds nothing at all. Finally, clean outlines. When the outline is whole and there is little noise, a least-squares fit to the contour is faster and more exact. When some points are wrong but the object is one shape, RANSAC is the other common choice. Hough is best when several lines or circles hide in one set of broken edges, because every shape gets its own peak.

You never have to write the voting loop yourself. OpenCV provides functions for Hough lines, including a faster probabilistic version that returns line pieces with two end points, and a function for Hough circles. The scikit-image library also provides functions for Hough lines and circles, along with functions to pick the peaks.

The next section explains where these methods are used on a robot arm. Edges and contours turn up at many points, and these are the most common ones. First, sorting blocks by shape. A camera looks down at blocks on a table, a threshold gives a mask, the contour gives a polygon, and the corner count and roundness then give the shape. Second, finding which way a part is turned. The smallest turned rectangle round a contour gives the angle of a long part, and the wrist then turns by that angle so the gripper fingers close across the part's width. Third, measuring a part before grasping it. The contour's width in pixels, with the depth and the camera's focal length, gives the width in millimetres. The program then checks that the part fits between the gripper fingers. Fourth, checking a part against a drawing. For flat parts, the contour can be compared with the outline in a drawing, so holes, burrs and missing corners show up as differences. Fifth, finding printed markers. Square fiducial markers are found by looking for contours whose polygon has exactly four corners. Sixth, finding the rim of a cup or bowl. An ellipse fitted to the rim's edge tells the program where the opening is and how it is tilted. Finally, checking the result of a place. After placing a part in a tray, the arm takes a picture, and a small gap between the part's contour and the tray pocket's contour means the part sits correctly.

The uses in the last section all share the same conditions. Edges and contours work best when the object is flat or seen straight from above, has a clear border against a plain background, and does not touch anything else. This means they get worse quickly as soon as any of those conditions fails.

A table lists the common failures. If there is low contrast between the object and the table, Canny finds only parts of the outline, and contours come back open or too small. People use better lighting, a contrasting table colour, or a depth threshold instead. With textured objects or a patterned table, you get hundreds of edges inside the object, and no single contour is the object. People use a colour or depth mask first, then find contours of the mask. With shadows, the contour includes the shadow, so the object looks bigger or the wrong shape. People use diffuse lighting from several sides, or a depth camera. When objects touch each other, two objects come back as one contour with too many corners. People use the distance transform and watershed, or depth clustering. If the camera is at a slant, squares look like irregular four-sided shapes, circles look like ellipses, and roundness drops. People look from straight above, or correct the view with the camera's known pose. For a small or far object, corners get rounded off, and a hexagon might report four or eight corners. People move the camera closer, or use a larger tolerance only if the shapes are very different. If there is noise in the mask, a single stray pixel adds an extra corner or an extra tiny contour. People use an opening step on the mask, and a minimum contour area. Finally, for see-through or shiny objects, the edge found is a reflection, not the object's border. People use a segmentation model trained on such objects.

The next section covers libraries that provide these methods. Because every step on this page is available in well-known libraries, nothing has to be written from scratch. OpenCV provides functions for the Sobel gradient, the full Canny detector, border following on a mask, polygon simplification, perimeter and area, fitting shapes like rectangles, ellipses, and circles, and the Hough transform for lines and circles. It can be used from C++, Python, and Java. The scikit-image library in Python provides similar functions for gradient, Canny, contours, polygon simplification, the Hough transform, and measuring region properties like area and centroid. SciPy provides the Sobel gradient on any NumPy array, including a depth picture. For 3D point clouds, the Point Cloud Library in C++ provides boundary estimation, which marks points on the border of a surface.

The next section asks why you should use edges and contours, and what they cost. Edges and contours are a fixed set of rules. The gradient finds where brightness changes, Canny thins that into lines, and border following turns a mask into an ordered outline. Then polygon simplification and shape fitting describe that outline in a few numbers.

What they do for you is give exact geometry. A contour tells you the border of an object to within about a pixel, and from that border you get the area, the centre, the angle, the width and the shape class. Every one of those numbers is easy to check by hand. So when one of them is wrong, you can see why by drawing the contour on the picture.

The costs of these methods come in three parts. First, you must control the scene, because every method on this page depends on a clear border between the object and its background. Second, you must choose thresholds, which are the two Canny thresholds, the mask threshold, the polygon tolerance and the roundness cut-off. Because each one depends on the lighting and the camera, each one needs checking on real pictures. Third, a contour is flat, so it says nothing about height, and it says nothing about the parts of the object the camera cannot see.

The obvious alternative is a trained segmentation model or object detector. The next section says what that model gives you and what it asks for in return. A segmentation model or an object detector finds the object's outline or box, and a keypoint and pose model finds which way that object is turned. A model copes with the texture, clutter and shadows that break contours. But it needs labelled training pictures and a computer that can run it, and it can fail in ways that are hard to explain. So choose contours instead when the scene is controlled, which means a plain table, good light, parts that stand apart, and shapes that differ in clear geometric ways. Because a model's output mask still has to be turned into a centre, an angle and a size, programs often run contour steps on it even when a model is used.
