Iterative closest point. This page explains iterative closest point, or ICP. It is a method that moves one set of points onto another set of points that shows the same shape. On a robot arm, the first set is usually a stored model of a part, and the second is a depth scan of the real part. So ICP answers the question, exactly where is the part, and which way is it turned? This page shows how ICP works, round by round, on a small example with real numbers. It then shows when ICP gives a wrong answer, and how you can tell that it has.

It is written for a listener who has already learned about nearest-neighbour search, because every round of ICP runs that search. It also helps to know what a rotation and a shift are, which is explained on the page about rigid transforms, although this page explains each term where it needs it.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. ICP guesses which scan point each model point belongs to by taking the closest one, moves the model to fit those guesses as well as possible, and repeats until the model stops moving.

The move ICP finds is a rigid transform, which means a rotation plus a shift with no stretching and no bending. That is the right kind of answer, because a part lying on a table cannot change its shape.

Here is an everyday example of the same loop. Suppose you have a paper outline of a key, and you want to lay it over the real key lying on a table. You put the paper down roughly where the key is. Then you see that each edge of the paper sits a little to the left of the matching edge of the key. Because of that, you slide the paper to the right and turn it a little. Now the edges are closer, which means you can see better which edge of the paper goes with which edge of the key, so you slide and turn again. After a few small moves the paper sits on the key, and ICP does the same thing with points instead of edges.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step. It walks through that same loop on one small example, one round at a time. So every claim made about it here comes with a number you can check.

The example is an L-shaped metal bracket lying flat on a table, seen from above by a depth camera. It is kept flat only so that the pictures fit on a page. A real scan has three coordinates per point, but every step below works the same way in three dimensions.

First, the model is the bracket's outline as it was drawn. It is 120 millimetres long, 80 millimetres high, with arms 30 millimetres wide. It has one point every 10 millimetres round the outline, making 40 points in all.

Second, the scan is what the camera saw, with one point every 6 millimetres and 66 points in all. Each point is moved by a small random amount, the way a real depth camera's points are. That random amount has a standard deviation of 0.8 millimetres in each direction, and the standard deviation is the typical size of a random error.

Third, the real bracket is turned 20 degrees from the model and shifted by 30 millimetres in x and 20 millimetres in y. ICP is not told any of this, because finding it is its job.

Finally, ICP starts from a first guess, which is the model exactly where it was drawn, not turned and not shifted.

Step one is to pair each model point with its closest scan point. Because ICP does not know which scan point belongs to which model point, it starts by guessing. Each model point is paired with the scan point nearest to it. That guess is a nearest-neighbour search run once for every model point, and the pairs it produces are called correspondences.

In a diagram of the pairs that this first search produces, before the model has moved at all, you can see that many of these pairs are wrong. The model points along the left edge all reach across to the scan's left edge, which is higher up and turned. Several model points even share one scan point. The mean length of the lines connecting the pairs, called the mean gap on this page, is 15.7 millimetres.

The pairs are wrong, but they are not random, because on the whole they pull the model up, to the right, and anticlockwise. That is already enough, since the next step only needs to move the model in roughly the right direction.

Step two finds the best move for those pairs. Once the pairs exist, ICP works out the one rotation and shift that brings each model point as close as possible to its partner. As close as possible means that the sum of the squared distances between partners is as small as it can be. There is an exact method that finds this move, and it takes three steps.

First, find the middle of the model points, and the middle of their partners. The middle is the average of all the x values and of all the y values. It is called the centroid.

Second, measure every model point from the model's centroid, and every partner from the partners' centroid. Now both sets are centred on the same spot. Find the turn that lines the first set up with the second as well as it can. A standard piece of linear algebra, the singular value decomposition, or SVD, gives this turn directly. The method is known as the Kabsch method, or the Umeyama method.

Third, the shift is whatever then moves the turned model's centroid onto the partners' centroid.

Here is the same method on three points, which is small enough to check by hand. The model is a triangle with corners at zero zero, forty zero, and zero twenty. The partners are that same triangle turned 30 degrees and shifted by ten and five, so their corners are at ten five, forty-four point six four and twenty-five, and zero and twenty-two point three two.

Following the steps, first, the model's centroid is at thirteen point three three and six point six seven. The partners' centroid is at eighteen point two one and seventeen point four four. Second, centred on their centroids, the two triangles differ only by a turn. The SVD finds it to be exactly 30 degrees. Third, turning the model's centroid by 30 degrees about the origin moves it to eight point two one and twelve point four four. The shift that takes it to eighteen point two one and seventeen point four four is exactly ten and five.

The method finds the turn and the shift exactly, because in this small example the pairs are right. In ICP itself the pairs are only guesses, so the move the method finds is only partly right. The model is then moved by it, and ICP goes back to step one.

This repeats until the gap stops shrinking. Because each move brings the model closer to the scan, the pairs found after it are better, which means the next move is better as well. If you plot the model after each round, you see that after one round the model has barely turned, after four rounds it is part of the way there, and after 18 rounds it sits on the scan.

The numbers show this progress. At the start, the mean gap is 15.74 millimetres, with zero turn and zero shift. After round 4, the gap falls to 7.62 millimetres, the turn reaches 7.77 degrees, and the shift is roughly halfway to the target. By round 12, the gap is down to 1.91 millimetres, and the turn is 18.33 degrees.

ICP also needs a rule for when to stop. The rule here is to stop when the mean gap falls by less than 0.001 millimetres in one round, which happened after round 18. Real libraries use the same kind of rule, and they add a largest number of rounds, such as 30 or 50, so that ICP always ends whatever the scan looks like.

If you graph the mean gap after each round, it falls fast for the first twelve rounds, then hardly changes, and that is when the stop rule ends the loop.

The answer ICP reaches is 19.2 degrees instead of 20 degrees, and a shift of 30.0 and 20.3 millimetres instead of exactly 30 and 20. As a result, it is close but not exact, and there are two reasons for that. The first reason is that the scan's points carry noise. The second reason is that the model's points and the scan's points do not sit at the same places along each edge, because one set is spaced 10 millimetres apart and the other 6 millimetres. So even at the right pose, each model point's closest scan point is up to 3 millimetres along the edge from it. This is also why the mean gap ends at 1.70 millimetres rather than at the 0.8 millimetres of noise.

The pseudocode for this process puts the two steps and the stop rule into a plain sequence. It takes the model points, scan points, a first guess, a maximum number of rounds, a stop change limit, and a maximum pair distance. It starts by setting the pose to the first guess and moving the model points by that pose. Then it repeats the loop up to the maximum number of rounds.

In step one, it finds the pairs. For each point in the moved model, it finds the nearest point in the scan using a k-d tree built once at the start. If the distance between them is less than or equal to the maximum pair distance, it keeps the pair. It calculates the mean distance over these pairs, which is the gap. If the previous gap minus the current gap is less than the stop change limit, the loop stops.

Otherwise, it moves to step two, finding the best move for these pairs. It calculates the centroid of the model points in the pairs, and the centroid of the scan points in the pairs. It uses SVD to find the rotation that best lines up the centred points, and calculates the shift to match the centroids. It applies this rotation and shift to the moved points, combines it with the overall pose, and continues the loop. Finally, it returns the pose and the gap.

The scan does not move during the loop, so its k-d tree is built once, before the first round. The maximum pair distance test throws away pairs that are too far apart to be real. That test matters when the scan also holds other things, such as the table or a neighbouring part.

There is a faster version called point-to-plane ICP. The version just described is called point-to-point ICP, because it measures the gap from each model point to one scan point. But that measure is slow when a surface can slide along itself, which is why the long flat edges of the bracket took many rounds to slide into place in the example.

Point-to-plane ICP measures the gap from each model point to the flat surface through its partner instead. That surface is set by the partner's normal, which is the direction straight out of the surface. With this measure a model point that slides along the right surface costs nothing. So ICP spends its moves only on the direction that matters, and it usually needs far fewer rounds. However, it needs normals for the scan, which means one more k-nearest search per scan point before ICP starts, and libraries like Open3D and PCL provide the method.

The next section covers where ICP is used on a robot arm. Because ICP sharpens a pose rather than finding one from nothing, it is used wherever the arm already has a rough idea of where something is and needs an exact one. There are several common jobs, and each one supplies that rough idea in a different way.

First, finding the exact pose of a known part. A detector or a simple rule finds roughly where a part lies in a bin. ICP then lines the part's computer-aided design, or CAD, model up with the depth scan, and gives a pose accurate enough to grip it at a chosen spot.

Second, finishing a learned pose. A learned pose model gives a pose that is often a few millimetres or a few degrees off. A few rounds of ICP from that pose fix the last part of the error. This is a common pairing: the learned model gives the first guess, and ICP gives the precision.

Third, joining several views into one cloud. A camera on the wrist takes pictures from several arm poses, and the arm's joint readings say roughly where the camera was for each picture. ICP then lines each new cloud up with the ones before it, to remove the small errors left by calibration and by the joints.

Fourth, following a part that moves a little. When a part is being pushed or is in the gripper, its pose in one frame is a good first guess for the next frame. ICP from that guess follows the part with a small number of rounds per frame.

Fifth, checking a placement. After the arm sets a part down, a scan and ICP against the model say where the part actually ended up. If the pose differs from the planned one by more than a limit, the robot can try again.

Finally, checking calibration. If the camera-to-arm calibration is right, a scan of a known object at a known place lines up with its model with almost no movement. If ICP has to move it by several millimetres, the calibration has drifted.

The next section explains where ICP is useful, and where it is not. All the jobs just mentioned rely on ICP being precise, which it is whenever it starts close to the answer. The danger is that ICP always returns an answer, even when that answer is wrong. It stops as soon as the gap stops shrinking, and the gap can stop shrinking at a wrong pose.

For example, if the same bracket is scanned twice, once turned 20 degrees and once turned 120 degrees, and ICP starts from the same first guess in both runs, the results are very different. From 20 degrees away, ICP finds the bracket. It also finds it from 45, 60, and 90 degrees away, after 25 to 36 rounds. From 120 degrees away, however, it stops after 9 rounds with the model turned minus 5.9 degrees and lying across the scan. Its mean gap there is 13.7 millimetres, against 1.7 millimetres for the good result. That difference is the sign to watch for, because a final gap much bigger than the camera's noise is what a wrong pose looks like. Such a stuck answer is called a local minimum, which is a pose from which every small move makes the gap worse, although it is not the best pose overall.

There are several common problems to watch out for. If the first guess is too far off, the sign you see is that the final gap is much larger than the camera noise. Instead of relying on ICP alone, people use a global method first, such as random sample consensus, or RANSAC, on matched surface features, or they run ICP several times from different starting turns and keep the best.

If the part is symmetric, such as a cylinder or a square plate, the gap is small, but the turn about the symmetry axis is random from run to run. In this case, you must accept that the turn is unknown, or use colour or a marking.

If the scan also holds the table or other parts, the model is pulled towards them. To fix this, cut the part out of the scan first, and reject pairs further apart than a limit.

If only part of the object is visible, the model slides to cover the missing side. You should reject far pairs, or use a model of only the side the camera can see.

If dealing with a large flat surface, such as a box lying on a table, the model slides along the surface and never settles, or settles in the wrong place along it. Point-to-plane ICP helps with speed here, but a feature such as an edge or a hole is needed to fix the position.

If the model is in millimetres and the scan in metres, you will get a nonsense answer after one round. Always check the units before calling ICP.

Finally, if ICP takes many rounds on large clouds, it becomes too slow for the camera rate. The solution is to downsample both clouds to a point every few millimetres first, and use point-to-plane ICP.

The next section is about getting a first guess using 3D features and global registration. Because ICP needs a first guess close to the answer, this method shows how a program can get one from the scan alone, with no idea at all where the part is or which way it is turned. The method is called global registration, in which registration means lining two point sets up. Global means that the method searches every possible pose rather than only the poses near a guess.

It does for depth scans what image features and matching does for pictures, and it does so in four steps. First, work out which way the surface faces at every point. This direction is the point's normal. Second, describe the shape round every point as a short list of numbers, called a local 3D feature. Third, pair each model point with the scan point whose feature is most alike, and keep the pairs that agree on one rigid move, using RANSAC. Fourth, use that rough move as ICP's first guess.

The example here is the case where ICP failed earlier, which is the bracket turned 120 degrees.

The first step is to find surface normals. A normal is the direction straight out of a surface, at right angles to it. A single point on its own has no direction, so the program looks at the point's neighbours instead. It finds the point's 5 nearest neighbours with a nearest-neighbour search, and it fits the straight line that passes closest to them and to the point itself. Then it takes the direction at right angles to that line. In 3D it fits a flat plane instead of a line, so the normal is the direction at right angles to that plane. The fit uses principal component analysis, or PCA, a standard method that finds the direction in which a set of points is spread out most and the direction in which it is spread out least. That is why the normal is the direction of least spread.

On the straight edges of the bracket, these normals are neat and parallel. At the corners they point at an angle, because the neighbours there come from both edges. The fit cannot tell out of the part from into the part, so a normal can point either way. Real programs therefore turn every normal to face the camera, because a camera can only see surfaces that face it. The feature described next ignores which way a normal points, so it does not need that step.

The second step is local 3D features. Every point now has a normal, but a normal only says which way a surface faces, and many points on the same part face the same way, so a normal on its own cannot tell those points apart. To tell them apart, the program describes the shape of the whole neighbourhood round each point. The best-known description of that kind is the fast point feature histogram, or FPFH. For one point, FPFH looks at every neighbour within a set radius. For each of those neighbours it measures, in three angles, how the neighbour's normal is turned relative to the point's own normal. It then counts how many neighbours fall into each range of angles. Those counts form a histogram, which is a row of bins, with each bin holding how many things fell into it. FPFH has 33 bins in all, and because each bin describes part of the shape round the point, two points on similar shapes, such as two sharp edges, get similar histograms. However, a point on a flat surface gets a very different histogram.

For example, a point in the middle of a long straight edge has most of its neighbours far away and facing the same way. A point at an outer corner has neighbours on two edges at right angles, so a share of them are far away and facing across. A point at the tip of an arm sees the short end and the inner edge, giving a different mix again. Points along the middle of a straight edge therefore all look alike, which is why the corners and the arm ends are what make the bracket recognisable.

A second well-known kind of feature is the point pair feature, or PPF, which describes two points at a time rather than one. For two points with normals it stores four numbers: the distance between them, the angle between each normal and the line joining them, and the angle between the two normals. These four numbers stay the same wherever the object is and however it is turned. A program stores the four numbers for every pair of points on the model in a table. In the scan, each pair of points then looks itself up in that table, and every model pair it matches votes for a pose, so the pose with the most votes wins.

The third step is matching features, then RANSAC. Once every point has a feature, the program pairs each model point with the scan point whose feature is most alike. This is again a nearest-neighbour search, but among features instead of among positions. Most of these pairs are wrong, because edge points all look alike, and in the example only 5 of the 40 pairs lie within 5 millimetres of the right scan point.

RANSAC does not mind that most of the pairs are wrong, because it repeats a set of steps many times and keeps only the move that the most pairs agree with. First, it picks two pairs at random. In 3D it picks three. Second, it checks that the two model points are about as far apart as the two scan points. If not, at least one pair is wrong, so the try is skipped at once. This cheap check saves most of the work. Third, it computes the rigid move that fits the two pairs. Fourth, it counts the pairs that the move brings within 5 millimetres of each other. Finally, it remembers the move with the most such pairs.

In the example, RANSAC found a move that 5 pairs agree with on its 90th try out of 500. Every one of the 35 wrong pairs is thrown away, because it does not agree with that move. Five right pairs out of forty is a small share. So two pairs picked at random are both right only about once in 64 tries, which is why it took 90 tries.

Fast global registration, or FGR, is a common alternative to RANSAC for this step, and it does not pick random pairs at all. Instead, it fits one move to all the pairs at once, then repeats the fit while giving the pairs that disagree with the move less and less weight. In practice it is usually faster than RANSAC, and it gives the same kind of rough answer.

The fourth step is finishing with ICP. The move from RANSAC rests on a handful of pairs, so it is only rough, and ICP is what then makes it exact. Comparing the results for the bracket turned 120 degrees shows this clearly. ICP alone, from the drawn pose, gets stuck after 9 rounds, ending up at minus 5.9 degrees with a large mean gap of 13.7 millimetres. Features and RANSAC, with no ICP, get the turn to 118.7 degrees with a gap of 2.6 millimetres. But features and RANSAC, followed by ICP, take just 5 rounds of ICP to reach 118.9 degrees with a small gap of 1.7 millimetres.

The feature pose alone is already within about 1.3 degrees of the right turn. So ICP starting from there needs only 5 rounds, where it needed 18 rounds from a 20 degree start earlier. The final answer is 1.1 degrees from 120 degrees, and the part's centre moved by 30.2 and 19.2 millimetres. It stops short of the exact answer because the model points and the scan points sit at different places along each edge. The final mean gap, 1.7 millimetres, is the same as in the good result earlier, and that sameness is the sign that the answer is right.

Everything just described works the same way in 3D, with three changes. First, the normal comes from a plane fitted to the neighbours rather than from a line. Second, RANSAC picks three pairs at a time rather than two. Third, the clouds are first downsampled, which means thinning them to one point every few millimetres, because computing a feature for every one of 300,000 camera points is slow.

On a robot arm, global registration is used for picking a known part that can lie any way up, because no first guess is good enough for ICP alone. It is used for getting going again after tracking is lost, finding the part again from nothing. It is used for joining two scans with no idea how the camera moved. And it is used for checking an ICP result; if ICP from the previous pose and global registration disagree, one of them is wrong, and the arm should not grip yet.

Global registration also fails in predictable ways. If the part has few distinct shapes, such as a flat box or a ball, you see few RANSAC inliers and a pose that changes from run to run. Instead, use colour or texture, or a learned pose model. If the normal and feature radius do not suit the scan, features are noise and almost no pairs are right. You should set the normal radius to a few times the point spacing, and the feature radius to a few times that. If the part is a small share of a cluttered scan, RANSAC might fit the move to the table or a neighbouring part. Cut the part out of the scan first, for example with clustering. If the part is symmetric, the pose flips between look-alike turns. You must accept the ambiguity or use a marking that breaks the symmetry.

The next section covers the libraries that provide ICP. You do not have to write any of this yourself, because ICP is part of every major point cloud library. Open3D provides it in Python and C plus plus, and also has coloured ICP and global registration with RANSAC on features. The Point Cloud Library, or PCL, provides it in C plus plus, including a generalised version that models each point as a small patch of surface. OpenCV provides the point pair feature method and ICP in its surface matching module. Trimesh provides a small and easy version in Python that aligns points to a mesh. Finally, the Eigen library in C plus plus provides just the best-move step, for writing your own ICP.

The next section explains why you would choose ICP, and what it costs. ICP moves a model point set onto a scanned point set by pairing closest points and fitting a rigid move, over and over. So it turns a rough pose into an exact one, often to within a millimetre or a degree, using only the shape of the part.

The obvious alternative is to try every pose, checking every turn in steps of one degree and every shift in steps of one millimetre, and keeping the pose with the smallest gap. In 3D that is millions of poses, each checked against the whole scan, which is far too slow for a robot. The second alternative is to match distinctive features, such as corners, between the model and the scan, and fit the pose to those matches with RANSAC. Feature matching does not need a first guess, which is its great advantage, but it is less exact, because it uses only a few points. That is why the usual answer is to use both: features for the first guess, then ICP to make it exact.

ICP costs you several things, starting with a first guess. Because it gives a confident wrong answer when that guess is poor, you must check the final gap. It also needs a model of the part, which means a CAD file or an earlier scan. And it needs a clean scan, with the table and other objects cut away. Because it runs a nearest-neighbour search for every model point in every round, it needs downsampled clouds to run at camera rate. Finally, it cannot tell apart the poses that a symmetric part makes look the same.

The next section covers the learned alternative. Networks can give an object's pose directly from a picture. Models such as MegaPose and FoundationPose need only the part's CAD model, or for FoundationPose a few photos of it. Because they cope with clutter and need no first guess, they can take the place of the 3D features and RANSAC. Once the part has been found, FoundationPose can also follow its pose from frame to frame, which is the job ICP does from the last pose. Then there are networks that read point clouds, which can learn their own 3D features in place of hand-written ones. But a pose model needs a graphics processor, and it gives no warning when it is wrong. So ICP is still run after it, and ICP's final gap is the check. That is why ICP alone still wins when a first guess is already at hand, such as the last frame's pose or a part in a fixed tray.

The final section explains how to use ICP in Python. It shows the Open3D call, because ICP is a case where the whole algorithm is one function and the difficulty has moved entirely into its arguments.

The code loads a stored model of a part and a scan of the real part, thins both, and then runs ICP. It thins both to one point per 3 millimetre cube, which makes each round faster and the distances more even. It then estimates normals on the scan. It creates a first guess, perhaps from a detector or a fixture. Then it calls the Open3D registration ICP function. It passes the model, the scan, a maximum correspondence distance to ignore pairs further apart, the first guess, the estimation method set to point-to-plane, and the convergence criteria set to a maximum of 50 iterations. Finally, it prints the fitness and the inlier root mean square error to see how much overlapped and how well it fitted, and it extracts the final transformation.

The library does all the loop steps for you. Every round it runs the nearest-neighbour search, throws away the pairs that are further apart than the maximum correspondence distance, computes the best rigid move for the pairs that are left, applies it, and stops when the improvement falls below a limit or when it has done the maximum number of rounds. Choosing the point-to-plane variant usually converges in fewer rounds on surfaces, which is why the code estimates normals on the scan first.

What you still write yourself is the first guess and the verdict. ICP has no way to find a pose, only to improve one, so the first guess comes from somewhere else, like the middle of a cluster, a detected marker, or global registration. You also write the check, because the function always returns a transform and never raises an error when the answer is nonsense. The two numbers to check are fitness, which is the fraction of model points that found a partner and which should be near 1 for a part you can see fully, and the inlier error, which should come out near your camera's noise rather than several times larger.

What you have to decide or measure is three physical numbers. The voxel size is how coarse to thin the clouds, and 2 to 5 millimetres is usual for a table-top part, because thinning speeds up every round and stops the dense parts of the scan from dominating. The maximum correspondence distance is the pair-rejection distance and it is the argument that matters most. You set it to a few times the depth camera's noise, and start it larger when the first guess is poor and shrink it once the model is close. Finally, the maximum iteration count is a time budget rather than an accuracy setting, since a run that has not converged in 50 rounds is usually one whose first guess was wrong.
