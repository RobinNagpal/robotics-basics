The title of this page is Visibility and next-best-view. It explains two linked techniques. The first is visibility, which means working out what a camera can see from a given place, and what is hidden behind something. The second is next-best-view planning, which means choosing where to put the camera next. This is done so that it sees as much as possible of what the robot does not know yet. 

The page answers four questions. The first two are about the technique itself: how do you test whether one object hides another, and how do you score a place to look from? Then come the practical ones: where does a robot arm use these, and when is a fixed list of views the better choice?

This assumes you know what a camera pose is, at the level of the pinhole camera model, and you only need basic geometry involving a straight line and a circle. The page focuses on the technique itself, meaning the test and the loop that uses it, rather than the physical camera timings.

The idea can be stated in one sentence. A camera sees a point only if the straight line from the camera to that point passes through nothing on the way. To choose where to look next, test that line for every point you still know nothing about, from every place the camera could go, and then go to the place that would show you the most.

An everyday example shows this without a robot. Imagine looking for your keys on a crowded shelf. From where you stand, a tall vase hides a patch of the shelf behind it. You do not know if the keys are there, so you think about where to move. Stepping to the left would show you the patch behind the vase. Standing on a chair would show the whole shelf, but the chair is in the next room. So you step to the left, because it shows you the most for a place you can actually get to. Then you look again and decide again. That everyday choice is the whole technique. The rest of the page makes each part exact.

Before counting anything, we need to define lines of sight and shadows. A line of sight, also called a sight line or a ray, is the straight line from the camera's centre to a point in the scene. Light travels along it, so if an object sits across that line, the point is occluded, meaning hidden. Each object therefore hides a region behind it, called its shadow, or occlusion shadow. It is the same shape a real shadow would be if the camera were a lamp, and the camera cannot see anything inside it.

To illustrate this, the page describes a scene with a row of four upright cylinders on a table. There are two bottles and two cups. The bottles are tall, at 30 and 26 centimetres, while the cups are shorter, at 10 and 8 centimetres. 

A diagram shows this row from the side, with the shadows marked in grey. When the camera is at the side of the table and lower than the first bottle, that bottle's shadow covers everything behind it, hiding both cups and the second bottle. As a result, 55 per cent of the free space is hidden, and the camera would report seeing only one bottle. However, when the camera is moved high above the middle of the table, looking down, each bottle casts only a thin shadow leaning away from the camera. From up here, only 11 per cent of the free space is hidden, and the camera can see the tops of almost everything.

Two things follow from this. First, a tall object close to the camera hides far more than a short object further away. Second, the same scene can be almost invisible from one place and almost fully visible from another, which is why choosing where to look is worth doing.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step. The technique has two layers. The lower layer is a test asking if one point is visible from one camera position. The upper layer is a loop that uses this test many times to score camera places and pick the best.

Starting with the lower layer, many objects on a table are close to upright cylinders, like bottles or cans. An upright cylinder has a simple, exact test using a formula, which is called an analytic test. You can write every point on the sight line as the camera's position, plus a number 't' multiplied by the difference between the target and the camera. The number 't' says how far along the line the point is. At 't' equals 0, the point is at the camera. At 't' equals 1, it is at the target.

A point is inside a solid upright cylinder when two things are true at the same time. First, seen from above, ignoring height, it is inside the circle. Putting the point's formula into the circle's equation gives a quadratic equation in 't'. Its two answers are where the line enters and leaves the circle. Second, seen from the side, ignoring the circle, the point's height must be between the table and the cylinder's top. This gives another stretch of 't'. 

Each test gives a stretch of 't', called an interval. The cylinder blocks the sight line only if these two intervals overlap, and that overlap must happen between 't' equals 0 and 't' equals 1. It is tempting to test only from above, or only from the side, but both are wrong on their own. A line might cross a cup's circle but pass high over its top, or cross a bottle's height but pass beside it. 

A worked example shows these traps using two sight lines. A red sight line goes to the top of the first cup. When tested against the first bottle, the line is inside the circle from 66 to 76 per cent of the way along. It is also below the bottle's top during that same stretch. Because the intervals overlap between the camera and the target, the bottle blocks the red line. 

A green sight line goes to the top of the second bottle. Against the first cup, it enters the circle, but only when it is far above the cup's top, so it is not blocked. Against the first bottle, it is below the top the whole way, but it never enters the bottle's circle, so it is not blocked there either. This test costs one square root and a few multiplications, meaning a program can test thousands of sight lines in well under a millisecond.

With this cheap test, the upper layer can run it everywhere. This loop is the next-best-view planning. It follows six steps. First, keep a record of what you do not know yet, usually a grid of small cells marked as unknown. Second, list the candidate poses where the camera could go. Third, remove the ones the arm cannot reach, using distance or inverse kinematics. Fourth, predict what each remaining candidate would reveal by counting how many unknown cells are inside the picture and not hidden. This count is the candidate's gain. Fifth, take the best candidate, move the camera there, take a picture, and mark what it saw as known. Finally, repeat from step four, because the gains have changed. You stop when the budget runs out or the gains are too small.

The recounting in step six is crucial. A view that scored well in round one might gain almost nothing in round two, because the first view already saw that area. 

A worked example demonstrates this with eleven candidate views arranged in a half-circle around the table. The budget is three views. First, three candidates on the far right are removed because the arm cannot reach them. In round one, the view at 105 degrees sees the most, revealing 218 unknown cells, so the robot takes it. In round two, views that looked strong before now offer very little, because they look at the same space. Instead, a view at 135 degrees wins by adding 129 cells, seeing into a shadow the first view missed. In round three, a low view from the left adds 72 more cells. After three views, the right-hand end is still unknown because the views that could see it were out of reach. The loop tells you this plainly, meaning the robot must either move its base or accept the unknown space.

The page provides pseudocode for this loop. It defines a function that checks if the circle and height intervals overlap. Another function checks if a cell is in the camera's picture and not blocked by any object. The main loop filters for reachable candidates, calculates the gain for each, picks the one that sees the most, updates the map, and repeats until the budget is hit. On a real robot, the map is updated from the real picture, so if a new object is discovered, it will cast its own shadow in the next round.

Sometimes, you need to pick a fixed set of views all at once, rather than one by one. This is a different problem called set cover. The same greedy rule solves it well: you take the view that adds the most, then the next, and so on.

The next section covers where this is used on a robot arm. The visibility test and the full loop are used in different tasks. The test is used to throw away useless camera poses early, before motion planning. It is also used offline to pick a small fixed set of views for a cell. The full next-best-view loop is used for looking into a bin or a shelf, finding a hidden object, building a model of an unfamiliar object, checking a grasp before committing, and keeping the camera's view clear of the arm itself while moving.

These uses divide along a clear line, showing where the technique is useful and where it is not. The visibility test is exact for simple shapes. But the full loop is only as good as its record of the unknown and its candidate list. A table of common problems explains that if a task only needs one or two views, the loop wastes time, and you should use a fixed set chosen offline instead. If objects are complex, you can wrap them in larger cylinders or test against meshes. If the best view is out of reach, you must check reach before scoring. If the scene changes, you have to re-mark cells as unknown. The main takeaway is that the visibility test is almost always worth having, while the full loop is only for when the robot genuinely does not know what is in front of it.

Because the cylinder test and the loop are just a few dozen lines of code, no library is needed for them. However, libraries are useful for ray casting against complex shapes or managing the grid of unknown cells. Open3D and trimesh in Python, or MuJoCo and PyBullet, can cast rays against triangle meshes or simulated scenes. OctoMap and PCL in C++ provide standard grids for free, occupied, and unknown space. MoveIt 2 can ask a planner to keep a sensor's view clear.

So, why use this, and what does it cost? The technique replaces guessing with counting. Without it, a robot drives to a view only to find the target is hidden. With it, the program knows before the arm moves. A wasted move costs seconds, while the test costs microseconds. The obvious alternative is to render each candidate view like a game engine, but that is far slower and needs a full 3D model. Another alternative is a fixed list of views, which is actually better for most known tasks. The costs of the loop are that you must model objects, keep an updated map of unknown space, accept a variable cycle time, and rely entirely on the quality of your candidate list.

There is no learned model that replaces the exact, microsecond sight-line test. The nearest learned alternative is shape completion, which guesses the hidden back of an object from one picture. This wins when the camera cannot get around an object, or when moving costs too much time. However, the back it gives is invented, not measured. The rule is to use completion when you cannot look, and to look when you can afford it. If a learned model has low confidence, that is the signal to look again, and this technique chooses where.

Finally, the page explains how to use this in Python. While the simple cylinder test can be written by hand, ray casting against real shapes requires a library. A Python example uses Open3D. It sets up a bottle as an obstacle and a target point, like a screw you need to see. It then lists three candidate camera positions. The rays are defined by a starting point and a direction. If you leave the direction as the full vector from the camera to the target, then 't' equals 1 lands exactly on the target. 

When the library tests these rays, it returns whether the target is hidden, and how far along the ray the hit happened. For example, it might say the bottle blocks the line 37 per cent of the way from the camera to the screw. The library handles the fast geometry against real meshes, which is necessary for complex shapes like a mug with a handle. 

However, you still have to write the next-best-view loop yourself. You must generate the candidate views, score them by what they reveal, subtract the movement cost, and pick the winner. You also have to track the unknown space, check that views are reachable, manage the coordinate frames so you don't mix camera and table coordinates, and account for the camera's actual field of view.
