Sensor streams: time stamps, filters and thresholds.

This page explains how to work with a stream of sensor readings. This means readings that arrive one after another, many times a second, for as long as the arm runs. It answers the five questions that come up most often once you write a program against such a stream. How do you pair a camera picture with the joint angles of the same moment? What goes wrong when two devices keep their own clocks? How do you smooth a noisy reading, and what does smoothing cost? How do you get a rate of change, such as how fast a force is rising, out of noisy readings? And how do you turn a reading into a yes-or-no flag, such as asking if the force is too high, without the flag flickering on and off?

The page is for a reader who already knows what a joint angle, a wrist camera, and a force sensor are. It also helps to have read the page on the Kalman filter, because that page handles one stream in a more careful way than anything here does. Every number mentioned comes from a real run of a script, rather than from an estimate. Two running examples are used throughout, and both come from an ordinary arm. The first is a wrist camera, which is a camera fixed to the arm near the gripper, so that it moves whenever the arm moves. The second is a force-torque trace, which is the stream of readings from a force-torque sensor at the wrist. That means a stream of numbers saying how hard the tool is pushing on something.

The first part of the page explains what these techniques answer. A robot arm has many sensors, and each one sends its own stream at its own rate. The joint encoders report the joint angles perhaps a hundred or a thousand times a second. A wrist camera is much slower, sending only thirty pictures a second, while a force-torque sensor is faster again at five hundred or a thousand readings a second.

Because those rates differ, and because no reading is perfect, these streams cause four everyday problems. First, the streams do not line up, because a picture is taken between two joint readings rather than at the same instant as one. Second, every reading is late, so a picture arrives at the computer some tens of milliseconds after it was taken. Third, every reading is noisy, since a force sensor's reading shakes a little even when nothing touches it, and now and then it jumps for no real reason. And fourth, decisions made from noisy readings flicker, so a check such as asking if the force is above ten newtons can switch between yes and no hundreds of times while the real force sits near ten.

The techniques used to solve these problems are small, and none of them needs a model of the arm, but almost every arm program uses all of them. Getting them wrong causes some of the hardest bugs to find, because nothing crashes. Instead, the arm just places things a few millimetres off, or stops for no visible reason.

Since the four problems just mentioned all come from time, the core idea is stated in terms of time as well. You should treat each reading as a value together with the moment it was true, combine readings by their moments rather than by when they arrived, and smooth and threshold them in ways whose delay you have measured.

Here is an everyday example of the same rule, taken from outside robotics. You take a photo from a moving train, while a friend on the train writes down where the train is every second. Later you want to know where each photo was taken, so you must match each photo to one of the positions your friend wrote down. You do not use the position written down at the moment you looked at the photo, because that moment has nothing to do with the photo. Instead, you use the position at the time printed on the photo. So if the photo says ten fifteen and three and a half seconds, you take the positions at three seconds and four seconds and pick the point halfway between them. And if your camera's clock is a minute fast, you must correct for that first, or every photo is placed a minute's travel down the line.

The next section breaks down how this works, step by step. The rule starts from the moment a reading was true, so the first thing every stream needs is a way of writing that moment down. A time stamp is the moment a reading was true, recorded together with the reading itself. In the Robot Operating System, or ROS, most messages carry a header. This header holds the time stamp and the name of the frame the numbers are measured in. The rule about stamps is short, and it decides everything that follows: the time stamp should say when the reading was true, not when it arrived. For a picture, that is the moment the camera's sensor was exposed to light, and for a force reading, it is the moment the sensor sampled. So a program that uses the time its callback ran, instead of the stamp, is using the arrival time.

Once every reading carries a stamp, two streams can be paired by those stamps. The page includes a diagram showing the two streams of the running example on one time line. The joint readings arrive every ten milliseconds, while the camera pictures arrive every thirty-three milliseconds. The arm moves the tool one hundred millimetres in zero point six seconds along a smooth path, so at the moment of the marked picture, the tool is moving at two hundred and sixty-one millimetres per second.

The picture has the time stamp zero point two eight three three seconds, which falls between the joint readings at zero point two eight seconds and zero point two nine seconds. There are three ways to get the arm's pose for it.

First, you could read the pose now, when the picture arrives. The picture arrives forty-five milliseconds after it was taken, so by then the tool is at fifty-seven point four millimetres although it was really at forty-five point six millimetres. That means the pose is eleven point seven millimetres wrong, and this is the most common mistake of the three.

Second, you could take the nearest joint reading. The nearest one is at zero point two eight seconds, which is three point three milliseconds before the picture, and it gives forty-four point eight millimetres, so the error is zero point nine millimetres. At one hundred readings a second, the nearest reading is never more than five milliseconds away, so at this speed the error is never more than about one point three millimetres.

Third, you could interpolate at the picture's stamp. Interpolate means working out a value between two known ones, here by drawing a straight line between the readings at zero point two eight seconds and zero point two nine seconds. It gives forty-five point six millimetres, which is the same as the true position to one decimal place.

The second method is called approximate-time matching, because it pairs each message with the message from the other stream whose stamp is closest. ROS provides it as Approximate Time Synchronizer in the message filters package. You give it a slop, which is the largest difference between two stamps that still counts as a pair. That is why the method suits two streams at similar rates, such as a colour picture and a depth picture.

The third method suits a slow stream against a fast one instead, such as pictures against joint angles. You keep the last second or so of joint readings in a buffer. Then, when a picture arrives, you look up the two readings either side of its stamp and interpolate between them. This is exactly what the ROS transform library, tf2, does when you ask it for the pose at a given time.

Pairing by stamps only works when the stamps themselves are right, and two different delays hide in a stream, so each one needs its own fix. Latency is the time between a reading being true and it arriving, and for the camera in the example, it is forty-five milliseconds. It comes from the exposure, the transfer over the cable, and the driver's work. Latency on its own is harmless if the stamp is right, because you look up the pose at the stamp. So it only matters when you act on the result, since an arm steering by a picture is always steering by the past.

A clock offset is a difference between two clocks. Such an offset appears because the camera may stamp its pictures with its own internal clock, while the joint readings are stamped with the computer's clock. If the camera's clock runs twelve milliseconds ahead, every picture looks twelve milliseconds later than it was. So in the example, pairing by the uncorrected stamp gives the pose at zero point two seven one three seconds instead of zero point two eight three three seconds. That is forty-two point five millimetres instead of forty-five point six millimetres, which is three point one millimetres wrong. Nothing warns you, because the pairs look perfectly matched.

Clocks also drift, which means that two clocks agreeing at start-up slowly move apart, because no two crystals tick at exactly the same rate. So an offset measured once is not enough for a long run.

Three fixes are used against these clock problems, starting with the most reliable. First, put every device on one clock. Computers are kept in step with the Precision Time Protocol, which some cameras and arm controllers also support, or with the Network Time Protocol through a program such as chrony. Second, let the driver translate, because some camera drivers convert the camera's own time into the computer's time. The Intel RealSense library, for example, has a global time setting that does this. Finally, you can measure the offset yourself by moving the arm at a steady speed past a marker the camera can see. Then find the time shift that makes the marker's position in the pictures agree best with the arm's recorded position. That shift is the offset, together with any stamping delay in the driver.

Correct stamps put every reading at the right moment, but they do nothing about noise, so the next step is to smooth each stream. A filter here is a small calculation that takes the stream of raw readings and gives back a smoother stream. Filters that let slow changes through and damp fast ones are called low-pass filters.

The page shows a diagram of a noisy force trace, where the sensor sends five hundred readings a second with zero point two five newtons of noise. The tool moves freely until half a second and then touches a surface, so the force rises at twenty newtons per second to six newtons and stays there. Three single-reading spikes of plus four newtons stand for electrical glitches. Three filters run on these same raw readings.

First, a moving average reports the average of the last twenty-five readings, which is fifty milliseconds of readings. On the quiet part before contact, it cuts the spread of the readings from zero point two five newtons to zero point zero four newtons. A spike of four newtons becomes a bump of about zero point three newtons, because the spike is shared among twenty-five readings.

Second, an exponential filter keeps one number, the estimate, and each new reading moves the estimate a fixed fraction of the way towards itself. Here that fraction is zero point zero seven seven, which is the usual match for a twenty-five-reading average. That is why it behaves much like the moving average, with a spread of zero point zero five five newtons and a spike bump of about zero point four newtons. It needs no buffer of past readings, which is why it is popular on small processors.

Third, a median filter reports the middle value of the last twenty-five readings once they are sorted. It has a spread of zero point zero four newtons, and the spikes almost vanish, because the worst bump is zero point two newtons, which is the noise itself. A single wild reading only moves the median by one place in the sorted list.

Every filter is late, however. The diagram shows the moment each output first crosses three newtons. The true force crosses at zero point six five zero seconds, while the moving average and the exponential filter cross twenty-six milliseconds late. The median crosses thirty milliseconds late. A causal moving average of a given number of readings is late by about half its window, which here is twelve readings or twenty-four milliseconds. The word causal means that it uses only readings that have already arrived, as any filter on a running robot must.

The raw readings are not late, but they cannot be used either. A three-newton threshold on the raw readings would have fired at zero point two seconds on the first spike, while the tool was still in free air. This is the trade that runs through every filter, since a longer window gives a smoother signal and a later one. You must choose the window from the noise you must remove and the delay you can afford, and measure both.

Smoothing gives a steadier value, but often the useful number is how fast a reading changes rather than the reading itself. How fast the force rises tells you how stiff the surface is, and how fast a joint angle changes is the joint's speed. This rate of change is called the derivative, or the slope.

The obvious way to find it is to subtract neighbouring readings and divide by the time between them, but a diagram shows why that fails. It uses the rising part of the force trace, where the true slope is twenty newtons per second. The readings are only two milliseconds apart, so noise of zero point two five newtons divided by zero point zero zero two seconds is already one hundred and twenty-five newtons per second. As a result, the neighbour differences swing wildly, with a spread of one hundred and sixty-four newtons per second around a true value of twenty. Their average is right, but no single value is any use.

Two better ways use a window of twenty-five readings, which is fifty milliseconds long. The first is the difference across the window, which subtracts the reading twenty-five places back. The noise is the same, but the time between the two readings is twenty-five times longer, bringing the spread down to seven point one newtons per second. The second is a least-squares line through all twenty-five readings. This uses every reading in the window rather than just the two ends. Its slope has a spread of only two point seven newtons per second, and its average is nineteen point eight newtons per second.

Both methods are late by about half the window, twenty-four milliseconds, for the same reason as the filters. The Savitzky-Golay filter in the SciPy library fits a small curve in a sliding window and can return its slope, which is the same idea as the least-squares line.

Once a value is smooth and its slope is known, the last step is to turn it into a decision. A threshold does that by turning a reading into a flag, so that a statement like "the force is above ten newtons" is either true or false. The trouble comes when the real value sits near the threshold, because noise then pushes each reading to one side or the other at random.

A diagram shows a force that climbs to nine point eight newtons, creeps up to ten, rises to eleven point five, then falls back to six point five. The noise is zero point three newtons and the limit is ten newtons.

With a single threshold, the flag is on whenever the reading is above ten newtons, and it changes one hundred and forty-two times in two seconds. A program that stops the arm when the flag goes on, and restarts it when the flag goes off, would jerk the arm about.

The first fix is hysteresis. The flag turns on above ten newtons but only turns off below nine newtons. Hysteresis means that the switch-on level and the switch-off level are different, so the flag remembers which side it came from. The gap between the two levels must be wider than the noise. Here it changes four times, since the gap of one newton is only about three times the noise, so one dip early on still gets through. A wider gap would remove that dip.

The second fix is debouncing. The flag only changes after ten readings in a row, which is twenty milliseconds, agree with the new state. Here the flag changes twice, turning on and later turning off. That is because it ignored the early touches of ten newtons, when the real force was still nine point eight newtons, and waited until the real force was clearly above the limit.

The two tricks do different jobs, and they are often used together. Hysteresis stops the flag chattering once it has changed, while debouncing stops a brief excursion from changing it at all. But debouncing adds a delay, because here the flag waits twenty milliseconds before acting. For a safety stop that delay must be counted, and it should be short. Instead, for a slow decision such as checking if a part is seated, a longer wait is fine.

The next section lists where these techniques are used on a robot arm, because almost every program reads a stream. They appear when placing what the wrist camera sees, because a detection only becomes a point in the room through the arm's pose at the picture's time. They are used when stitching several views from a swept depth camera, where wrong pairing smears the surface. They are used in guarded moves, where an arm creeps towards a surface and stops when the filtered force passes a threshold. A long median filter over a still arm is used to weigh a held object without measuring the arm's own shaking. Force limits use a flag with hysteresis and a short debounce to stop a press without flickering. Joint speeds are calculated as a filtered slope from the encoders. When recording demonstrations for learning, streams must be paired correctly so the policy doesn't learn that the arm reacts before it sees. Finally, gripper checks use a debounced slope near zero on the finger position to confirm the fingers have stopped closing.

These techniques are the right tool whenever one stream must be read at another stream's times, or whenever a noisy stream must be turned into a steady number or a clean flag. They are cheap, need no model of the arm, and are easy to test on a recording.

However, they can fail, and the page provides a table of common problems and what to use instead. If objects land a few millimetres off when the arm moves fast, the pose is likely being read when the picture arrives, so you must look up the pose at the picture's stamp. If you see the same error even when using stamps, the driver might be stamping pictures on arrival, so you should measure the stamping delay with a moving marker and subtract it. If moving views show a constant offset that slowly grows, the clocks are drifting, and you need one clock for everything, like PTP. If pairs are dropped because there is no pose within the slop, you need a longer pose buffer or a higher joint rate. If contact is detected late, your filter window is too long, and you should use a shorter window for detection. If a mean filter jumps because of a spike, use a median filter instead. If a speed swings wildly because it is calculated from neighbouring readings, use a least-squares slope over a window. If a flag chatters, add hysteresis or debouncing. And if a value really changes in steps but the filter smooths them into a ramp, you should use a Kalman filter that is told when the arm moves, or reset the filter at known events.

You rarely have to write these techniques yourself. The page lists well-known libraries that provide them. In ROS 2, the message filters package provides Approximate Time Synchronizer to pair messages with close stamps, and tf2 provides lookup transform to get the pose of any frame at a given time. In Python, NumPy provides interpolation, moving averages, medians, and least-squares slopes. SciPy provides proper low-pass filter design and the Savitzky-Golay filter for smoothing and slopes. For C++ ROS nodes, the filters package and the control toolbox provide low-pass filters that can run directly in a control loop. Finally, command line tools like chrony and ptp4l keep the computers' clocks in step.

Whichever libraries you use, they all follow the same rules: pair readings by their stamps, correct the clocks, smooth them with a known delay, take slopes over a window, and put hysteresis and debouncing on every threshold.

The obvious alternative for pairing is to use the latest pose, which is simpler, but in the running example it put the tool eleven point seven millimetres from where it was. The obvious alternative for smoothing is a Kalman filter, which gives a smoother value and a speed, but it needs a model of how the value changes and its settings are harder to choose. That is why most arms use simple filters for force and joint signals, and a Kalman filter for tracking things the camera sees. The obvious alternative for a flag is a single threshold, but it chatters when the value lingers near the limit.

These techniques do carry costs. The first is delay, as every filter and slope window adds about half its window, moving every threshold later. A pose buffer uses memory, and clock synchronisation is work to set up. Debouncing trades chatter for a fixed wait that must be counted. And none of these tricks can fix a stream that is wrong in a steady way, because the median of a drifting sensor drifts with it.

It is worth asking what a learned model would do instead. There is no learned model that replaces time stamps and clock correction, because pairing readings by time is bookkeeping with one right answer. For thresholds, however, learned models can read a short window of readings. A learned collision detector can tell a gentle bump from a fast movement by the shape of the torque gap over time, which one fixed stop line cannot. A slip model can call a slip from the pattern of the force signal, where a fixed rule needs a friction number that changes when the surface is wet. Both need recorded examples and retraining when the gripper or payload changes. A learned detector adds to the certified safety function and never replaces it, so the filters, hysteresis, and debouncing stay underneath as the check that always works. When the job is to name the contact state from a few numbers per window, a decision tree or forest can learn the thresholds from labelled windows, and a hidden Markov model can follow the states over time so a single noisy reading does not flip the answer.

The final section shows how to use these tools in Python. The first problem, pairing streams, is solved inside a ROS 2 node with the message filters package. You set up an Approximate Time Synchronizer with your subscriptions. Instead of one callback per topic, you get one callback that is only called when messages from both topics have close time stamps.

The second problem, smoothing and taking a slope, is plain arithmetic on arrays, so it belongs to SciPy and NumPy. The Savitzky-Golay filter from SciPy fits a short polynomial to a sliding window of readings. Calling it once gives a smoothed value, and calling it again while asking for the first derivative gives the slope. NumPy's interpolate function does the linear interpolation between the two joint messages that straddle the picture's time stamp.

The libraries do the bookkeeping for you, but you still have to write the decision about what to do when the pairing fails. A missing camera message means your callback simply never runs, and only you can decide whether that should stop the arm. You also have to publish honest time stamps in the first place, using the time of the measurement rather than the current time. Finally, you have to decide on the slop and the window length. The slop has to be larger than the real jitter between your two streams and smaller than the time in which the arm moves enough to matter, so you should measure the jitter from a recording. For the filter, a longer window is smoother but lags further behind, and the lag is about half the window. You also have to pass the real time between readings to the Savitzky-Golay filter, because it has no way to know your sample rate and will silently return a slope in the wrong units if you leave it out.
