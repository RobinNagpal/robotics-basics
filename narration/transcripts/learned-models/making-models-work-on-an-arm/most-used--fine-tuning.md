Fine-tuning. This page answers one question. You have downloaded a model that someone else trained, and it almost does what your robot needs, so how do you teach it the last part using only a small amount of your own data?

The page explains the three usual ways to do this, and they differ mainly in how much of the model you allow training to change. You can freeze most of the model and train only a new last layer, or you can add a small side path called LoRA and train only that, or you can train every number in the model. For each of the three, the page says how much data and how much memory it needs, what the model can forget along the way, and how to choose between them.

It is for a reader who has read the chapter so far, because it uses words that the earlier pages introduced. You should know what a layer, a backbone, a head, and a parameter are, from the page inside a neural network. You should also know what pretraining is, from the page about where the data comes from. That page introduced fine-tuning in a single section, whereas this page goes inside it and explains how the work is actually done.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. Fine-tuning means taking a model that was already trained on a large, general pile of data, and training it a little more on a small pile of your own data, so that it does your job.

Here is an everyday example. When an experienced lorry driver joins a new company, the driver does not relearn how to steer or brake, and instead learns only the new routes and where the loading bays are. In the same way, a pretrained seeing model already knows about edges, surfaces, and shapes, so it only needs to learn what your own parts look like.

The model you start from is called the pretrained model. Its numbers are the pretrained weights. Most pretrained models are split into a backbone, which is the large first part that turns the input into useful numbers, and a head, which is the small last part that turns those numbers into the answer. The seeing models overview describes this split for pictures.

The next part explains three ways to fine-tune. The three ways differ in which numbers training is allowed to change, and that single difference is what decides their cost. A number that training may not change is called frozen.

The page shows a diagram of the same small network three times, to illustrate these ways. In the diagrams, grey parts are frozen and red parts are trained.

First is way one, which is to freeze the backbone and train a new head. You keep every number in the backbone exactly as it was, and then you throw away the old head and put a new, small head in its place, so that only the new head is trained. People also call this a linear probe when the new head is a single layer. In the diagram, only the head is red. It is the cheapest of the three ways, because so few numbers change, and it works whenever the backbone already turns your pictures into numbers that separate your answers well.

Second is way two, which is to add a small side path and train only that. You keep every original number frozen, but you add a few small extra layers next to or inside the old ones, and then train only those together with a new head. In the diagram, a small side path and the head are red. These extra layers are called adapters, and the most used kind of adapter is LoRA, which stands for low-rank adaptation and is explained in the next section. This way is useful because it lets the backbone's behaviour change a little while still training very few numbers.

Finally, way three is full fine-tuning. Every number in the model may change, which makes this the most flexible of the three ways. In the diagram, everything is red. However, that flexibility has a price, because it needs the most memory and it is the most likely to forget what the model knew before.

There are mixtures of these three ways as well, and a common one is to freeze the first layers of the backbone while training all the rest. This works because the first layers find edges and colours, which look the same for every job, so they rarely need to change at all.

Moving on to LoRA in plain words, with real counts. A layer in a network holds its weights in a grid of numbers. A layer that turns 4,096 numbers into 4,096 numbers has a grid of 4,096 rows and 4,096 columns. That is over 16 million numbers. The large language models inside vision-language-action models, such as the one inside OpenVLA, are built from many layers of about this size.

Full fine-tuning would change all 16 million numbers in this one grid, whereas LoRA leaves the grid frozen and adds a separate change to it, building that change from two thin grids multiplied together. Grid A has a few rows and 4,096 columns. Grid B has 4,096 rows and a few columns. B multiplied by A gives a full 4,096 by 4,096 grid of changes.

The "few" is called the rank, so a rank of 32 means that grid A has 32 rows and grid B has 32 columns. Together the two thin grids then hold 32 times the sum of 4,096 and 4,096, which equals 262,144 numbers. That is only 1.56 percent of the full grid. The layer works out its answer using the frozen grid plus the change, which means the original numbers are never touched.

The page shows a diagram of a large 4,096 by 4,096 grid next to the two thin grids that LoRA trains. Beside it is a bar chart and a table showing how the trained share grows as the rank increases from 4 to 64. For a rank of 4, LoRA trains about 32,000 numbers, which is 0.2 percent of the full grid. This rises steadily until a rank of 64, where it trains about 524,000 numbers, which is 3.12 percent of the full grid.

Why does such a thin change work? The authors of LoRA found that the change which fine-tuning makes to a large pretrained model usually has a simple shape, and a simple change can be written well with only two thin grids. You do lose a little flexibility this way, but in return you train and store far fewer numbers.

LoRA has two further benefits on a robot. First, because the side path is a small separate file, you can keep one pretrained model together with several small LoRA files, one for each task, and load only the one you need. Second, you can switch the side path off, and the model is then exactly the pretrained model again.

OpenVLA's own LoRA script uses a rank of 32 and adds LoRA to every fully connected layer. The LoRA paper reports that, for the 175-billion-parameter language model GPT-3, LoRA cut the number of trained numbers by 10,000 times and the graphics card memory by 3 times, compared with full fine-tuning.

The next section is a worked example showing 50 examples trained four ways. This example is a real run in numpy, in a diagram script. The network is tiny, so that the run takes seconds, but the steps are the same as for a large model.

The set-up is this. First, a pretrained network reads 16 numbers. Its backbone has one layer of 32 neurons, which is 544 numbers. Its old head gives 4 answers for an old job. Second, the new job needs 1 answer. It uses features close to, but not the same as, the ones the backbone already makes. In the script, the new job is built from the same backbone with a small change added. Third, there are only 50 examples of the new job to learn from. That is like having 50 demonstrations of a new task. Finally, the network is trained four ways, for 3,000 steps each, and tested on 2,000 new examples it never saw.

The error is the mean squared error, which is the average of the squared difference between the answer and the right answer, so a lower number is better. To give the numbers a scale, a model that always guessed the average answer would score 0.487.

The page includes a table giving the results for the four ways of training. Freezing the backbone and training a new head trained only 32 numbers. It achieved an error of 0.041 on the new job, and its error on the old job remained at zero. Using LoRA with a rank of 1 plus a new head trained 80 numbers. It achieved an error of 0.045 on the new job. Its error on the old job was 0.049 with the side path on, but zero with it off. Full fine-tuning trained all 576 numbers. It had an error of 0.069 on the new job, and its error on the old job worsened to 0.098. Finally, training from scratch with no pretraining also trained all 576 numbers, but it performed poorly, with an error of 0.172 on the new job.

Four things show up from these results. First, starting from pretrained weights matters most. All three fine-tuning ways beat training from scratch by a wide margin. From scratch, 50 examples are not enough to learn good features. Second, with little data, training fewer numbers did better. Full fine-tuning learned the 50 examples perfectly, with a training error of zero, and still did worse on new examples than the two cheaper ways. It had enough freedom to learn the examples' noise as well as their pattern. This is called overfitting, as the page on how a model learns explains. Third, here the new job was close to the old one. So a new head on the frozen backbone was enough, and it did slightly better than LoRA. When the new job needs features the backbone does not make, the head alone cannot fit it, and LoRA or full fine-tuning pulls ahead. Finally, full fine-tuning damaged the old job. The next section is about this.

This is only one small run, so with another set-up the order of the first three rows can change. However, the first and the last of the four points above hold in general.

The next section covers forgetting. When full fine-tuning changes the backbone, it also changes the features that the old head relied on, so the old job gets worse. This is called catastrophic forgetting, although the word "catastrophic" is historical and the loss is often only partial, as it is here.

The page shows two panels of training curves. On the left, all fine-tuning ways reach a low error on the new job as the trial gets longer. On the right, the curves show the error on the old job during the same training. The line for freezing the backbone stays at zero, because freezing the backbone means the old job's features cannot change at all. The line for full fine-tuning jumps to 0.098 within 50 steps, which shows that full fine-tuning bent the backbone towards the new job straight away. The line for LoRA with its side path switched on damages the old job too, by 0.049. However, switching that side path off gives back the pretrained model exactly, with an error of zero.

Forgetting matters on a robot in two places. First, a vision-language-action model fine-tuned on your task can lose some of its general knowledge of objects and words, so it may follow your task well and then fail on a new instruction it would once have understood. The vision-language-action page describes how laboratories mix general data into training to reduce this. Second, a detector fine-tuned only on your five parts can stop finding people and hands, if it once found them, so if a safety check depends on those classes you should keep a separate model for them.

There are four usual defences against forgetting, and they can be combined. You can freeze more of the model, so that fewer of the old features are able to move. You can use LoRA and keep the pretrained model beside it, because the side path can then be switched off. You can mix some of the original kind of data into your fine-tuning data, so that the old job is still being practised. Finally, you can train for fewer steps with a small learning rate, which is the size of each step in training.

The next part explains how much data and how much memory you need.

Starting with data. The amount of data depends more on how far your job is from the pretrained job than on the way you fine-tune. A table gives the usual ranges. For a detector for your parts, people usually start with a few hundred labelled pictures, or 80 drawn pictures in a simple case. For a vision-language-action model for a new task, people start with tens to hundreds of demonstrations. When your data is at the small end of these ranges you should freeze more of the model, whereas at the large end you can afford to train more of it.

Moving to memory. Training needs far more memory than running a model. The graphics card must hold the model's weights. For every trained number it must also hold that number's gradient, which says which way to change it, and the extra numbers the optimiser keeps. The optimiser is the part of the training program that makes the changes. A widely used optimiser called Adam keeps two extra numbers for every trained number.

A rough count shows why full fine-tuning is so expensive. A common figure is about 16 bytes of memory per trained number, when training with Adam and a mix of 16-bit and 32-bit numbers. OpenVLA has about 7 billion parameters. Full fine-tuning would need about 112 gigabytes, before any pictures are loaded. No single graphics card holds that. With LoRA, only 1 to 2 percent of the numbers are trained, so the gradients and optimiser numbers become small. The frozen weights still have to be held. At 2 bytes each, 7 billion weights take about 14 gigabytes.

The real figures come from the projects themselves. The page shows a chart of memory requirements stated by the openpi and OpenVLA projects, with lines marking common graphics card sizes of 24 gigabytes and 80 gigabytes. Running the openpi models takes more than 8 gigabytes. LoRA fine-tuning for openpi takes more than 22.5 gigabytes, while full fine-tuning takes more than 70 gigabytes. For OpenVLA, LoRA fine-tuning with a smaller batch takes at least about 27 gigabytes, but with a batch of 16 it takes about 72 gigabytes. Full fine-tuning for OpenVLA requires a full node of eight A100 cards. A batch is the number of examples the training program looks at before it makes one change, and a smaller batch needs less memory, which is why OpenVLA's LoRA figure has two different memory sizes.

For a detector, memory is rarely the problem at all, because a small YOLO model fine-tunes on an ordinary processor with no graphics card whatsoever.

The next section gives two examples on a robot arm.

The first example is fine-tuning a detector on your parts. A cell sorts five kinds of machined bracket off a conveyor, however a detector trained on the COCO photo collection knows about cups and bottles and has never seen a bracket. First, you take a few hundred pictures of the brackets with the robot's own camera, in the cell's own light, with the parts in many positions. Second, you draw a box around each bracket and give it one of five names. Third, you start from a pretrained YOLO model and train it on these pictures. The backbone is not frozen by default in Ultralytics, so this is full fine-tuning of a small model. Ultralytics has a freeze training option that freezes the first layers if you have very few pictures. Finally, you test on pictures the model never saw, including awkward ones like parts touching, parts half out of the picture, or the light turned down. This works well because the new job is close to the pretrained job, since finding objects is the same skill either way and only the names and the shapes are new.

The second example is fine-tuning a vision-language-action model on 50 demonstrations. A lab wants a vision-language-action model to put a cup on a saucer, on its own arm. First, you record 50 demonstrations. A person drives the arm through the task with a leader arm or a hand controller, and the robot records the camera pictures, the joint angles, and the instruction "put the cup on the saucer". Second, you convert the recordings into the format that the model's scripts expect. Third, you run LoRA fine-tuning. This fits the memory of one rented card. Finally, you test on the real arm, with the cup in places the demonstrations did not use, and count successes out of, say, 20 tries. Fifty demonstrations is at the small end of the usual range, so it can be enough as long as the scene stays much like the demonstrations themselves. If the model fails once the cup moves, then the usual fix is more demonstrations with more variety in them, rather than a different way of fine-tuning.

The page then discusses where fine-tuning works and where it does not. The two examples above both worked, so it is worth saying plainly when fine-tuning does not. Fine-tuning works when the new job is close to what the model was pretrained on, which is why a detector pretrained on everyday photos adapts well to parts on a table, because both are colour pictures of solid objects.

It works badly in several cases. If the new data looks nothing like the pretraining data, such as giving depth images or X-rays to a colour-photo model, even full fine-tuning gives poor test results. Instead, you should pick a model pretrained on similar data, or collect much more data. If there is too little data for the number of trained numbers, you will see the training error near zero but the test error high. To fix this, freeze more, use LoRA with a small rank, or collect more data. If your test pictures are too like your training pictures, you will get good scores in testing but failures in the real cell. You should test on pictures from another day, another light, and new part positions. If the model forgets something it needs, a class or a skill that used to work will fail. You can use LoRA with the side path kept separate, or mix in general data. Finally, if the labels are inconsistent, the model will be unsure in exactly the cases where people disagreed. You have to fix the labels, because no training method fixes them for you.

The next section lists libraries and scripts. These are all real tools that you can download today.

First is Hugging Face PEFT, which is a Python library for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, meaning training a small part of a model. You describe the adapter, wrap the model, and it can tell you how many numbers will be trained. OpenVLA's LoRA script is built on it.

Second is OpenVLA, which ships scripts for LoRA fine-tuning and for full fine-tuning across several cards. Its documentation now also points to a newer recipe, called OFT, for better results.

Third is openpi, which fine-tunes the pi-zero and pi-zero-point-five models. You first run a script which measures the range of your robot's numbers, and then a training script with a training configuration. Its original version uses the JAX library, whereas its newer PyTorch version does not yet support LoRA training.

Fourth is Ultralytics, which fine-tunes YOLO detectors with one call. It has an option to freeze the first layers.

Finally, LeRobot trains and fine-tunes smaller robot policies, such as SmolVLA and ACT, and runs on a Mac.

The next part explains how to choose, and what each way costs. You should start with the cheapest way that could work, and move to the next only if the results show you need it.

First, freeze the backbone and train a new head when your data is small and your job is close to the pretrained job. It is fast, it cannot forget, and it needs little memory. It costs you flexibility: if the backbone's features do not separate your answers, no head can fix it.

Second, use LoRA or another adapter when a new head is not enough, or when the model is too big to fully fine-tune on the card you have. For vision-language-action models, this is the usual first choice. It costs you a little accuracy compared with full fine-tuning on large data, and a few extra settings to choose, such as the rank.

Third, fully fine-tune when you have plenty of data, the job is far from the pretrained job, and you have the memory. For small detectors, this is the normal choice, because the model is small. It costs you memory, time, and the risk of forgetting.

The obvious alternative to all three is to train a model from scratch on your own data. The worked example shows why people rarely do: with 50 examples, the model trained from scratch had an error of 0.172, against 0.041 to 0.069 for the three fine-tuning ways. Pretraining gives you knowledge that your small dataset could never teach. Training from scratch only makes sense when no pretrained model has seen anything like your data, and you have a very large amount of it.

The cost of fine-tuning in general is the data you collect and label, the graphics card time, and the testing. The testing is the part that people most often skip, and the part that decides whether the robot works.

The final section is about using it in Python. Earlier, the page explained LoRA as two thin grids, and named the library that inserts them. This section puts the two together, so that you know that using LoRA means writing one configuration object and one wrapping call, and that you never touch the thin grids yourself.

The page shows a short Python script. It imports the necessary libraries, including PEFT, and loads a pretrained OpenVLA model. Then, it creates a LoRA configuration object. This object sets the rank to 32, sets how strongly the side path is added on, and tells the library to put a side path on every fully connected layer. The script then wraps the model with this configuration. Finally, it prints how many numbers will really be trained.

Those are the settings OpenVLA's own fine-tuning script uses, so the numbers in the earlier LoRA table are the numbers this code produces. Training then runs as normal, and when it finishes, saving the model writes only the side paths, which is why an adapter is a few megabytes rather than a few gigabytes.

The PEFT library gives you the whole of way two. It walks through the model, replaces every layer that matches your settings with a version that has the two thin grids beside it, and freezes everything else, so the 1 to 2 percent of the numbers being trained happens without your counting anything. Printing the trainable parameters is worth reading every time, because a setting that matches nothing fails silently and trains nothing at all.

What you have to collect yourself is the data, and that is the part that takes weeks rather than minutes. You record the demonstrations, and you convert them into the format the project's scripts read, which is the LeRobot dataset format for openpi and RLDS for OpenVLA. You also have to write the training loop, or else use the project's script, because PEFT only prepares the model and does not train it.

What you have to decide is the rank, which layers get a side path, and how long to train. Start with the rank and the settings from the example, because they are what the model's authors used, and change the rank only when the results tell you to. Fine-tuning a detector needs none of this, because the whole job is a single call to the YOLO library to train on your data, where you can pass an option to keep the first ten layers fixed for the case where you have very few pictures.
