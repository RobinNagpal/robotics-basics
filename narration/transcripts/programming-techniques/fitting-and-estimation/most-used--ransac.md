RANSAC: fitting when some points are wrong. 

This page explains random sample consensus, or RANSAC, which fits a line, a plane, a circle, or a cylinder to a set of points. It is meant for the case where many of those points belong to something else. It answers four questions, and the first of them is how RANSAC finds the right shape when a third or half of the points are wrong. The other three are how many tries it needs, where a robot arm uses it, and what makes it fail.

It is for a reader who has read the page on least-squares fitting. That page explains parameters, residuals, and the root mean square residual, and it shows that one bad point can move a least-squares line a long way. This page starts from that problem, and every number on it comes from a real run of the diagram script.

RANSAC is the standard way a table-top robot finds the table in a depth camera's point cloud. Although Martin Fischler and Robert Bolles published it as long ago as 1981, it is still the first thing most perception pipelines run on a new point cloud.

The first section answers why this method is needed. Real point clouds are messy, and a depth camera looking at a table shows why. It sees the table, but it also sees the objects standing on it, the edge of the table, and the floor beyond. Then there are the false readings, which are scattered points that appear where the light hit a shiny surface. So if you want the table's plane, only some of those points belong to it.

Least squares uses every point, so the other points drag the answer away. You could remove them first, but to know which points to remove you would need to know where the table is, and that is the question you started with.

RANSAC breaks this circle, because it does not need to know in advance which points are good. Instead, it finds the shape and the points that belong to it at the same time. The points that belong to the shape are called inliers, and the rest are called outliers.

The next part gives the idea in one sentence. The previous section said that RANSAC finds the shape and its good points at the same time. However, it did not say how, and the answer fits into a single sentence. Guess the shape from a few randomly chosen points, count how many other points agree with the guess, repeat many times, and keep the guess that most points agree with.

Here is an everyday example, in which a group of people each point at where they think a lost ball landed. Most of them saw it, so they point near the same spot, while a few are guessing and point all over the field. If you take the average of all the pointing directions, the guessers drag it away from the true spot. Instead, you pick one person at random and ask everyone else whether they agree with that person, give or take a metre. If most hands go up, then you have found the spot. But if only two hands go up, you picked a guesser, so you pick someone else and ask again.

The key point is that a guess made only from good points will be agreed with by all the other good points. However, a guess that includes even one bad point will be agreed with by almost nobody. So you do not need to know which points are good. You only need to keep trying until one guess is made entirely from good points.

The third section explains how it works, step by step, starting with the loop. RANSAC needs three things from you: the kind of shape, the smallest number of points that defines one such shape, and a distance limit. First, the smallest number of points is two for a line, three for a plane, and three for a circle on a table. Second, the distance limit says how close a point must be to the shape to count as agreeing with it, so it should be a little larger than the sensor's noise.

Once you have given it those three things, it runs a loop. First, it picks the smallest number of points at random. Second, it makes the one shape that passes exactly through them. Third, it measures every point's distance from that shape, and counts the points closer than the distance limit, because those points are this try's inliers. Fourth, if this try has more inliers than the best try so far, it remembers it. Fifth, it repeats from the first step a fixed number of times. Finally, it takes the inliers of the best try, and fits the shape to all of them with least squares, so that this last step uses every good point and not just the two or three that were picked.

Next is a worked example of a table edge with stray points. The example has 55 points in a side view of a table edge, measured in millimetres. Forty of them lie along the edge, whose true line is y equals 0.25 times x plus 20, with 1.5 millimetres of noise. The other fifteen are stray readings scattered above the edge. So 27 percent of the points are outliers.

A diagram shows three of the 100 tries RANSAC made, with a distance limit of 4 millimetres. Try 1 picked a stray point and an edge point. Its line therefore crosses the edge at an angle, so only 7 points lie within 4 millimetres of it. Try 3 picked two edge points close together at the right end, and their noise tilts the line a little. So it fits the right part of the edge and misses the left part, which leaves only 20 points agreeing. Try 9 picked two edge points 65 millimetres apart, so its line follows the whole edge and all 40 edge points agree with it. No try did better than 40, which is why try 9 is the one RANSAC kept.

The final step fits a least-squares line to those 40 inliers, and a second diagram compares the result with a least-squares line through all 55 points. The diagram shows that a least-squares line through all points is pulled up by the outliers, while the RANSAC line follows the edge, with the outliers marked and ignored.

The least-squares line through everything is y equals 0.188 times x plus 34.0. Its offset is therefore 14 millimetres too high, and its slope is a quarter too shallow. The RANSAC line is y equals 0.260 times x plus 19.1, which is close to the true y equals 0.25 times x plus 20. RANSAC also labelled every one of the 55 points correctly, with the 40 edge points as inliers and the 15 stray points as outliers.

The page then shows these steps as pseudocode. It defines a function that takes the points, the sample size, the distance limit, and the number of tries. It starts with an empty set of best inliers, then loops for the number of tries. In each try, it chooses the sample size of points at random without repeats, and makes the shape that passes exactly through them. If the shape cannot be made, for example if the points are on one line, it skips to the next try. Otherwise, it finds every point whose distance to the shape is less than the distance limit. If this set of inliers is larger than the best inliers so far, it updates the best inliers. After the loop, it does a least-squares fit of the shape to the best inliers, finds the final inliers using the distance limit again, and returns the final shape and its inliers.

Two common improvements to this loop are worth knowing, and the first of them stops early. Once a new best try is found, you recompute how many tries are needed from the share of inliers just found, and you stop as soon as that many tries have been done. The second improvement, called MSAC, scores each try by how close the agreeing points are, not just how many there are, which breaks ties between guesses more sensibly.

The next part explains how many tries are enough. The loop repeats a fixed number of times, so the next thing to work out is what that number should be. A try succeeds when every point in its sample is an inlier. So if a share w of the points are inliers and a sample has s points, one try succeeds with a chance of w multiplied by itself s times, written as w to the power of s. To be sure, with a chance p such as 99 percent, that at least one try succeeds, you need a specific number of tries. The formula is the logarithm of one minus p, divided by the logarithm of one minus w to the power of s.

In the worked example, w is 40 out of 55, which is 0.727, and s is 2. So one try succeeds with a chance of 0.53. For 99 percent certainty the formula gives 6.1, which means 7 tries are enough, and yet the example used 100. That is common in practice, because a line or plane try is cheap, so people use a generous number.

A diagram plots this formula on a logarithmic scale, showing the tries needed for 99 percent success against the share of outliers, for samples of 2, 3, and 6 points. The diagram shows that the number of tries grows slowly at first and then very fast. For a plane with half the points as outliers, 35 tries are enough. But for a plane with 70 percent outliers, 168 tries are needed. A shape that needs 6 points, with half the points as outliers, needs 292 tries. This is why RANSAC works best for shapes defined by very few points. It is also why it slows down sharply when the wanted shape is only a small part of the scene.

The page moves on to planes and cylinders in a point cloud. A plane needs a sample of only 3 points, so it is one of the cheap shapes in the chart just described. This is also the shape a table-top robot wants most often. The plane through those 3 points has a normal, which is the direction at right angles to the plane. Because the three points make a triangle, that normal is equal to the cross product of two edges of the triangle. The cross product is a standard operation that gives the direction at right angles to two other directions. A point's distance from the plane is then the size of its offset from one of the three points, measured along the normal.

A diagram shows RANSAC running on a simulated depth camera view of a table with a box and a cup on it, plus 25 stray readings. It used 200 tries and a distance limit of 5 millimetres. The diagram shows the full point cloud on the left, and on the right, it shows the table plane's points in brown and the leftover points of the box, the cup, and the stray readings in blue.

Of the 1669 points, 1120 are on the table plane and 549 are left over. The fitted plane's normal is minus 0.030, 0.020, 0.999. The table itself was made tilted with a normal of minus 0.030, 0.020, 0.999, so the fit matches to three decimal places. The top of the box is also a plane, but it has only 90 points against the table's 1100 or so, so RANSAC keeps the table. The leftover points go to clustering, which is explained on another page, and this splits them into one group per object.

A cylinder, such as a can, a bottle, or a pipe, is harder, because two or three positions alone do not fix its axis. The usual method therefore works out each point's normal first, meaning the direction the surface faces at that point, from a small plane fit to its nearest neighbours. Then a try picks 2 points together with their normals. Because both normals on a cylinder point straight away from the axis, the axis must run at right angles to both. That gives the axis direction, and from the axis direction the position and the radius follow. A point agrees if its distance from the axis is close to the radius and its normal points away from the axis. The Point Cloud Library does this with its cylinder model, which is described later.

The final part of this section covers choosing the distance limit. Of the three things RANSAC needs from you, the distance limit is the one that matters most. A good start is two to three times the sensor's noise. For example, on a table half a metre away, a depth camera has about 1.5 millimetres of noise, so a limit of 4 to 5 millimetres is common.

If the limit is too small, real surface points count as outliers. So the inlier count of the true shape drops, and a wrong shape can win. If the limit is too large, points just above the surface count as inliers. On a table, that means the bottom few millimetres of every object are deleted along with the table. That matters most for flat objects such as a coin or a phone lying down, because those can vanish completely.

The fourth section explains where RANSAC is used on a robot arm. It is mostly a perception tool, but it also appears in calibration and sensing. First, it is used for removing the table. The classic table-top recipe finds the biggest plane, deletes it, and clusters what is left into objects. Second, it finds walls and bin sides. A robot picking from a bin runs RANSAC several times to find the biggest plane, which is the bin floor, remove its points, find the next, which is a wall, and so on, until what is left is the parts. Third, it fits cylinders. A robot that picks cans, bottles, or pipes fits a cylinder to each cluster to get its axis and radius, and so the direction to approach it from. Fourth, it finds a circle in a noisy rim. A glass's rim seen by a camera often has a few pixels from a reflection or from a neighbouring glass, so a circle RANSAC finds the rim first, and then least squares refines it. Fifth, it matches features between pictures. When a camera on the arm moves, a program matches small patches between the old and the new picture, and some of those matches are wrong, so RANSAC finds the camera movement that most matches agree with. The same idea finds an object's pose from matched points. Sixth, it is used in calibration. When some checkerboard corners or marker detections in a calibration set are wrong, a RANSAC step can drop those poses before the final least-squares fit. Finally, it checks a known table. When the table is fixed to the arm and measured once, RANSAC is not needed on every picture, but it can still run now and then to check that the camera has not been knocked.

The fifth section covers where it is useful, and where it is not. The shape you want must be the biggest single shape in the data, and it must be defined by a few points. You must also be able to say how far from the shape a good point may be, because that distance is what decides which points agree. Once those conditions hold, it copes with half or more of the points being outliers, which almost nothing else does.

But once one of them fails, RANSAC fails with it, so the page provides a table listing the ways that happens, the signs you would see, and what to use instead. If the wanted shape is not the biggest, for example a large box top beside a small patch of visible table, the sign is that the table is 60 millimetres too high, or tilted. Instead, you should check the plane's normal and height against what you expect, restrict the search to a region, or use a known table plane. If there are two shapes of similar size, the answer flips between them from one picture to the next. Instead, find one, remove its points, find the other, and decide between them by a rule such as choosing the lowest. If there are too few tries for the share of outliers, you will see a different, wrong answer on some runs. The solution is to raise the number of tries, or use the formula mentioned earlier to set it. If the distance limit is too small, many real surface points are marked as outliers and the answer jumps around. You should raise the limit to two or three times the noise. If the distance limit is too large, flat objects vanish with the table and the bottom of every object is cut off. You should lower the limit and keep points just above the plane for small objects. If the shape needs many points to define, RANSAC takes very many tries and is slow. Instead, use a method that starts from a good guess, such as iterative closest point, or a learned model. If you need the same answer every run, RANSAC's tiny differences between runs can make tests flaky. You should fix the random seed, or refit with least squares on the inliers. Finally, if no shape dominates, such as in a cluttered pile of parts, you will see low inlier counts for every try. Instead, use a point cloud model that labels each point, or use clustering first.

The sixth section lists libraries that provide RANSAC. You do not have to write the loop yourself, because RANSAC is built into every major point cloud and vision library. Open3D, used from Python and C++, provides a segment plane function on its point cloud object. It returns the plane and the inlier indices, and is the usual table-removal call. The Point Cloud Library, or PCL, used from C++, provides segmentation classes for planes, lines, circles, spheres, and cylinders, and also includes MSAC and other variants. OpenCV, used from C++ and Python, provides functions for matching points between pictures, and newer versions offer the USAC family of improved methods. Scikit-learn, used from Python, provides a RANSAC regressor that wraps any regression model, which is good for lines and planes in NumPy arrays. Finally, scikit-image, used from Python, provides a general RANSAC measure with ready-made line, circle, and ellipse models. For a table plane in Python, the Open3D call is a single line, usually called with a distance threshold of 0.005, which is 5 millimetres written in metres, and 3 points per try.

The seventh section explains why to use RANSAC, and what it costs. RANSAC is a fitting method that tries many shapes, each one through a few random points, and keeps the shape that most points agree with. It gives the arm the right shape even when many of the points belong to other things, and it says which of the points belong to that shape.

The obvious alternative is least squares on all the points, perhaps after throwing away the points far from a first fit and fitting again. That works when only a few points are bad. But the first fit is already pulled towards the bad points, so it may throw away good points and keep bad ones. In the worked example, the first least-squares line was 14 millimetres too high at the left end. RANSAC never averages bad points in, so it works when a third or half of the points are bad.

A second alternative is a robust loss, which is a least-squares variant that counts large errors less. It is smooth and repeatable, and it is good when outliers are a small share. But it starts from a guess, so with many outliers it can settle on the wrong shape, while RANSAC needs no starting guess at all.

RANSAC costs you five things. First, it uses random choices, so two runs can give slightly different answers unless the seed is fixed. Second, it needs a distance limit, which means you must know the sensor's noise before you can set it. Third, it is slower than one least-squares fit, because it makes many tries, and it gets very slow for shapes that need many points. Fourth, it finds only the biggest shape, which may not be the one you meant. Finally, it needs a least-squares refit at the end to get the best accuracy.

The eighth section discusses the learned alternative. A segmentation model gives every point a name, such as mug, box, or table. So it replaces RANSAC when no single shape dominates the scene, such as a cluttered pile of parts. For shapes that need many points to define, a pose model finds the object's pose directly. But a point cloud model needs labelled 3D data, which is much scarcer than labelled photos. It also needs a graphics card for large clouds, and it knows only the kinds of object it was trained on. RANSAC is still the better choice for the table, the walls of a bin, and other simple shapes. This is because it needs no training, runs on an ordinary processor, and says exactly which points belong to the shape.

The final section shows how to use it in Python. It puts the Open3D and scikit-learn libraries together in a program you can run. Open3D works on a point cloud object and knows what a plane is, so the plane fit is one method call. Scikit-learn works on plain NumPy arrays and knows nothing about geometry, so you hand it the model you want made robust and it wraps RANSAC around it.

The code first loads a point cloud scene in metres using Open3D. It calls the segment plane function with a distance threshold of 0.005, a sample size of 3, and 1000 iterations. This returns the plane parameters and the inliers. It then selects the points that were not inliers, which gives the objects that stood on the table. Next, the code fits a robust line through two-dimensional edge points in millimetres using scikit-learn. It loads the x and y coordinates, then creates a RANSAC regressor wrapping a linear regression model, with a residual threshold of 2.0 and a maximum of 100 trials. It fits this to the points, extracts the slope, and gets a mask of the points the line agreed with.

On a made-up scene of 4000 table points scattered by 2 millimetres, 600 points belonging to a box, and 200 stray points, the segment plane function with a 5 millimetre limit finds a plane facing almost perfectly upwards and keeps 3966 of the 4800 points as inliers. That leaves 834 points standing above the table, which is the box and the strays together, and is the input the clustering page starts from.

The libraries give you the whole loop described earlier. They pick the random samples, fit the candidate shape, count the agreeing points, repeat, and return the best shape together with the list of points that agreed with it. Open3D also gives you a select by index function to invert the selection, which is how you keep everything that was not part of the plane.

What you still have to write is the step before and the step after. Before the call you have to load the cloud and cut it down, because RANSAC on a full-frame depth picture wastes time on the floor and the walls. After the call you have to check that the shape is the one you wanted, because RANSAC always returns its best guess and a table-shaped guess can just as easily be a wall. Checking usually means testing that the plane faces roughly upwards and that the number of inliers is a reasonable share of the points.

What you have to decide or measure is the distance threshold, and it is the one number on this page that nobody can choose for you. It has to be a little larger than your sensor's own depth noise, because otherwise the real table points count as outliers, and it has to be smaller than the thinnest object you want to keep, because otherwise the bottom of a sheet of paper is swallowed by the table. For the depth cameras used on arm workcells, 5 millimetres written as 0.005 is the usual starting point. You also choose the sample size, which is 3 because three points define a plane, and the number of iterations, which you can work out from the share of points you expect to be good.
