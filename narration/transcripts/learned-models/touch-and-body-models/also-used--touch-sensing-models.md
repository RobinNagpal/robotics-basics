Touch sensing models. This page answers one question: how does a robot turn a picture of its finger pad into facts about what it is touching? To answer that, it explains the sensors that make such a picture, what a model learns to read from it, how that model is trained, and where it helps a robot arm. 

It is written for a reader who already understands the basics of how a model learns. You do not need to know anything about touch sensors. However, you should know what a digital picture is, which is a grid of small squares called pixels, each with a number for its brightness.

The first section explains what a touch sensing model is. It takes a small picture of a contact and says where the contact is, what shape it has, and how hard it presses. Here is an everyday example of the same skill. Close your eyes and pick up a pen, and your fingertip skin bends around the pen. From how it bends, you know the pen is round, you know roughly where it sits on your finger, and you know how hard you are pressing. In other words, you did not need to see it. A robot finger with a tactile sensor gets a similar signal, where "tactile" means to do with touch, and the sensor reports how its soft surface has bent. So a touch sensing model is the part that turns that raw signal into the facts you would have known with your eyes closed.

Before a model can read a tactile picture, something has to make one. Most of the models discussed here work with one kind of sensor, called a vision-based tactile sensor. This is a small camera that films the inside of a soft pad. A diagram shows a cut-through of a camera-behind-gel touch sensor. The left half of the picture shows the inside of the sensor, and the right half shows the kind of picture its camera takes when a screw presses into the pad. 

The sensor has four parts, from the outside in. First, there is a soft pad made of a clear rubbery material called gel, which is a few millimetres thick. Second, there is a thin painted skin on the outside of the gel. The paint stops the camera from seeing through to the object, so the camera sees only the shape of the skin. Third, there are small coloured lights around the edge. Each one shines across the gel from a different side. Finally, there is a small camera underneath, looking up at the skin. 

Once an object presses into the pad, the skin bends. A slope that faces one light looks bright in that light's colour, while a slope that faces away looks dark. So the camera picture shows the shape of the dent as coloured shading. The best-known sensors of this kind are GelSight, first built at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and DIGIT, a low-cost design from Meta. Another kind, called TacTip, uses a soft skin with small pins on the inside, and the camera watches the pins move. 

Some sensors are not cameras at all, and two other designs are common. For example, a pressure array is a grid of small pressure sensors, each giving one number, and a magnetic skin has tiny magnets in soft rubber, with a chip underneath that measures how the magnets move. Both of those still produce a grid of numbers, so the same kind of model can read them.

Now that the sensor is described, the next part covers what passes in and out of the model. The input is one tactile picture, or a short run of them. For a camera-based sensor, that picture is an ordinary small colour image, while for a pressure array it is a grid of pressure numbers. A diagram illustrates this, showing a simplified tactile image as a grid of numbers, where each number says how deep the gel is pressed at that spot. A small network reads the whole grid and gives back the answers. 

The outputs a robot arm needs most are these. First, contact or no contact, meaning is the pad touching anything at all? Second, where on the pad the contact is, such as in the middle or near the edge. Third, the shape of the contact, like a flat face, an edge, a corner, or a round surface. Fourth, the force. This includes the normal force, which is how hard the pad is pressed straight in, and the shear force, which is how hard it is pushed sideways. Finally, a height map. For each pixel, this asks how deep the dent is, which gives a small three-dimensional shape of the part of the object that touches the pad. Some models give only one of these answers, while others give several at once.

The next section explains how the model reaches these answers. There are two ways to get from the picture to the answers: the first uses no learning, and the second does. 

Without learning, you can find the shape from the shading. Because the coloured shading in the picture follows a simple rule, the depth can be worked out without any learning at all. The brighter a spot is in the red light, the more that spot's slope faces the red light, and the same holds for each colour. So with three lights you can work out the slope at every pixel, and if you add up the slopes across the picture you get the depth of the dent. This method is called photometric stereo, where "photometric" means measuring light, and "stereo" here means using more than one view of the light. However, the method needs a calibration before it can be used. You press a small ball of known size into the pad and record how each slope looks. After that, it works for any shape.

Instead, a neural network can learn the same thing, and more besides. The network used is usually a convolutional neural network, which is the kind of network used for ordinary photos. It slides small filters across the picture, looks for simple patterns such as edges, and then combines them into larger patterns. The steps are as follows. First, the camera takes a tactile picture. Second, the model subtracts a picture of the same pad with nothing touching it. What is left is only the change caused by the contact. Third, the network reads the change and builds up a set of numbers that describe it. Finally, a small last part of the network turns those numbers into the answers, such as contact or not, where, what shape, and how much force.

To see the sideways push, the shading shows how deep the pad is pressed, but it does not show well how hard the pad is pushed sideways. So for that, many gel sensors have a grid of small black dots printed on the gel. A diagram shows this grid of dots three times. When nothing touches, the dots sit on their grid. When something presses straight in, the dots near the contact spread outwards a little. When something presses and also pushes sideways, the dots in the contact all move the same way. A model can track each dot from one picture to the next. Then how far and in which direction the dots move tells it the sideways force. This is the signal used to catch an object starting to slide.

The next section explains how the model is trained, and where its examples come from. A touch sensing model learns from examples, and each example is a tactile picture together with the right answer. So the hard part is getting those right answers. 

For contact shape and height maps, people use the photometric stereo method just described to make the answers. Instead, they may press objects of known shape, such as balls, cylinders and cubes, into the pad at known places. For force, people mount the tactile sensor on top of a separate force sensor. Then they press many objects into it at many angles and record both at once, so the force sensor gives the right answer for each tactile picture. Thousands of presses is a normal size for such a set. 

A newer way needs no answers at all, and it is called self-supervised learning. The model is given a very large number of tactile pictures with no labels. Then it learns by solving a made-up puzzle: parts of each picture are hidden, and it must guess what was hidden. Because it can only do that well by learning what tactile pictures are like in general, the puzzle teaches it something useful. Afterwards, a small extra part is trained on a few labelled examples for the task you actually need. 

There is a second route to large amounts of data, and that is simulation. A tactile simulator draws the picture the sensor would take for a given object and contact. Two of them are free to use, called TACTO and Taxim. However, the pictures are not quite the same as real ones, so a model trained only on simulated pictures usually needs some real ones too.

The page then lists well-known models and tools. This area has far fewer models than the camera side of robotics, and the ones listed are all real and published. First are GelSight's own shape and force methods. The MIT group that made GelSight published how to get a height map from the shading, and how to estimate force from the moving dots. Much later work starts here. Second is a model called "The Feeling of Success" from 2017. A network looks at GelSight pictures from both fingers, plus a camera picture, and predicts whether a grasp will hold once the object is lifted. Third is Sparsh, released by Meta in 2024. It is a self-supervised model trained on a large set of tactile pictures from several sensor types. It is meant as a starting point that other tactile models build on, though its licence forbids commercial use. Fourth is Transferable Tactile Transformers, or T3, from 2024. This is one model shared across many different tactile sensors, with a small separate part for each sensor. The idea is to stop training from scratch every time the sensor changes. Fifth is NeuralFeels, which joins touch with a camera to rebuild the shape and position of an object while a robot hand turns it. Finally, TACTO and Taxim. These are not models but simulators. They make realistic tactile pictures, so you can build and test the rest of a pipeline without a sensor on the desk.

Next is a worked example of checking the grip on a mug. Here is one grip on a mug from start to finish. A two-finger gripper with a gel sensor on each finger picks up the mug by its side. First, the fingers close, so both tactile pictures change, and the model says "contact" on both fingers. Second, the model finds where on each pad the contact is. Suppose both contacts are near the lower edge of the pads, which means the fingers closed on the mug too high, so only the lower edge of each pad touches it. Third, the model finds the shape of the contact, and it is a long, gently curved strip. That matches the side of a round mug rather than the thin handle. Fourth, the model estimates the normal force on each pad, and both are similar, which means the mug is centred between the fingers. Finally, the program checks these facts against what it expected. The contact is near the edge, so the grip is less secure than planned. The program opens the fingers, moves down a little, and closes them again. A camera could not do the second step at all. Once the fingers are closed, they hide exactly the part of the mug that the answer depends on.

The page then covers what goes wrong. The previous sections described these models at their best, but touch sensing models also have problems that camera models do not. There are six that matter most. First, the gel wears out. The pad is soft and it rubs against every object, so its surface gets scratched, the paint wears, and the dots fade. This means a model trained on a new pad sees different pictures from an old one, and one maker rates its gel for about a thousand presses. So people replace gels and re-check the model after each change. Second, every sensor is a little different. Two sensors of the same model have slightly different lights, cameras and gels, so a model trained on one often does worse on the next. People calibrate each sensor with a reference press, or use models such as T3 that are built to share across sensors. Third, the reading drifts with temperature. The gel's stiffness changes when it warms up, so people take a fresh "nothing touching" picture before each grasp and subtract it from the new one. Fourth, it sees only the contact. A tactile picture shows a patch a couple of centimetres across, and it says nothing about where the object is in the room or what the rest of it looks like. So people combine it with a camera for that. Fifth, simulated pictures are not real pictures. A model trained only in a simulator usually does worse on a real sensor, so people mix real examples into the training set. Finally, the software is thin. There is very little maintained open-source code, and some of what exists has licences that forbid commercial use or require you to share your own code.

Weighing these problems, the next section asks why to use this rather than the obvious alternative, and what it costs. There are two obvious alternatives, and each is right in some cases. 

The first alternative is not to use a tactile sensor at all, and to use the gripper's own finger position and a wrist force sensor instead. This is cheaper, and it is what most working robot cells do. It tells you that the fingers stopped on something and how much the held object weighs. However, it does not tell you where on the pad the object sits, or what shape the contact has. So a tactile sensor with a model is worth it when those things decide success, such as a small part that must be held exactly in the middle, a thin edge, or an object the camera cannot see once it is held. 

The second alternative is a tactile sensor without a learned model, using the photometric stereo method. This is a good choice for a height map, because it is exact, it needs no training data, and it is easy to check. However, a learned model is worth it when you want answers that the height map does not give directly, such as force, slip, or whether the grasp will hold. 

What it costs you is money and upkeep for the sensors and their spare gels. It also costs a training set, which means many presses with a reference force sensor, or the licence limits of a pretrained model. You also face retraining or recalibrating when the gel or the sensor changes. Finally, it costs you a model that gives no guarantee. It can be confidently wrong on a contact unlike anything in its training set, so a program that uses it should still check the wrist force before trusting a grip.

The written alternative section notes that part of the written alternative is already on this page. Photometric stereo turns the tactile picture into a height map with no learning. For the cheaper choice of no tactile sensor, other parts of the textbook explain how to turn the finger position and the wrist force into clean yes-or-no checks, such as "the fingers stopped on something", and what those checks miss. The written way wins when a height map, or the finger position and the weight, answers your question. The learned model wins when you need force, slip, or whether the grasp will hold.

It also points to image classification to understand how a convolutional neural network reads an ordinary picture, and grasp quality models that score a grasp before the fingers close, whereas touch models check it after.

The final section is about using it in Python. It shows the shortest honest version of the code needed to read one frame from the sensor, subtract the blank pad, and pass the difference through a pretrained backbone with a new head. 

The code uses the digit-interface library to connect to the sensor using its serial number. It grabs a blank frame of the pad with nothing touching it. Then, it sets up a standard neural network backbone from Torchvision, pre-trained on ordinary photos, and replaces its final layer with a new head that outputs three numbers: where on the pad the contact sits, across and down, and how hard it presses. When a new frame comes in, the code subtracts the blank frame from it, scales the result, and passes it through the network to get those three numbers.

The digit-interface library gives you the frames as plain picture arrays, so nothing about the camera, the lights or the video stream is your problem. However, its licence forbids commercial use, which is exactly the licence trouble warned about earlier. For a GelSight or any other camera-behind-gel sensor there is no such library, but you do not need one, because the sensor appears to the computer as an ordinary camera and standard computer vision libraries can read it.

The code uses a backbone with its pretrained numbers already downloaded. The numbers it produces were learned from photographs rather than from tactile pictures, so they are not ideal, but they are a much better starting point than random numbers when you have only a few thousand presses.

What you have to collect yourself is the training data, because the code only runs the network, and the network has to be trained first. To get the right answers, you mount the tactile sensor on top of a force sensor and press many objects into it, and the force sensor's reading is the answer for each picture. You also have to take the blank frame again before each grasp rather than once at startup, because the gel's stiffness drifts as it warms up.

What you have to decide is which answers the head gives, how often you retake the blank frame, and when a worn gel means the model must be checked again. Because every sensor differs a little, a model trained on one pad is not safe to move to another without at least a reference press.
