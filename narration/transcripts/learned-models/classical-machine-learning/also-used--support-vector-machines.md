Support vector machines. This page explains support vector machines, or SVMs. These are classifiers that split two groups of examples with the line that leaves the widest possible gap between them. It answers five questions, taking them in turn. Which line does an SVM choose, and why? What happens when the groups overlap? How can it draw a curved boundary? Where were SVMs used on robot arms? And why do trees and small networks usually do their job today?

It is for a reader who understands how a model learns, specifically what an example, a label, a feature, and overfitting are. It also helps to know about linear and logistic regression, because a linear SVM also scores an input with a weighted sum of its features.

SVMs were the most common classifier in robotics research from the late 1990s to the early 2010s. But they are in the "also used" group now, because new projects seldom choose them. You will still meet them in older papers, in older code, and in a few small problems where they remain a sound choice. The numbers discussed come from a real run of a script on simulated data, trained in NumPy.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. A support vector machine separates two groups of examples with the straight line that stays as far as possible from the nearest example on each side. For example, a council paints a line down the middle of a park path to separate walkers from cyclists, and it could paint that line anywhere between the two streams of people. The sensible place is right in the middle of the gap, as far from both streams as it can be. Then a walker who drifts a little to one side still ends up on the walkers' half. The SVM makes the same choice for data, so a new example that lands a little off from the training examples is still likely to fall on the right side.

The next part of the page explains how it works. Since the SVM stays as far as it can from both groups, this section says how it finds that place. The example used is contact detection, where a robot arm moves through free space and sometimes touches something. From its sensors, the program computes two features, each scaled to run from about zero to one. The first is the jump in wrist force, meaning how much the force at the wrist sensor changed in the last few milliseconds. The second is the unexpected torque, which is the gap between the torque the arm's joints measure and the torque the arm's model says it should need. Each example is one short moment, labelled "touching" or "moving freely".

Looking at the widest gap, a linear SVM gives each feature a weight, multiplies, adds the results, and then adds a constant. The result is called the score. A positive score means "touching", while a negative score means "moving freely". So the points where the score is exactly zero form a straight line, which is the decision boundary. With three features it is a flat plane, and with more it is the same idea in more directions.

When the two groups do not overlap, many lines separate them. A diagram shows forty examples with three different lines that all split the groups without a mistake, passing close to some examples. The gap, or margin, of a line is the width of the empty band around it, from the nearest example on one side to the nearest on the other. These three lines have gaps of 0.08, 0.06, and 0.13. The diagram then shows the SVM's choice, which is the line with the widest possible gap. Here, that gap is 0.22 wide.

Only three examples touch the edges of this gap. These are the support vectors, and they give the method its name. The line depends only on them, so you could move or delete any of the other thirty-seven examples, as long as it stayed outside the gap, and the line would not change. The SVM sets its weights so that the score is exactly plus one on one edge of the gap and minus one on the other. The larger the weights, the faster the score changes across the plane, and so the narrower the band between minus one and plus one. So, to make the gap as wide as possible, the SVM makes the weights as small as possible, while every example still has a score of at least plus one or at most minus one on its own side.

The next concept is the soft margin, for when the groups overlap. The widest gap only exists if there is a gap at all, and real data overlaps. A light touch can look like free motion, and a fast turn can look like a touch. Then no line separates the groups, so the rule above has no answer. The soft margin lets some examples sit inside the gap, or even on the wrong side, but it charges a cost for each one. The cost is zero for an example outside the gap on its own side. It then grows in a straight line with how far the example sits inside the gap or beyond it. This cost is called the hinge loss, because its graph is flat and then bends upward, like an opened hinge.

The SVM now balances two wishes, which are a wide gap and a small total cost. A setting called C decides the balance. A large C makes each example inside the gap expensive, so the SVM narrows the gap to keep examples out. A small C makes them cheap instead, so the SVM keeps a wide gap and lets many examples in. 

A table and diagram compare two SVMs trained on the same eighty overlapping examples, and tested on four thousand new ones. The first uses a small C of 0.42, creating a soft, wide gap. This gap is 0.58 wide and rests on fifty-four support vectors. It leaves four training examples on the wrong side, but gets 95.6 percent right on new data. The second uses a large C of 125, creating a strict, narrow gap. This gap is only 0.14 wide and rests on just ten support vectors. It leaves only two training examples on the wrong side, but gets slightly less right on new data, at 95.0 percent. With a soft margin, every example inside the gap or on the wrong side is also a support vector, so there are many more of them. Because the soft line rests on fifty-four examples rather than ten, one odd example moves it less, which here gave a slightly better score on new data. The two lines are close, but that is not always so. As with the depth of a tree, the right C is found by trying several and testing each on examples it did not train on.

The next part explains the kernel trick, for curved boundaries. A soft margin copes with overlap, but sometimes no straight line can do the job at all. The next example is about grasping, where a gripper closes on a small object. The features are how far the gripper's centre was from the object's centre, left-right and front-back, in millimetres. The made-up rule is that the grasp holds when the gripper is within 11 millimetres of the centre, in any direction. So the "held" examples form a disc in the middle, with "failed" examples all around it.

A diagram shows a linear SVM trying to separate the two offsets. No straight line can put a disc on one side and a ring on the other, so the best it can do is to call every grasp a failure. Since 76 percent of the new grasps did fail, that scores 76 percent, but it is useless. Next, it shows what happens when a third column is added. This column is the left-right offset squared plus the front-back offset squared, which is the squared distance from the centre. In this new column, every "held" example has a small value and every "failed" example a large one, so now a straight cut works. Finally, it shows that straight cut back in the original picture, where it forms a circle with a radius of about 10.9 millimetres, against the true 11. This SVM gets 97.3 percent of the new grasps right.

The script built the new column by hand, but the kernel trick is a way to get the same effect without building the columns at all. The training and the prediction of an SVM can be written so that they never need the features on their own, because they only need a similarity number between pairs of examples. A kernel is a formula that gives this number directly from the original features, and each kernel matches some set of extra columns. So with the right kernel, the SVM works as if it had many extra columns, even an endless number, while computing only one number per pair of examples. The most used kernel is the radial basis function, or RBF kernel. This says that two examples are similar when they are close together and that the similarity fades with distance. An SVM with this kernel can draw almost any smooth boundary. It has one more setting, often called gamma, that says how fast the similarity fades. A large gamma gives a wiggly boundary that can overfit. This is the same kind of kernel used in Gaussian processes.

Everything so far predicted a class, but an SVM can also predict a number, which is called support vector regression, or SVR. Instead of a gap that should stay empty, it fits a tube around a line, and examples should stay inside that tube. Errors smaller than the tube's half-width cost nothing, while larger errors cost in a straight line with their size. As before, only the examples on the tube's edge or outside it decide where the line goes. A diagram shows a linear SVR fitted to thirty readings of a force sensor with nothing attached, at different sensor temperatures, because the reading drifts as the sensor warms up. Two of the readings were hit by a knock, so they are far too high. The SVR line is shown with its tube, which has a half-width of 0.2 newtons, so only the two knocked readings sit outside it. The SVR found a drift of 0.089 newtons per degree, against the true 0.090. A plain least-squares line is shown next to it, which found 0.091, which is just as good here. So SVR is seldom the best choice for a number on a robot, because ridge regression, Gaussian processes, and trees usually do the job with less tuning.

The third section covers how it is trained, including what data it needs and how much. An SVM needs a table of examples, where each row is one example, each column one number, and the label is one of two classes. For more than two classes, libraries train one SVM per pair of classes, or one per class against the rest, and then combine their answers. 

Training means choosing the weights. For a linear SVM, the quantity to make small is the size of the weights, which keeps the gap wide. To that, it adds the average hinge loss over the examples, which keeps examples out of the gap. The script does this with subgradient descent. It is the same as standard gradient descent, but because the hinge loss has a sharp bend where the slope is not defined, the script uses the slope on one side of the bend. 

The steps are: first, start with all of the weights at zero. Second, find every example that is inside the gap or on the wrong side. Third, move the weights a little toward scoring those examples correctly, and shrink all the weights a little, which widens the gap. Finally, make the step a little smaller than last time, and go back to the second step. The script ran forty thousand to one hundred thousand such steps, taking a few seconds, but libraries use faster, exact methods for the same problem.

Kernel SVMs are trained in a different form, which works with the similarity of every pair of examples. That is why they slow down so much as the data grows. A few thousand examples train in seconds, while tens of thousands take minutes. Beyond about a hundred thousand, a kernel SVM is usually too slow, and people switch to a linear SVM, trees, or a network. So SVMs do well with small datasets, from a few dozen to a few thousand examples. The columns must be scaled to similar ranges first, for example each to run from zero to one, because otherwise the column with the largest numbers decides what "wide" means.

The fourth section lists where SVMs were used on a robot arm. An SVM wants a small table of scaled numbers, and five jobs on an arm used to look exactly like that. First, grasp classifiers. Before deep learning, many grasp studies described each candidate grasp with a hand-made list of numbers, such as the object's shape, the hand's pose, and the finger positions. An SVM then learned which ones would hold. Today, networks score grasps straight from a picture or a point cloud. Second, contact, slip, and material classifiers. Features from force sensors or tactile sensors, such as how much the signal shakes or how fast it changes, went into an SVM that said "slip" or "hold", or that named the material being touched. This is a small-feature problem, so an SVM fits it well. Third, collision detection. A few numbers from the joints, such as the gap between expected and measured torque, went into an SVM that told a collision from normal motion. Fourth, finding objects in pictures. Older detectors described each patch of a picture with a list of edge directions and used a linear SVM to decide whether it held a person or an object, until network-based detectors replaced them. Finally, a small head on a big network. A linear SVM can still sit on top of the list of numbers a pretrained network makes from a picture, to learn a few new classes from a few dozen examples. However, logistic regression does the same job and gives a chance, so it is the more common choice.

The fifth section explains what goes wrong, and gives the usual fix for each problem. First, unscaled columns spoil the gap. If one column is in newtons from zero to fifty and another is in radians from zero to 0.1, the gap is measured almost entirely in newtons. The fix is to scale every column to a similar range before training, and to scale new inputs the same way. Second, the settings matter a lot. The C setting, and for the RBF kernel the gamma setting, can move the score on new data from poor to good. The fix is to try a grid of values and test each of them on held-out examples. Third, there is no chance, only a score. An SVM says which side of the line an example is on and how far, but it does not say how likely each class is. A robot that must weigh a 60 percent chance of slip against the cost of squeezing harder needs a chance. The fix is an extra fitting step that turns scores into chances, called Platt scaling, which libraries can run for you. Or, you can use logistic regression or trees instead, which give chances directly. Fourth, kernel SVMs are slow on large data. Training time grows much faster than the number of examples, and every prediction compares the input with every support vector. The fix is a linear SVM, or a switch to boosted trees or a network. Finally, it cannot read raw input. An SVM needs a short list of meaningful numbers, so feeding it raw pixels or a raw force recording works poorly. The fix is to compute good features first, or to use a network.

The sixth section covers libraries, because you would use a library rather than write subgradient descent yourself. In Python, the place to start is scikit-learn, which provides functions for both classification and regression, in both linear and kernel forms. Underneath, scikit-learn uses a C++ library called LIBSVM for kernel SVMs, which you can also use directly from C++. For fast performance on large, linear problems, there is a C++ library called LIBLINEAR. Finally, if your robot program already uses OpenCV, it provides its own SVM tools in both C++ and Python. In scikit-learn, it is best to put a standard scaler and the SVM into one pipeline. The pipeline then scales new inputs the same way as the training data, which fixes the problem of unscaled columns.

The seventh section asks why you would choose an SVM, and what it costs. An SVM is a classifier that chooses the boundary with the widest gap, and with a kernel it can draw curved boundaries as well. It gives a good yes-or-no answer from a small table of scaled numbers, and its answer depends only on the few examples near the boundary. In its time, it won over the alternatives because it found good boundaries from a few hundred examples, it was hard to overfit with a sensible C, and its training always reaches the single best answer. Neural networks of that time were hard to train and needed more data. 

Today, the obvious alternatives usually win. Boosted trees handle columns in different units without scaling, train quickly on millions of rows, give chances, and report which columns mattered. Small neural networks learn their own features from raw signals and pictures, saving the effort of designing the list of numbers. And logistic regression does the linear SVM's job while giving a chance as well. But an SVM is still a reasonable choice for a small, clean table of well-scaled features, of a few hundred to a few thousand rows, where a curved boundary is needed and you have time to tune C and gamma. 

The costs to remember are that you must scale the columns and tune the settings, it gives a score rather than a chance without an extra step, kernel SVMs become slow beyond tens of thousands of examples, they are hard to read to see which columns mattered, and someone must design the features.

The eighth section looks at the written alternative. While learned models are useful, contact can be detected without learning at all. Techniques like impedance and force control stop the arm when the measured force passes a set limit, and safety monitoring does the same for the whole arm. Each of these is a boundary set by hand, meaning one threshold on one number, or a straight line through two numbers. A linear SVM is the learned version of that line. The hand-set line wins when one or two numbers clearly show contact, because it needs no data and a person can check it. But the learned line wins when the right place for it depends on the arm, the tool, and the speed in ways that are hard to work out by hand. A useful middle way is to train the SVM, read its weights, and then fix a hand-checked line close to it.

The final section shows how to use it in Python. The concepts of the widest gap, the soft margin, and the kernel trick all turn out to be just three arguments in the code. The page describes a Python script using scikit-learn. It builds a pipeline that first applies a standard scaler, and then creates the SVM. The SVM is set up with an RBF kernel, a C value of 1.0, and a gamma set to "scale". It then uses a grid search with five-fold cross-validation to try sixteen combinations of C and gamma, ranging from 0.001 to 100, and keeps the best one. 

The library handles the whole optimisation. The kernel argument applies the kernel trick, C sets the soft margin, and gamma controls how quickly the bell-shaped kernel falls off with distance. Setting gamma to "scale" means it uses a starting value worked out from the spread of your own data, which is the default. What you have to provide are your features and labels, and you must not forget the scaling, because unscaled columns spoil the gap. If you need a chance rather than a side of the boundary, you can tell the library to calculate probabilities, which runs the Platt scaling mentioned earlier. But expect the fitting to take several times longer, because that extra fitting is done by cross-validation inside the call. 

What you have to decide is C and gamma, and the grid search in the code is the honest way to do it. Because these settings matter a lot, trying a grid of values on held-out examples is the standard method. The cost is that the search fits the model eighty times, which is quick on a few hundred to a few thousand rows, but slow enough on more data to be one reason the method was left behind.
