Sampling-based planning. This page explains sampling-based planning, which means finding a route for the arm by trying random joint angles, keeping the ones that hit nothing, and joining them up. It covers the three methods you will meet most, which are the rapidly-exploring random tree, or RRT, RRT-Connect, and the probabilistic roadmap, or PRM. It also explains the two ideas those three rest on, which are the configuration space and collision checking. Two further sections then open up the collision checker, and show how to plan when a rule, such as keeping a cup upright, must hold all along the path.

This assumes you already understand graph search, because it uses words like node, edge, and path without explaining them again. 

Sampling-based planning is what most arm software uses to find a route. For example, MoveIt 2 calls the Open Motion Planning Library, or OMPL, and when no other planner is set, it uses RRT-Connect. The page on planning a path covers that in practice, whereas this explanation shows how the methods work inside. Every number and picture described here comes from a real run of the planners on a two-joint arm.

The idea in one sentence is that a sampling-based planner picks random sets of joint angles, throws away the ones where the arm would hit something, and joins the rest into a tree or a network until one of its routes links the start to the goal. 

Here is an everyday example of that idea. You are in a dark, cluttered garage with a torch that only lights a small spot, and you want to reach the door on the far side. Because you cannot see the whole garage, you point the torch at a random spot, and if it shows clear floor you step a little way towards it. Then you pick another random spot and step towards it from whichever point on your trail is closest. So your trail spreads out across the clear floor, until sooner or later a step lands next to the door. A sampling planner does the same thing, with joint angles in place of floor and a collision checker in place of the torch.

The next part explains the configuration space, drawn for a real arm. The random spots in the garage example were places on a floor, but a planner does not search the room at all. Instead, it searches the space of joint angles, which is called the configuration space. One set of joint angles is one point in that space, and such a point is called a configuration. 

The arm used here is a two-joint arm, whose first link is three metres long and whose second link is two metres long. Joint one, the shoulder, can turn from minus 180 degrees to 180 degrees, and joint two, the elbow, can turn from minus 160 degrees to 160 degrees. Three objects stand on the table: a box, a post, and a jar. All three of them are more than three metres from the base, so only link two and the gripper can touch them. The start is at 10 degrees for joint one and 20 degrees for joint two, which puts the gripper at roughly 4.7 metres across and 1.5 metres forward. The goal is 130 degrees and 20 degrees, which puts the gripper at roughly minus 3.7 metres across and 3.3 metres forward.

The diagram shows the table from above, and the same scene as joint angles. The left panel is the table, and the right panel is the configuration space. Its horizontal axis is joint one's angle, and its vertical axis is joint two's angle. Each coloured band is then the set of configurations where the arm touches the object of the same colour. This right panel was made by checking every configuration at one-degree steps, which comes to 115,881 checks. The bands cover 9.2 percent of the space, made up of 3.8 percent for the box, 2.6 percent for the post, and 2.8 percent for the jar.

Three things in this picture are worth noticing, because each one explains why a planner has to work the way it does. First, a square box on the table becomes a curved band, and there is no simple formula for that band's shape. This means that planners do not describe the free space, they probe it. Second, the straight line from start to goal crosses two bands, so turning both joints at a steady rate would hit the post between 23 and 38 degrees on joint one, and then the box between 63 and 83 degrees. Finally, the bands stop at about 95 degrees and minus 95 degrees on joint two, because with the elbow bent more than that, link two folds back towards the base and passes inside the objects. So a free route does exist, and it is to bend the elbow sharply, swing round, and straighten it again. 

For a six-joint arm, the configuration space has six axes, so nobody can draw it, and checking every cell would take billions of checks. So a sampler checks only a few thousand of them instead.

The next section covers collision checking, which is the only question a sampler asks. The previous section described the bands where the arm touches something, but a planner never sees those bands, because it only ever asks about one configuration at a time. A collision checker is the program that answers that question, since it takes one configuration and replies yes, the arm hits something, or no, it is clear. It does that by placing the arm's shapes where the joint angles put them, and then testing those shapes against the shapes of the obstacles. In this example, the test is a simple one, because it takes points every five centimetres along each link and tests each point against the box and two circles. Real checkers, such as the Flexible Collision Library, test meshes and simple shapes for overlap instead, and they are much faster than the planner around them.

A sampler asks the checker two kinds of question, and the second kind is where most of the checking work goes. First, it asks if this one configuration is clear. This question is asked once for each random sample. Second, it asks if this whole straight move between two configurations is clear. This question is asked for each new edge, and because the checker cannot test every point on a line, it tests points along the line at a fixed spacing.

The spacing in that second question is a real risk, because anything thinner than the spacing can slip between two checks unseen. A diagram illustrates checking one move of the arm, in which joint one turns from zero degrees to 60 degrees with joint two held at 30 degrees. For that move, a rod 12 centimetres across stands on the gripper's path. When the move is checked every 10 degrees, which is seven checks in all, the edge passes and the arm would hit the rod. This happens because the gripper is 4.84 metres from the base, so it moves 0.84 metres between one check and the next. Because the rod sits between the 40-degree check and the 50-degree check, no check touches it. However, when the move is checked every one degree, which is 61 checks, the check at 45 degrees hits the rod, so the edge is correctly rejected. 

This is the problem of collisions being checked at sampled points, not continuously. OMPL sets the spacing as a fraction of the size of the whole joint space. So on an arm with wide joint limits, the default comes to the order of ten degrees. The planners on this page check every one degree instead, which is safe here but costs time, because most of the checks counted below are edge checks. However, a continuous check removes the risk altogether.

The next section explains how a collision checker answers. The previous section treated the collision checker as a box that says yes or no, and this section opens that box. A planner may ask it tens of thousands of questions for one move. So the checker is built to answer most of them without ever doing the hard sum. It works in three stages, which are a cheap test with simple wrappers, a tree of wrappers for detailed shapes, and an exact test only where the cheap ones cannot decide. It can also answer two harder questions, namely how far apart two shapes are and whether a whole motion is clear.

The first of those three stages wraps each shape in something simple. It uses a bounding volume, which is a simple shape that completely contains a detailed one. If two bounding volumes do not touch, then the shapes inside them cannot touch either. Because testing two simple shapes takes only a few comparisons, this rules out most pairs almost for free. 

Three kinds of bounding volume are common, and they trade how tightly they fit against how much the test costs. First is an axis-aligned bounding box, which is a box whose sides run along the x, y, and z axes. Two such boxes overlap only if their ranges overlap on every axis, which is six comparisons in 3D. Second is an oriented bounding box, which is a box turned to fit the shape, so it fits much more tightly, although the test is a little more work. Third is a set of spheres, where two spheres touch when the distance between their centres is less than the sum of their radii, which is why many arm planners describe each link as a row of spheres.

A diagram shows link two wrapped in an axis-aligned box and in four spheres, and then the whole arm and scene with boxes. Link two is a bar two metres long and 30 centimetres wide, in the pose 10 degrees and 30 degrees. That link's own area is 0.6 square metres, but the axis-aligned box around it is 2.61 square metres, which is 4.4 times bigger, because the link lies at a slant. Four spheres of radius 29 centimetres cover it more tightly, and an oriented box would fit it exactly, because the link is itself a box. When this first stage is run on the whole scene, there are two links and three objects, and therefore six pairs to test. Because the boxes do not even overlap in five of those pairs, those pairs are clear, and only link two and the post have overlapping boxes. Then that one pair goes on to the exact test, which finds a gap of 49 centimetres, so the pose is clear and only one exact test was needed. So this first stage is called the broad phase, and the exact test that follows it is the narrow phase.

The broad phase puts one wrapper around a whole object, but a real object is often a mesh, which is a surface made of thousands of small flat triangles. Because testing a fingertip against every triangle would be slow, the checker builds a bounding-volume tree once, at the moment the object is loaded. First, it puts one box around the whole mesh, and that box is the top of the tree. Second, it splits the triangles into two halves along the longest side of the box, and puts a box around each half, so that those two boxes become its children. Finally, it repeats for each half until each box holds only one or two triangles. To test something against the mesh you start at the top, and if it misses a box you can skip everything inside that box. If it touches a box instead, you look at that box's two children, and only at the bottom of the tree does the checker test real triangles.

A diagram shows a mug outline of 64 edges inside a tree of boxes. Its tree has 63 boxes in six levels, with 32 boxes at the bottom that hold two edges each. A fingertip 1.2 centimetres across, held one centimetre from the mug, needed seven box tests and no edge tests at all to prove it was clear. However, a fingertip touching the handle needed 13 box tests and four edge tests to find the contact. Testing every edge would have taken 64 tests each time, and the saving grows with the size of the mesh. For a mesh of a million triangles, for example, the tree is only about 20 levels deep.

The two stages so far answer yes or no, but a planner often wants more than that. A trajectory optimiser needs the distance between the arm and each object, so that it can push the path away. In the same way, a safety check wants to know how close the arm came. A convex shape is a shape with no dents or holes, such as a box, a sphere, or a cylinder. For shapes like those, the standard method for the distance is GJK, named after its inventors Gilbert, Johnson, and Keerthi. 

GJK rests on one idea, which is to take every point of shape A and subtract every point of shape B. Then the result is a new shape, called the difference shape. If A and B overlap, then some point of A equals some point of B, so the difference shape contains the zero point, which is called the origin. If they do not overlap, then the distance between A and B is exactly the distance from the origin to the difference shape. GJK never builds the whole difference shape, because it only asks each shape one question, namely which of that shape's points is furthest in a given direction. From the answers it keeps up to three corners of the difference shape. Then it finds the point among them nearest the origin, and asks again in the direction of the origin. It stops once a new answer gets no closer, and for shapes like these it takes only a handful of rounds.

A diagram shows a finger and a jar 1.25 centimetres apart, and their difference shape. The nearest point to the origin on the difference shape is 1.25 centimetres away. GJK found the nearest point to the origin in two rounds, at a distance of 1.251 centimetres. A brute-force check of every corner against every edge gives the same 1.251 centimetres. 

When the shapes overlap the distance is zero, so a different number becomes useful. That number is the penetration depth, which is the shortest move that would separate the shapes. It is the distance from the origin, now inside the difference shape, to the nearest edge of that shape. A second method, the expanding polytope algorithm, or EPA, finds it by growing GJK's last triangle outwards until it reaches that edge. When the finger overlaps the jar, the shortest separating move is 1.88 centimetres. Physics simulators use this number to push objects apart, and some optimisers use it to push a path out of a collision. A shape with dents, such as a mug with a handle, is split into convex pieces first. Then the tree of boxes finds the pieces that might touch, and GJK runs on only those pieces.

The weak point of checking a move at a fixed spacing is that a thin rod fits between two checks. Continuous collision checking gets round that by answering a different question: does the arm touch anything at any moment during the move? One way to answer it is called conservative advancement, and it uses the distance that GJK gives. First, measure the distance from the arm to the nearest object. Second, work out how far any point of the arm can travel for a small turn of the joints. Here only joint one turns, and no point of the arm is further than 4.84 metres from the base, so a turn of a given angle in radians moves no point more than 4.84 times that angle in metres. Third, turn the joint by the distance divided by 4.84 radians, because no point of the arm can have reached the object yet. Finally, repeat until the distance is almost zero, which is a contact, or the move ends, which proves it clear.

A diagram shows this on the same move and the same 12-centimetre rod as before. The first query, at zero degrees, finds the arm 3.63 metres from the rod, which allows a turn of 43 degrees in one step. The second query finds 10.8 centimetres, which allows 1.3 degrees more. Then the third finds contact at 44.29 degrees, which is the same angle a fine search over the whole move gives. So three queries found the contact that seven fixed checks missed and that 61 fixed checks caught. With the rod moved nine centimetres further out, so the gripper passes it by three centimetres, the steps shrink as the arm nears the rod, down to 0.36 degrees, then grow again once it is past, and eleven queries prove the whole move clear. Unlike fixed spacing, this cannot miss the rod, because every step is proved safe before it is taken. Other checkers sweep each link's shape along the move and test the swept shape. Either way, a continuous check costs more per edge than a single point check, and it is worth that cost for thin objects and fast moves.

Because a planner believes whatever the checker tells it, the problems of a collision checker are the ones the planner inherits. First, a mesh with holes or flipped triangles gives wrong answers, and the sign is a collision reported in empty space, or none where two parts clearly overlap, so people repair the mesh or replace it with a few simple shapes. Second, a shape with dents, treated as convex, fills its own dents, and the sign is a gripper that cannot reach into a cup, so people split the shape into convex pieces. Third, a very detailed mesh makes every check slow, and the sign is planning time that grows when a new part is added, so people use a simplified mesh for checking and the detailed one for drawing. Finally, a point check at a fixed spacing misses thin objects, so people use a finer spacing, padding, or a continuous check.

The libraries that do this work include the Flexible Collision Library, or FCL, which provides bounding-volume trees, GJK, and continuous checks in C++ and Python, and is MoveIt's default checker. Coal, formerly HPP-FCL, is a faster fork of FCL with an improved GJK and EPA, used by Pinocchio. Bullet is a physics engine whose collision code is also used on its own, and MoveIt can use it as its checker.

The next section explains how the planners work. All three use the same pieces, which are a sampler, a nearest-neighbour search, a step, and the two collision questions. But they differ mainly in how they grow their structure.

The rapidly-exploring random tree, or RRT, grows a tree of free configurations out from the start. A tree here is a graph in which each node has exactly one parent, except for the very first node. So from any node you can follow parents back to the start. First, put the start in the tree. Second, pick a random configuration inside the joint limits, but now and then pick the goal itself instead, which this script does five percent of the time. This is called goal bias, and it pulls the tree towards the goal. Third, find the node in the tree that is closest to the random configuration. This is a nearest-neighbour search. Fourth, take one short step from that node towards the random configuration, and this script uses steps of eight degrees. Fifth, ask the checker whether the new configuration is clear, and whether the move to it is clear. If both are, add it to the tree, with the nearest node as its parent. If not, throw it away. Sixth, if the new node is within one step of the goal, and the move to the goal is clear, add the goal and stop, then follow parents back from the goal, because that chain of parents is the path. Otherwise, go back to the second step. 

The name comes from the nearest-neighbour search, because a random configuration in a large empty region is most likely to be closest to a node on the edge of the tree. So the tree grows fastest into the space it has not yet covered.

A diagram shows a real run of RRT after 40 tries. Its tree has 28 points, the start and 27 new ones. That is because the other 13 tries landed in a band or crossed one. At the moment it found the goal, after 504 tries, it had 393 points in the tree and had made 3,505 collision checks. Its path goes over the top of the bands, and it is 305 degrees long in total joint movement, with 40 waypoints.

Here are the first three tries of that RRT run, written out with the real numbers. On the first try, the random number is 0.625, which is not below 0.05, so the planner picks a random configuration at 143 degrees and 88.2 degrees. The tree only holds the start, at a distance of 149.5 degrees. One eight-degree step towards the random point reaches 17.12 degrees and 23.65 degrees. It is clear, and so is the move, so it joins the tree. On the second try, the random configuration is minus 71.9 degrees and 119.5 degrees. The closest node is still the start, 128.9 degrees away. One step reaches 4.92 degrees and 26.18 degrees. It is clear, so it joins the tree. On the third try, the random number is 0.005, which is below 0.05, so the planner aims at the goal, at 130 degrees and 20 degrees. The closest node is the one from the first try, 112.9 degrees away. One step reaches 25.1 degrees and 23.4 degrees. That configuration lies in the post's band, where link two touches the post, so the checker says no and the point is thrown away. The third try shows why a planner that only heads for the goal gets stuck, because from here, heading straight for the goal runs into the post's band. So the random tries are what carry the tree up and over the bands.

RRT-Connect grows two trees instead of one, with the first from the start and the second from the goal, and it takes turns between them. First, it grows one tree by one step towards a random configuration, as in RRT. Second, if that worked, it takes the new node and tries to reach it from the other tree. It keeps stepping from the other tree's nearest node towards it until it reaches it or hits something. This greedy stepping is the connect in the name. Third, if the other tree reached the new node, then the two trees are joined, and the path is the start tree's branch followed by the goal tree's branch. Otherwise, it swaps the roles of the two trees and goes back to the first step. On this run, the planner needed 459 tries, 242 points, and 2,612 collision checks. Over 100 runs, RRT-Connect needed a median of 1,922 collision checks against 2,805 for RRT, which is about a third fewer. This is only a small, open problem with two joints. In larger spaces with more joints the gap is usually much wider, which is why MoveIt uses RRT-Connect by default.

The probabilistic roadmap, or PRM, splits the work in two, so that the expensive half of it is done only once. The first part builds a roadmap, and it runs only once for a given scene. It picks many random configurations, and throws away the ones in collision. For each one that is left, it finds its nearest few neighbours, and adds an edge to each neighbour where the straight move is clear. The second part answers a query, and it can run as many times as you like. It joins the start and the goal to their nearest roadmap nodes, the same way. Then it runs Dijkstra's algorithm or A-star on the roadmap from the start to the goal.

A diagram shows a PRM on the two-joint arm. This run picked 150 random configurations, of which ten fell inside a band and were rejected. The other 140 each tried to join their ten nearest neighbours within 45 degrees, and 446 of those edges were clear. Building the roadmap took 15,151 collision checks, whereas answering the query took only 369 more, to join the start and the goal. The path goes under the bands this time, with the elbow bent the other way, and it is 337 degrees long. The build cost is high, but it is paid only once, so when the scene does not change every later query is cheap. When the scene does change, however, edges that pass through the new object are wrong, and the whole roadmap must be checked again. That is why RRT-Connect, which builds nothing in advance, is the usual choice for an arm whose scene changes every time.

The page shows pseudocode for RRT. It loops up to a maximum number of tries. Inside the loop, it picks a target, which is either the goal or a random configuration. It finds the nearest node in the tree, and takes a step towards the target to create a new node. If the new configuration and the move to it are clear, it adds the new node to the tree. If this new node is close enough to the goal and the move to the goal is clear, it adds the goal to the tree and returns the path by following the parents back. If the loop finishes without reaching the goal, it returns that no path was found in time. Notice that it does not say no path exists, it says that none was found in the time allowed. A sampler cannot tell those two cases apart.

The planners are random on purpose, so the same problem gives a different answer each run. The script ran RRT and RRT-Connect 100 times each on the problem above, with 100 different random seeds. A seed is the number that starts a random number generator, so the same seed always gives the same run. A table shows how wide that spread is. For RRT, the fastest run took 170 tries, the median was 408, and the slowest took 964 tries. The 90th percentile was 617, meaning 90 runs out of 100 needed that many tries or fewer. So RRT's slowest run took nearly six times as many tries as its fastest. The paths vary just as much, because the longest RRT-Connect path, at 692 degrees, is more than twice the shortest, which was 295 degrees. This spread is the main reason samplers do not suit a cell that must do the same thing every time. RRT-Connect found paths with fewer checks, but its paths were longer on the median, because joining two trees greedily finds a path fast rather than a short one. That is the reason for the step described next.

A sampler's path has detours, because each step went towards a random point, and two fixes for that are common. The first is shortcutting, in which you pick two points on the path at random. If the straight move between them is clear, you cut out everything between them, and you repeat that a few hundred times. On one run, this step took the RRT-Connect path from 353 degrees to 290 degrees, with four waypoints left. The second fix is to hand the path to a trajectory optimiser, which makes it smooth and pushes it away from obstacles as well as shortening it. There are also planners that keep improving their own path, because RRT-star and PRM-star reconnect nodes whenever a shorter route through them appears. Given more and more time their paths approach the shortest possible, a property that is called asymptotic optimality. But the cost of that is more time per try.

The next section covers constrained planning, which means planning with rules on the way. In every section so far a path only had to avoid collisions, but many tasks add a rule that must hold at every moment of the move, and not just at its two ends. For example, an arm carrying a cup of water must keep the cup upright, and a camera on the wrist must keep pointing at a shelf. In the same way, a cloth must stay flat on the table while the arm wipes it. A rule like this is called a path constraint, and planning with one is called constrained planning. This section shows why an ordinary sampler cannot follow such a rule, and how the usual fix, called projection, works.

The example uses an arm with three joints, seen from the side. The last link is the hand, a cup nine centimetres tall stands on its end, and a box 18 centimetres tall stands on the table. The arm must move the cup from one side of the box to the other. The cup's tilt is the angle of the hand to the horizontal, and for this arm it is simply the sum of the three joint angles. The rule is that the tilt must be zero within a small tolerance, and both the start and the goal already satisfy it. 

A diagram shows an RRT-Connect path with no rule. Its path lifts the cup over the box, tilting the cup by up to 158 degrees on the way, which is nearly upside down. Over 20 runs with different seeds, the largest tilt on each path, before shortcutting, had a median of 139 degrees, and only one run stayed under 45 degrees. Nothing in the planner cares about the cup, so nothing keeps it level.

The obvious fix is to throw away every sample that breaks the rule, which is called rejection sampling, but it fails because almost every sample breaks the rule. A histogram shows the cup tilt for 10,000 random joint samples. The tilts spread almost evenly over the whole circle. Only 105 of them were within two degrees of upright, which is about one in 100. A real six-joint arm has two tilt angles to hold, forward and sideways, so roughly one in 10,000 samples would pass. A rule that must hold exactly, such as keeping the gripper on a line, is passed by no random sample at all. The reason is geometric, because the poses that satisfy the rule form a thin surface inside the configuration space, in the same way a line is thin inside a page. So a random point almost never lands on a line.

Projection is the fix that does work, because it takes a random sample and moves it, by the smallest change it can find, until the sample satisfies the rule. It uses the same idea as numerical inverse kinematics. First, measure how badly the sample breaks the rule, which for the cup is simply the tilt, and call that number the error. Second, work out how the error changes when each joint moves a little. This is the rule's gradient. For the cup it is simple: every joint changes the tilt by the same amount, so the gradient is one, one, one. Third, move the joints against the gradient by exactly enough to make the error zero, as it would be if the rule were a straight line, and that is one Newton step. Finally, repeat until the error is below the tolerance. Throw the sample away if it takes too many steps or leaves the joint limits. 

For the cup rule one step is exact, because it subtracts a third of the tilt from each joint, but most rules are curved and so need a few steps. A diagram shows a curved rule on the two-joint arm, which is that the gripper stays on a rail at a fixed distance. In joint space, the poses that keep the gripper on the rail form a closed green curve. Poses within one centimetre of that rail cover only 0.2 percent of the joint space. Six random samples are shown being pulled onto it in two or three steps. 

A constrained sampler uses projection in three separate places, and the third of them is the one people forget. First, each random sample is projected before the planner uses it. Second, each short step towards a sample is projected, so the tree grows along the rule. Third, each point checked along an edge is projected, because the straight joint move between two poses on a curved rule leaves the rule in the middle. With these three changes, the cup now stays upright the whole way. Over 20 runs the median number of tries was 1,453, against 1,463 with no rule at all. For this arm the rule is flat in joint space, which is the easy case. On a six-joint arm with a curved rule, however, each projection costs a few Newton steps, so constrained planning is slower than free planning. 

OMPL has two further methods for the same job. An atlas covers the rule's surface with small flat patches and then samples on those patches. A tangent bundle does the same, but it builds the patches only when it needs them. Both give more even coverage of the surface than projection does.

Here are the places where an arm has to plan with a rule on the way. Carrying liquid or an open container, pointing a tool or a camera, staying in contact with a surface, keeping two hands on one object, and opening a door or a drawer. Constrained planning brings problems of its own. If projection cannot find the rule, the sign is samples that are thrown away, or that jump to far-off poses, and people limit the step size or use the atlas methods. If the rule is too tight with obstacles, the surface of allowed poses may be split by an obstacle into parts that do not connect, so the sign is a planner that always times out, and people loosen the tolerance. If the planner only rejects samples, and does not project them, it times out for any narrow tolerance. If the rule is checked only at waypoints, the move between two good waypoints can break the rule in the middle, so people project the points along each edge. Finally, if the rule is to follow an exact straight line, a sampler is the wrong tool, and Cartesian paths are the direct way. 

The libraries that provide this include MoveIt 2, which uses orientation constraints and can switch OMPL to its projection-based planning. OMPL itself provides constraint classes where you write the rule and its gradient. TrajOpt treats the rule as a hard constraint during trajectory optimisation.

The next section lists where sampling-based planning is actually used on a robot arm. It is the default way to move an arm through free space when the scene is not fixed. Concrete places include moving from the camera pose to above a detected object, reaching into a bin or shelf, carrying a held object, recovering after a fault, planning many queries in a fixed cell using a PRM, feeding an optimiser, and checking whether a grasp is usable at all before committing to it.

This section sets out the limits of a sampler. A sampling planner is probabilistically complete. This means that if a free path exists, the chance that the planner finds one grows towards certainty as it runs longer. It does not mean that the planner will find one in the time you give it. 

Common problems include a narrow gap, where few random samples land inside it, causing planning to time out. People fix this with samplers that aim near obstacles, a learned sampler, or a longer time limit. If the same move must be identical every run, the arm takes a different route each cycle, so people use graph search on a roadmap of taught poses, an optimiser, or computed industrial motion. If a straight-line tool motion is needed, the tool wanders, so people use a Cartesian path. If a rule must hold all the way, the held object tilts, so people use constrained planning with projection. If the planning time must have a hard limit, people use an optimiser warm-started from the last answer, or a planner on a graphics card. If collision checks are too far apart, the path goes through a thin rod, so people use a smaller check spacing, padding, or a continuous check. If the goal cannot be reached at all, it looks like a timeout, so people check reachability first. If the path is ugly, people use shortcutting and then a trajectory optimiser. If the scene changes after a PRM roadmap is built, the path goes through a newly placed object, so people rebuild the roadmap or use RRT-Connect. 

The narrow gap problem deserves a sentence more, because the chance that a random sample lands in a gap is simply the gap's share of the whole space. A gap that is one percent as wide as the space, in each of six joints, therefore holds a tiny fraction of the samples.

Because these problems are well known, almost nobody writes a production sampler from scratch. The well-known libraries include OMPL, which is the reference collection of samplers in C++ and Python, but brings no collision checker of its own. MoveIt 2 connects OMPL to the arm model, the planning scene, and the collision checker. FCL is MoveIt's usual collision checker. Coal is used by Pinocchio. Pinocchio provides arm kinematics plus collision checks in one library. PyBullet is a simulator whose collision queries are handy for quick experiments. To try the ideas in Python, OMPL's Python bindings are the most direct route. You give it the joint limits and a function that says whether a configuration is clear, and it gives you back a path.

This section answers the four questions for sampling-based planning: what it is, what it does for you, why it rather than the obvious alternative, and what it costs. It is a family of planners that probe the configuration space with random samples and join the clear ones into a tree or a roadmap. What it does for you is find a collision-free route for an arm with any number of joints, without anyone having to describe the free space first. 

The obvious alternative is graph search on a grid of the joint space, which gives the shortest path and the same answer every time. But the number of cells grows 36 times with every joint at 10-degree steps, so a six-joint arm would need over two billion cells. A sampler, by contrast, needs only a few thousand collision checks on this page's problem, and still a manageable number for six joints. So choose a sampler when the arm has more than about three joints and the scene changes. Choose graph search instead when the space is small, or when you already have a fixed roadmap. 

The second alternative is a trajectory optimiser, which gives a smooth path and the same answer every time. But it starts from a guess and improves it locally, so it can get stuck on the wrong side of an obstacle. A sampler does not get stuck that way, because it keeps exploring. That is why the two are so often used together. 

Against all of that, sampling brings costs of its own. The first is that the answer differs every run and so cannot be certified as always the same. The path also has detours and needs cleaning, and the time to find a path has no fixed limit. A failure then tells you nothing about why it failed. And the planner is only as good as its collision checker and its planning scene, because it cannot avoid what it was not told about, and, unless it checks continuously, it can miss what falls between two checks.

The next section covers a third alternative, which is learned models that either help this planner or stand in for it. A learned sampler picks its samples where routes usually pass, such as the front of a gap between shelves, which is exactly the fix for the narrow gap problem. A learned route planner gives a whole route from a point cloud in about the same short time every run. A learned collision checker also gives a quick guess for the thousands of checks a search makes. These win when planning is usually fast but sometimes far too slow, in a cell with a strict cycle time. In open space the ordinary planner still wins, because it is fast enough, free, and checks every move. Even beside a learned helper it stays in the system, because a network gives no guarantee and the exact checker must test the final route.

The final section shows how to use it in Python. The page provides a program that plans for a six-joint arm with OMPL. The code sets up the arm model and geometry using Pinocchio, and defines a function that answers the one question the sampler asks: is this configuration free? It does this by asking Pinocchio to compute collisions for the given joint angles. The code then sets up the joint limits, creates an RRT-Connect planner, sets the start and goal states, and gives the planner five seconds to find a solution. If it finds one, it simplifies the path, which is the shortcutting step, and interpolates points between the waypoints. 

OMPL does the sampling, the tree growing, the nearest-neighbour search inside the tree, and the shortcutting afterwards. Swapping RRT-Connect for PRM or RRT-star is a one-line change, which is the reason a library is worth using here. Pinocchio does the forward kinematics and the shape-against-shape tests. 

What you still have to write is the bridge between them. OMPL brings no collision checker of its own, so the function that checks if a state is clear is yours, and everything about the difficulty of planning lives in that function: how fast it is, whether it includes the table and the objects on the table as well as the arm's own links, and whether it checks the gripper's payload. You also write the conversion between OMPL's state and your own array of joint angles, which is worth writing carefully because it runs thousands of times per plan. 

What you have to decide or measure is the description of your robot and three numbers. The joint limits come from the URDF file. The five seconds allowed to solve is your patience, and a sampler that fails in five seconds may succeed in thirty, so the number is a policy rather than a fact. The state validity checking resolution decides how finely a motion between two samples is checked, and too coarse a value lets the arm pass through a thin obstacle. Finally, you decide what clearance you want, because the collision checker answers touching or not touching, and an arm that plans to within a millimetre of a real table will hit it, so you either inflate the shapes in the URDF or compute distances and insist on a margin.
