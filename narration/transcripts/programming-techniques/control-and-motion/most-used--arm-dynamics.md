Arm dynamics. 

This page explains arm dynamics, which is how the torques at an arm's joints and the arm's motion are tied together. It answers five questions. What torque does each joint need to make a given move, and what happens to the arm when you send it a given torque? How does a controller use those answers to hold the arm still and to follow fast moves closely? And what changes when the gripper picks up a load, and how do you find out how heavy the parts of your own arm are?

The page is written for a reader who has already read the page on PID control. That page explains torque, error, feedback, and feed-forward on a single joint, and it mentions gravity compensation in one paragraph. So this page takes that paragraph and builds it out for a whole arm. You do not need any mechanics beyond knowing that a heavier thing is harder to speed up. Every number on this page comes from a real run of a simulation script, which simulates a two-joint arm rather than drawing its curves by hand.

Dynamics is one of the most used techniques in arm software, even though most users never see it. Instead, it runs inside the drives of every torque-controlled arm, inside every arm simulator, and inside the collision detectors that stop an arm when it bumps a person.

The first section of the page answers why this is needed. A simple PID control loop drives one joint that has a fixed inertia and a fixed pull of gravity, but a real arm is not like that. The pull of gravity on the shoulder depends on how far out the rest of the arm reaches. In the same way, the effort needed to speed up the shoulder depends on whether the elbow is bent or straight. On top of that, when one joint swings fast, it pushes and pulls on the others.

A PID loop can still cope, because it corrects whatever error it measures. But it only corrects an error after that error has appeared, so on a fast move the arm always lags behind. That is why a torque worked out from a model of the arm's dynamics is used. The dynamics of an arm are the rules that link the forces on it to the way it moves. So once you have them, you can work out most of the torque each motor will need before the arm moves at all. This means the feedback loop then only has to clean up the small part that the model got wrong.

The next section gives the idea in one sentence. Since the model has to cover gravity, inertia, and the push between joints, the torque each joint needs is the sum of three parts: the arm's inertia times the acceleration you want, the pull of gravity on the arm in its current pose, and a part that depends on how fast the joints are already turning.

Here is an everyday example of those three parts, carrying a full shopping bag at arm's length. Just holding the bag still takes effort, and more effort the further out your arm reaches, and that is the gravity part. Swinging the bag up quickly takes extra effort at the start of the swing, and you must hold back at the end to stop it. That is the inertia part. And if you spin round on the spot while holding the bag, you feel it pull outwards even though you are not trying to move your arm at all, and that is the speed part.

A robot arm's software does exactly the same sums. It knows from the arm's description how heavy each link is and where its weight sits. So from that it works out all three parts, for every joint, a thousand times a second.

The next part of the page explains how it works, step by step. Because the three parts are easier to compare on one example, every picture on this page uses the same arm. It is scaled down to the size of a real table-top arm, and it swings in a vertical plane, so that gravity pulls on it. 

First, link one, from the shoulder to the elbow, is zero point three meters long and weighs two kilograms. Second, link two, from the elbow to the tip, is zero point two five meters long and weighs one kilogram. Each link is a plain rod, so its weight is centred at its middle. The point where a link's weight is centred is called its centre of mass. Angles are measured from the horizontal. Joint one's angle is the angle of link one. Joint two's angle is the bend at the elbow, measured from link one. Finally, the real arm has a little friction in each joint, zero point zero two newton meters for every radian per second of speed. The controllers' model of the arm believes it is zero point zero one six, which is twenty per cent too low, because a measured friction value is never exact. The controllers run a thousand times a second.

Once that arm is fixed, the torque its two joints need can be written as one equation. The torque equals the mass matrix times the acceleration, plus the speed-dependent part, plus the gravity part. 

The mass matrix is the arm's inertia as the joints feel it. Here a matrix is just a small table of numbers, two rows by two columns for two joints. The top-left number is how hard it is to speed up the shoulder, and it depends on the pose. With the elbow straight, the shoulder feels zero point two four six kilogram meters squared. But with the elbow bent at a right angle, link two sits closer to the shoulder, so the shoulder feels only zero point one seven one kilogram meters squared. The two numbers off the diagonal say how much speeding up one joint pushes on the other.

The speed-dependent part is often called the Coriolis and centrifugal part, after the two effects it contains. The centrifugal effect is the outward pull you feel when you spin a bag. Meanwhile, the Coriolis effect is a sideways push that appears when one joint turns while another joint changes the distance from its axis. Both are zero when the arm is still, because both grow with the square of the speed.

The gravity part is the torque each joint needs just to hold the arm up in this pose. So it is largest when the arm reaches out flat, and zero when the arm points straight up.

A diagram shows a two-joint arm swinging up in zero point eight seconds, and splits the torque at each joint into these three parts. The arm swings from a low, bent position to a higher, straighter position along a smooth path that starts and ends at rest. 

The chart for the shoulder shows that gravity is the largest part the whole time, at between four and six point eight newton meters, while the inertia part is between negative two point nine and positive two point five newton meters. That inertia part is positive while the arm speeds up and negative while it slows down. The speed part is small here, at most zero point four five newton meters, so the total the shoulder needs goes from one point five up to eight point eight newton meters.

The elbow chart looks different. Its gravity part is small and steady, at about one newton meter. But its speed part reaches zero point four six newton meters, which is almost as large as the inertia part and half of gravity. So on the lighter, outer joints of an arm, the speed part cannot be ignored on a fast move.

To show how this works in practice, the page gives a worked example of one instant, done by hand. The arm is at thirty degrees at the shoulder and forty-five degrees at the elbow. The shoulder is turning at one radian per second and the elbow at two. We want the shoulder to speed up at three radians per second squared and the elbow to slow down at two. 

By multiplying the mass matrix at this pose by the accelerations, the inertia part is calculated as zero point five seven six nine newton meters for the shoulder, and zero point one zero zero four for the elbow. The speed part is calculated using the lengths, masses, and speeds, resulting in negative zero point two one two one newton meters at the shoulder, and positive zero point zero two six five at the elbow. Finally, the gravity part is calculated using the weights and angles, giving five point four one four eight newton meters at the shoulder, and zero point three one seven four at the elbow. 

Adding these together, the totals are five point seven seven nine six newton meters at the shoulder and zero point four four four three newton meters at the elbow. This means more than nine tenths of the shoulder's torque is holding the arm up, even in the middle of a move. The page notes that most libraries compute this a second way, using the recursive Newton-Euler algorithm. It works outwards from the base to find each link's acceleration, then inwards to add up the forces. It gives the exact same result.

That same equation can be used in two directions, and each direction has its own name. Inverse dynamics goes from motion to torques. You give it the angles, the speeds, and the accelerations you want, and it gives back the torque each joint needs. The worked example just described is inverse dynamics, and a controller uses it to decide what to send to the motors.

Forward dynamics goes the other way, from torques to motion. You give it the angles, the speeds, and the torques, and it gives back the accelerations that will result. To find them, it solves the equation for the acceleration by undoing the mass matrix. Forward dynamics is what a simulator does, because it starts from a pose, works out the accelerations, moves the arm on by a tiny step of time, and repeats. 

A diagram shows forward dynamics with zero torque, where the arm starts still and the motors are switched off. The arm does not simply drop as a stiff stick would. As the shoulder falls, the elbow bends sharply upwards. This happens because the falling upper link flings the lower link round, and that folding comes from the off-diagonal numbers in the mass matrix and from the speed part. So a controller that treats each joint on its own knows nothing about it.

The simplest use of that equation is the one where nothing moves at all. When the arm is still, the speed and the acceleration are both zero, so the equation shrinks to a single part: the torque needed to hold still is just the gravity part. Adding this to the controller's output is called gravity compensation. 

A diagram shows what this does when holding a pose for three seconds. Without gravity compensation, a proportional-derivative controller sags by five point five degrees at the shoulder and nearly a degree at the elbow. This is because it only pushes when there is an error, so it must keep an error to hold the arm up. With gravity compensation, however, the arm sits exactly on the target with no error at all. The torque it sends is exactly the gravity part at that pose, so the feedback part of the controller has nothing left to do. Gravity compensation is also how a hand-guided arm works. If the controller sends the gravity part and nothing else, the arm floats, so a person can push it anywhere and it stays where it is left.

Once the arm moves, gravity alone is not enough, so there are two common ways to use the whole model instead. The first is feed-forward plus PID. The controller works out the torque that the planned motion needs, using inverse dynamics, and adds it to the output of an ordinary PID loop on each joint. This means the model supplies nearly all of the torque and the PID supplies only the rest.

The second is computed-torque control. Here the controller first works out, from the errors, the acceleration it wants each joint to have. That is the planned acceleration, plus a spring-like pull towards the planned angle, plus a damper-like pull towards the planned speed. Then it uses inverse dynamics, at the arm's measured pose and speed, to turn that acceleration into torques. 

A diagram and a table compare these controllers on the zero point eight second move. PID alone leaves a nearly five-degree error at the shoulder. Gravity compensation alone helps the hold, but it does not help much while the arm is moving, because most of the moving error comes from the inertia and speed parts. The two controllers that use the whole model do much better. At the shoulder they cut the largest error by fifty to five hundred times. At the elbow, feed-forward cuts it by forty times and computed torque by about four times. The tiny error they do leave comes from the friction value in the model being twenty per cent too low.

All the numbers so far are for an empty gripper. But a payload, meaning whatever the gripper is holding, adds mass at the very end of the arm, where it has the most leverage. A half-kilogram payload at the tip of this arm is only one sixth of the arm's own weight, yet it doubles the gravity torque needed at the elbow. 

A diagram shows that if the controller's model does not know about the payload, computed-torque control sags by over ten degrees at the elbow during the move. But when the model is told about the payload, the largest elbow error drops to a quarter of a degree, and the end error is zero. This is why arm makers ask you to enter the payload's mass, and why a gripper that picks up objects of different weights needs the controller to be told each time.

Everything above needs the masses, the centres of mass, and the inertias of the links. The arm maker's description gives values for them, but they are often rounded, and they do not include your gripper, your cables, or your payload. So measuring them on the real arm has a name of its own: identification. 

The key fact that makes identification easy is that the torque is a sum of known terms, each multiplied by an unknown number made from the masses. A diagram shows this fit being done. The arm stops at twenty random poses and records the torque each joint uses to hold still. Plotted on a chart, the elbow's torques lie on a straight line through zero. The slope of this line reveals the unknown mass numbers. By comparing the slope with the gripper empty to the slope with the payload, the math perfectly identifies the payload's mass as zero point five zero kilograms. The inertias need more work, because they only show up when the arm accelerates. The usual method runs the arm along a special path that shakes every joint at several speeds, called an exciting trajectory, and fits all the unknowns at once.

The page then provides the steps as pseudocode. The inverse dynamics function takes the angles, speeds, and accelerations, calculates the mass matrix, the speed part, and the gravity part, and returns the total torque. The forward dynamics function, used by simulators, solves for acceleration, then updates the speed and position by a small time step. Finally, the control loop runs every tick. It compares the planned and actual motion to find the errors, calculates the torque using either feed-forward or computed torque, and sends that torque to the motors.

The next section explains where this is used on a robot arm. First, it runs inside the drives of torque-controlled collaborative arms, providing gravity compensation and dynamics feed-forward. Second, it is used for hand guiding and teaching, where the arm holds itself up while a person moves it. Third, it enables impedance control, making the arm act like a soft spring without sagging. Fourth, it is used for collision detection. By comparing the torque each joint is actually using with the torque that inverse dynamics says it should need, a large gap means something unexpected is pushing on the arm. Fifth, simulators run forward dynamics a thousand times a second to move a simulated arm. Sixth, it is used to check a trajectory before running it, ensuring the planned move won't exceed a motor's torque limits. Finally, it is used for payload checks, comparing holding torques after a grasp to determine the mass of what was picked up.

The page then covers where this model is useful, and where it is not. It helps most on fast moves, heavy arms, soft control, and collision detection. It helps least on slow moves of a stiff, geared, position-controlled arm, where the drive's own PID loops already keep the error small. 

A table lists common failures. If the payload in the model is wrong, the arm sags or triggers false collision alarms. If friction in the gearboxes is large, the arm lags when starting or reversing, which requires identifying a friction model or using a learned arm model. Cables and hoses cause steady errors that change with pose, requiring an extra identified term. If the link masses are wrong, gravity compensation leaves a small sag, which is fixed by identification. If the links or gearboxes bend, the arm rings after fast moves, requiring smoother trajectories. If the arm touches something, the model's torque no longer matches, which requires force control. Finally, many industrial arms only accept joint positions, meaning there is no place to send a computed torque. On such an arm, you can use dynamics to detect collisions, but you cannot send the controller's torque yourself.

Since the equations grow quickly with the number of joints, you almost never write dynamics by hand for a real arm. Instead, you give a library the arm's description, usually a Unified Robot Description Format file, which lists each link's mass, centre of mass, and inertia. A table lists well-known libraries that provide this, including Pinocchio, Drake, the Orocos Kinematics and Dynamics Library, MuJoCo, the Rigid Body Dynamics Library, and libfranka. They provide functions for inverse dynamics, forward dynamics, and the mass matrix.

To put all of this together, a dynamics model gives a controller most of the torque each joint needs before any error appears, holds the arm still against gravity, and lets a simulator predict what the real arm will do. The obvious alternative is to use no model and let the feedback loop do all the work with higher gains. But feedback only corrects an error after it has appeared, so the error grows with speed. Higher gains make the arm stiff and noisy, and they push harder on anything it touches. The costs of using a model are that you need accurate masses and inertias, you must update the payload, the equations ignore friction and bending, and you need an arm that accepts torque commands.

There is also a learned alternative. Most learned arm models keep this physics model and add a small neural network that learns only what is left over, like gearbox friction and cable pull. A learned correction wins when those effects do not fit a simple number. But the physics model still wins as the base, because it needs only a handful of numbers per link and gives sensible answers everywhere. A purely learned model needs hours of varied data and can give strange answers for moves it has not seen. That is why the usual practice is to keep the physics model and let a learned model correct it.

The final section shows how to use this in Python. It explains that instead of writing the equations, you make four calls to a library. The example uses the Pinocchio library. The code builds a model from the robot description file to get the masses and inertias. Then, it sets up the current joint angles, speeds, and desired accelerations. 

It makes one call for inverse dynamics to get the total torque to apply. It makes a second call to get just the gravity part, which is what gravity compensation needs. It makes a third call to get the mass matrix, which is used in computed-torque control. And it makes a fourth call for forward dynamics, going from torques to accelerations, which is what a simulator needs.

The library handles the complex algebra and the speed. The recursive algorithms walk the chain of links just once, so their work grows only in proportion to the number of joints. That is why they are fast enough to run inside a loop at a thousand ticks a second. You still have to write the control loop, read the sensors, and add the PID output, because the model is never exact. And you have to ensure the description file has the correct masses, payloads, and gravity direction, because if the model is missing a held object, it will under-shoot by exactly its weight.
