Uncertainty and confidence. A model always gives an answer, and it gives one even when it has no good reason to. This page therefore answers one question: how can a robot tell when a model is unsure, and what should it do about it?

It is for a beginner who has read the earlier pages of this chapter, especially the page on running a model on a robot, because that page showed that a model can be sure and wrong at the same time. This page goes further than that, since it shows how to check a model's scores, how to fix them, how to measure how unsure a model really is, and finally how to turn all of that into one decision: act, look again, or ask a person.

Every number on this page comes from a real run of a diagram script, although the model and its pictures are simulated. The script makes up a five-class model and draws its right answers at random, so that we know the truth exactly, however the methods run on those numbers are the real ones. Before starting, it helps to have read the page on the Kalman filter, which explains the spread of a set of readings and how a program keeps track of how unsure it is.

The first section gives the idea in one sentence. A model's confidence is only useful if a score of zero point nine really means "right about nine times in ten", so you first check and fix the scores, and then let the robot act only when the chance of being right is high enough for what a mistake would cost.

Here is an everyday example. A weather forecast says "seventy percent chance of rain", and a good forecaster is right about this in a simple way, because on all the days they said seventy percent it rained on about seventy of every hundred. A forecaster who says seventy percent but gets rain on only forty of every hundred such days is not useful at all, even if they are often right about sunny days. You would also act differently on the very same forecast depending on what is at stake, since for a walk to the shop you might ignore a thirty percent chance of rain, whereas for a wedding outdoors you would put up a tent.

A robot needs both halves of this. It needs scores that mean what they say, and it also needs a rule that turns a score into an action, based on what a mistake would cost.

The next part explains that a score is not a probability until it is checked. Since a robot needs scores that mean what they say, it is worth knowing where those scores come from. The last layer of a classifying network gives one number per class, and these raw numbers are called logits, which can be any size and can be negative. A small function called softmax then turns them into scores between zero and one that add up to one, and it does this by making big logits bigger and small ones smaller, in proportion. The earlier page on what a model is showed two such scores for a mug and a bowl.

Scores that add up to one look like probabilities, and a probability is a number that says how often something happens, so a probability of zero point nine means "nine times in ten". However, nothing in training forces the scores to match how often the model is actually right, because training only pushes the score of the right class upwards. A large network trained for a long time learns to push that score very high indeed, even on the examples it gets wrong, which is why modern networks are often overconfident: they give scores that are higher than their real rate of being right.

The simulated model on this page shows the problem clearly. It was tested on two thousand pictures of five kinds of object, namely mug, cup, bowl, jar and box, and it gave the right class for eighty point five percent of them. However, its average score for its chosen class was zero point nine five seven, so it said "about ninety-six percent sure" while being right about eighty percent of the time. On the one thousand, seven hundred and fifty-one test pictures where its score was above zero point nine, it was right on only eighty-five point six percent of them.

A score that matches how often the model is right is called calibrated, so the next two sections show first how to check this and then how to fix it.

The section on checking the scores introduces the reliability curve. Because a score only means something once it has been checked, the check comes first. It needs pictures the model did not train on, with the right answers already known, so a test set works, as described in the page on how a model learns. The check itself has four steps.

First, run the model on every test picture. Write down its top score and whether its answer was right. Second, sort the pictures into groups by score. The usual groups are ten equal slices: scores from zero to zero point one, from zero point one to zero point two, and so on up to zero point nine to one. Third, in each group, work out two numbers: the average score, and the share of pictures the model got right. Finally, draw one bar per group. The bar sits at the group's average score, and its height is the share it got right.

This drawing is called a reliability curve, or reliability diagram. If the model is calibrated, every bar reaches the diagonal line, where the score equals how often the model is right. A bar below the line means the model was overconfident in that group.

The page shows a diagram of a reliability curve for two thousand simulated test pictures, both before and after a fix called temperature scaling. The left half of the picture shows the raw scores, and almost all the pictures, one thousand seven hundred and fifty-one out of two thousand, fall into the top group with an average score of zero point nine nine, where the model was right on only zero point eight six of them. The lower groups sit further below the line still. Groups with fewer than twenty pictures are left out, because a share worked out from a handful of pictures is mostly chance.

To sum up the whole curve in a single number, people use the expected calibration error, or E C E, which is the gap between each bar and the diagonal, averaged over all the pictures so that each group counts in proportion to how many pictures it holds. For the raw scores the expected calibration error is zero point one five three, which means the scores are off by about fifteen percentage points on average.

The next part covers fixing the scores using temperature scaling. Once the check has shown that the scores are wrong, the next step is to fix them, and the simplest fix is called temperature scaling. It divides every logit by one number, T, before softmax, and T is called the temperature. A temperature above one pulls the logits closer together, so softmax then gives less extreme scores, whereas a temperature below one does the opposite. The name comes from physics, where a hotter system is more spread out.

T is chosen on a set of pictures kept aside for exactly this purpose, called the calibration set or validation set, and it must not be the test set, because otherwise the check we just discussed would no longer be honest. The steps are these.

First, run the model once on the calibration pictures and store the logits. Second, try many values of T. For each one, divide the logits by T, apply softmax, and measure how well the scores match the right answers. The usual measure is the log loss: the average of minus the logarithm of the score given to the right class. It is small when the right class gets a high score, and very large when the right class gets a score near zero. Third, keep the T with the smallest log loss. Finally, from then on, divide the logits by that T every time the model runs.

The script tried every T from zero point five zero to five point zero zero in steps of zero point zero one on one thousand calibration pictures, and the best value was T equals two point four eight. The right half of the diagram mentioned earlier shows the test pictures with that temperature applied, and the bars now sit close to the diagonal, because the expected calibration error fell from zero point one five three to zero point zero two three. Of the nine hundred and seventy test pictures that now score above zero point nine, the model was right on ninety-five point three percent.

Temperature scaling has one very useful property, which is that dividing every logit by the same positive number never changes which logit is largest. This means the model's answers do not change at all and its accuracy stays at exactly eighty point five percent, so only the scores change, which makes temperature scaling a safe step to add to any trained classifier.

Two other fixes are also common. Platt scaling fits a small formula with two numbers instead of one, whereas isotonic regression fits a staircase that maps each raw score to a calibrated one. Isotonic regression can fix more shapes of error, however it needs more calibration pictures, because it has more numbers to fit.

The next section is about measuring how unsure a model is, using ensembles and dropout. Calibration fixes the scores on pictures that look like the calibration pictures, however it does not tell you when an input is unlike anything the model has ever seen. For that you need a second kind of signal, which answers a different question: does the model's answer depend on luck?

The idea behind that signal is simple. You train the same kind of model more than once, each time with different random starting weights. Where there were many training examples the copies are forced to agree, because they all had to match the same examples, whereas where there were no examples nothing forced them to agree, so they give different answers. The spread between their answers is therefore a measure of how unsure the model is, and it is often called model uncertainty.

There are two common ways to get several answers. First, an ensemble is a group of separately trained copies of the model. The page on learned dynamics models uses one to see how far a prediction can be trusted. Five copies is a common choice. Second, dropout is a trick used during training, because at each training step it switches off a random share of the neurons, for example one in five, and it was invented to stop overfitting. Monte Carlo dropout keeps dropout switched on when the model is used and runs the model many times on the same input, so that each run switches off different neurons and therefore gives a slightly different answer. "Monte Carlo" is simply a name for any method that uses repeated random tries.

The script tested both of these on a small example. A network learns how hard a gripper must squeeze to hold an object, using the object's mass as its only input, and it has twenty-five training examples, all with masses between zero point one and one point zero kilograms. The true curve is made up, and the network does not know it.

The page shows a diagram where five separately trained networks agree where there were examples and spread apart beyond them, whereas dropout spreads much less. A table then gives the average answer and the spread at three masses. The spread is the standard deviation: a measure of how far the answers typically sit from their average.

Inside the training range, at zero point five kilograms, both methods agree closely with each other and with the true force of eighteen point six newtons. The ensemble gives eighteen point six plus or minus zero point three newtons, and Monte Carlo dropout gives eighteen point three plus or minus zero point five. Outside that range, however, the ensemble's copies spread apart, and that spread is exactly the warning sign you want, because it says "I have not seen objects this heavy". At one point five kilograms, which is outside the examples, the ensemble's spread grows to plus or minus three point four newtons. At two point zero kilograms, which is far outside, it grows to plus or minus five point one newtons.

Monte Carlo dropout also spreads a little more outside the range, but much less than the ensemble does, spreading only to plus or minus one point zero newtons at one point five kilograms, which is about double, and plus or minus one point one newtons at two point zero kilograms, and its answers are all wrong in the same direction, which is a known weakness of it. Monte Carlo dropout is cheaper, because it needs only one trained network, but it tends to report too little uncertainty. An ensemble costs more to train and to run, and usually gives a more honest spread.

Note what neither method says. Neither gives the right answer at two point zero kilograms. The truth, forty-six point zero newtons, is far outside even the ensemble's spread. So the spread is not a range the truth is sure to be in. It only tells you that the answer there should not be trusted. That is what the robot needs to know.

The next part explains a guaranteed set of answers, called conformal prediction. The earlier sections made the scores match the truth on average, whereas conformal prediction goes one step further, because it gives a promise you can write down: "the right answer will be in this set of answers at least ninety percent of the time."

Instead of one answer, the model gives a prediction set, which is a short list of answers. When the model is sure the list holds one answer, whereas when it is unsure the list holds two or more. The list is never empty in practice, so its length is itself a measure of how unsure the model is.

The simplest version, called split conformal prediction, needs a calibration set and works in two steps. Step one is to learn how surprised the model usually is. First, choose how often you are willing to miss. Call it alpha. Here alpha is zero point one, which means "miss at most ten percent of the time". Second, for each calibration picture, look at the score the model gave to the right answer, and work out the surprise, which is one minus that score. A surprise near zero therefore means the model gave the right answer a high score, whereas a surprise near one means it gave the right answer almost nothing. Third, sort the surprises. Find the value q that ninety percent of them are below.

Step two is to build a set for each new picture. You put into the set every answer whose score is at least one minus q.

The script did this with the temperature-scaled scores from earlier, using one thousand calibration pictures that were not used to choose the temperature, and it found q equals zero point eight eight nine, which means each set holds every answer with a score of at least zero point one one one.

The page shows a diagram of a histogram of one thousand calibration surprises with the ninety percent line, then three test pictures and the sets they get. In step one, most calibration pictures have a small surprise and only a few have a large one, and the line for q sits near the top. Then for the test pictures, picture one gives "box" a score of zero point seven eight and every other class zero point zero nine or less. Only "box" clears zero point one one one, so the set is just "box". Picture two gives "jar" zero point six nine and "mug" zero point one three. Both clear the line, so the set is "mug" and "jar". Picture three gives "cup" zero point four three, "mug" zero point two six and "box" zero point one five. All three clear the line, so the set is "mug", "cup", and "box". The model is plainly unsure.

On the two thousand test pictures, seventy-one point four percent of the sets had one answer, sixteen point five percent had two, and the rest had three or more, so the average set held one point four four answers. The right answer was inside the set for eighty-eight point three percent of the test pictures, which is worth comparing with the single best guess, since that was right for only eighty point five percent.

Eighty-eight point three percent is a little under ninety percent, however this is not a fault, because the promise is about the average over many possible calibration sets and any one set of pictures wobbles a little around it. The script checked exactly this by mixing the three thousand pictures and splitting them into a new calibration set and a new test set two hundred times, and the average coverage was ninety point one percent, with the lowest at eighty-seven point three percent and the highest at ninety-two point eight percent.

The promise does need one condition, which is that the new pictures must come from the same kind of situation as the calibration pictures. The technical word for this is exchangeable, meaning that you could swap a calibration picture with a new picture and not be able to tell which was which. So if the robot moves to a darker room, the promise no longer holds.

Conformal prediction also works for numbers, not only for names. Then it gives a range, such as "the object is between forty-one and forty-seven centimetres away", instead of a set.

The next section covers declining to act, using the reject option and cascades. Once a robot has a trustworthy measure of how sure a model is, it can choose not to act on some answers. This is called the reject option. The model is allowed to say "I don't know".

The simplest rule is a threshold, where the robot acts only when the calibrated score is at least some value t. The higher t is, the fewer pictures the robot acts on, and the fewer of those it gets wrong.

The page shows a diagram where, as the threshold rises, the robot acts on fewer pictures and gets fewer wrong. A table gives five points on that curve. If the robot acts only when the score is at least zero, it always acts, and is wrong on nineteen point five percent of pictures. If the threshold is zero point eight zero, it acts on sixty-five point eight percent of pictures, and is wrong on eight point four percent. If the threshold is raised to zero point nine five, it acts on only thirty-two percent of pictures, but is wrong on just two point three percent.

So declining is not free, because to cut mistakes from nineteen point five percent down to two point three percent the robot must decline on sixty-eight percent of the pictures. This means what the robot does with the declined pictures matters just as much as the threshold itself.

A robot has three useful things it can do instead of acting. First, look again. Move the camera to a different angle, turn on a light, or wait for the arm to move out of the view, and ask the model again. A picture from a new angle often removes the doubt. Choosing the best new viewpoint is its own technique, covered in the page on visibility and next-best-view. Second, ask a bigger model. A cascade is a chain of models, from cheap to expensive. The small, fast model answers first. Only when it is unsure does the robot pass the picture on to a larger, slower, more accurate model. Most pictures never reach the large model, so the system is nearly as fast as the small model and nearly as accurate as the large one. Finally, ask a person. Show the picture on a screen and let a person choose. This is slow, so it suits rare cases. The person's answer can also be saved as a new training example, so the model improves where it was weakest.

With conformal sets the rule becomes even simpler, because if the set holds one answer the robot acts, whereas if it holds more than one the robot looks again or asks.

The next part explains setting the threshold from the cost of being wrong. The previous section showed that the threshold decides how often the robot declines, so the next question is which threshold is right. That depends on what a mistake costs compared with what declining costs, and the answer comes from a short calculation.

Say the calibrated probability that the model is right is p, that a mistake costs W, and that asking a person costs A, where both costs are in the same made-up units, such as seconds of a person's time or money. If the robot acts, it is wrong with probability one minus p. The expected cost, the average cost over many such cases, is one minus p, multiplied by W. If the robot asks, the cost is always A. So the robot should act when one minus p, multiplied by W, is less than A. That is the same as acting when p is greater than one minus A divided by W.

The diagram from the previous section also shows two examples of this expected cost, with asking costing one unit. For a sponge, dropping it costs little, because it only means a second try, which is two units, so the threshold is one minus one half, which is zero point five zero, and the robot acts on any grasp that is more likely right than wrong. For a glass, dropping it costs a broken glass and a clean-up, which is fifty units, so the threshold is one minus one fiftieth, which is zero point nine eight, and the robot acts only when the model is very sure.

The same model and the same calibrated scores therefore give different thresholds for different objects, and this is the right way to set a threshold. A fixed number, such as "always zero point eight", is only a guess, whereas a threshold worked out from the costs has a reason behind it.

This calculation only works if the scores are calibrated, however. With the raw scores from earlier, a score of zero point nine nine meant "right eighty-six percent of the time", so a robot that trusted that score with the glass would break about one glass in seven.

The next section lists where this is used on a robot arm. Now that the whole method is in place, it is worth seeing where it is actually used, because these ideas appear in many places. Before a grasp, a grasp model gives each candidate grasp a score. With calibrated scores, the arm tries a grasp only when its chance of success is high enough for the object. In object detection, a detector's scores decide which boxes the robot believes, so calibrating those scores and then using a cost-based threshold is what stops the arm reaching for objects that are not there. For sorting and picking in a warehouse, a cascade sends the rare unclear item to a person while the arm handles all the rest, which is how many commercial picking cells work. For movement models, an ensemble of movement policies, or of world models, can warn when the robot is in a situation that none of its demonstrations covered, so that the robot can then slow down or stop. For checking success, a model that answers "did the grasp work?" or "is the task done?" should also be able to say "not sure". And for learning with error bars, a Gaussian process gives a range with every prediction. It is often used when a robot tunes a setting, such as grip force, from a few tries.

The next part covers where it works, and where it does not. These methods are useful, but each has a known way of failing. The page provides a table listing the main ones. For temperature scaling, if the robot works in a new place, light or set of objects, the reliability curve drops below the line. The fix is to calibrate again on pictures from the new place. For ensembles, if all copies learned the same wrong thing from the same data, the copies will agree, but all be wrong. The fix is to add varied training data, or check with a second sensor. For Monte Carlo dropout, dropout can spread the answers too little, showing a small spread even far from the examples. People instead use an ensemble when the cost of a mistake is high. For conformal prediction, if new pictures are not like the calibration pictures, the right answer is missing from the set more often than promised. The fix is to re-calibrate and keep a log of misses to notice the change. Finally, for any threshold, if costs were guessed rather than measured, the robot might ask far too often, or break things. The fix is to measure real costs, and review them after a few days of running.

Two limits apply to all of them. First, they all need labelled pictures that the model did not train on. Without a calibration set there is nothing to check against. Second, none of them makes the model more accurate. They make the model honest about how accurate it is. A model that is right only half the time will, after calibration, say so, and the robot will decline to act on many pictures.

The section on libraries lists real libraries that provide these methods. Scikit-learn in Python provides the reliability curve, Platt scaling, and isotonic regression, which is useful for models trained with scikit-learn or any scores you can pass to it. TorchMetrics computes the expected calibration error for PyTorch models during testing. PyTorch itself needs no extra library for temperature scaling, as it is just a few lines of code fitting one learnable number divided into the logits. MAPIE in Python provides split conformal prediction for classification and regression around scikit-learn-style models, making it the simplest way to get prediction sets. TorchCP provides conformal prediction for PyTorch models, useful for sets from a neural network. Finally, netcal in Python provides temperature scaling, histogram binning, isotonic regression and reliability diagrams all in one place.

Ensembles and Monte Carlo dropout need no special library. An ensemble is several trainings of the same model with different random seeds. Monte Carlo dropout is the same trained model, run with its dropout layers left switched on.

The next part explains why to do this, and what it costs. The obvious alternative is to take the model's score as it comes, and act when it is above a round number such as zero point eight. That is what most first versions of a robot do.

Checking and fixing the scores is better for three reasons. First, the raw score from a modern network is usually too high. A threshold on the raw score does not mean what you think. Second, only a calibrated score can be combined with the cost of a mistake to give a threshold with a reason behind it. Third, uncertainty from an ensemble or a conformal set warns the robot about inputs unlike its training pictures, which a single score often misses.

The costs are real, though. You need a calibration set: labelled pictures the model did not train on, taken in the place where the robot works. A few hundred to a few thousand is typical. An ensemble of five models costs five times the training and five times the running time. Monte Carlo dropout costs many runs of one model per answer. Declining costs time, because a robot that looks again or asks a person is slower than one that just acts. And the work is never finished. Whenever the robot moves to a new place, new objects or new lighting, the calibration must be checked again.

Temperature scaling is almost free once you have a calibration set, and it never changes the model's answers. So it is worth doing on every classifier that feeds a decision. Ensembles and conformal sets are worth their cost where a mistake is expensive.

The final section is about using it in Python. It explains that temperature scaling, reliability curves, and conformal prediction are all shorter to write in code than their explanations. After reading this section you will be able to write all three with nothing but NumPy and SciPy.

The page shows a short Python script. It first defines a softmax function that safely turns raw logits into probabilities. It then loads a calibration set of logits and true labels. For temperature scaling, it defines a log loss function and uses SciPy's minimize scalar function to find the best temperature, T, between zero point five and five point zero. For the reliability curve, it scales the logits by T, applies softmax, and sorts the scores into ten slices, printing the average score and accuracy for any slice with more than twenty examples. For conformal prediction, it loads a separate set of logits and labels, scales them by T, and calculates the surprise for each right answer. It sorts these surprises and finds the quantile, q, that ninety percent of them fall below. A prediction set is then made by keeping every class whose score clears the line of one minus q.

On the simulated model of this page, that code gives T equals two point four eight. Its quantile comes out at zero point eight eight eight rather than zero point eight eight nine, because the repository's own script tries a grid of temperatures in steps of zero point zero one instead of calling minimize scalar, so the two searches land a thousandth apart.

NumPy and SciPy give you everything here, and that is the point. Minimize scalar searches between the two bounds you give it, so you do not write a search of your own. The conformal step needs even less than that, because once the surprises are sorted, q is simply one of them read off by its position, and counting from the end of a sorted list is the whole of the method. Taking q from the array rather than working out an in-between value is deliberate, because the promise holds for a reading the calibration set really produced. Nothing about any of these three methods is specific to neural networks, so they work on the scores from any model at all.

What you have to collect yourself is the calibration set, and both halves of it matter: the raw outputs before softmax, which many libraries hide, and the right answers, taken in the place where the robot actually works. It must be separate from the test set, and separate again from the pictures used to choose T if you then run conformal prediction on top. You also have to store T and q and apply them every time the model runs, because a temperature that lives only in the notebook that fitted it changes nothing on the arm.

What you have to decide is alpha, how many slices the curve has, and the threshold itself, which is worked out from the cost of a mistake rather than from a round number. If you would rather not write these fifteen lines, then MAPIE gives you conformal prediction and netcal gives you temperature scaling and the reliability diagram. They are worth using for their extra methods rather than for the lines they save.
