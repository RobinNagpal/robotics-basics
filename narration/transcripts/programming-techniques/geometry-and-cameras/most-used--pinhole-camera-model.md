The pinhole camera model. This page explains the pinhole camera model as a technique you can use in any program, and it answers four questions. How does a camera turn a point in the room into a pixel? How do you turn a pixel and a depth reading back into a point? How many millimetres does one pixel cover? And how do you find a point when the camera gives no depth at all? It is for a reader who has read the earlier pages on camera basics and finding one box, which explain pixels, focal length, and the four lens numbers. This page does not repeat those explanations, but instead collects the formulas into one method, adds the parts those pages leave out, and shows where the method is used on an arm.

The core idea is that every pixel looks out from one point along one straight line, and a camera records only which line, never how far along it. The one point is called the pinhole, or the camera's optical centre, and in a real camera it is the middle of the lens. The straight line through the pinhole and a pixel is called that pixel's ray. Here is an everyday example. Hold up a finger at arm's length, close one eye, and line the finger up with a distant tree. Both the finger and the tree then lie on the same line from your eye, so they land on the same spot in your eye and your one eye cannot tell which is nearer. A camera has the same limit, and the pinhole model is the arithmetic of that line.

The diagram shows two rays out of a pinhole, drawn from the side. The blue ray goes straight ahead and lands on the middle of the picture, at pixel u equals 160, while the orange ray lands on pixel u equals 212.5. Three red points sit on that orange ray at 0.20 metres, 0.34 metres, and 0.50 metres ahead. They are different points in the room, but all three land on the same pixel.

The next part of the page explains how this works as arithmetic. The whole model is two formulas, one for each direction. Every number here uses a simulated camera, whose picture is 320 by 240 pixels. Its four lens numbers are fx and fy, both equal to 277.1 pixels, cx equals 160, and cy equals 120. It hangs 0.40 metres above the middle of a table and looks straight down.

Projection takes a point measured from the camera and gives the pixel it lands on. The camera's axes follow the picture, so x points right, y points down, and z points straight out of the lens, and this frame is called the camera's optical frame. The formula is: u equals fx times x divided by z, plus cx. And v equals fy times y divided by z, plus cy. In other words, divide the sideways distance by the distance ahead, multiply by the focal length in pixels, and add the position of the middle of the picture. 

For example, take a spot on top of a box, which is at x equals 0.06442 metres, y equals negative 0.04110 metres, and z equals 0.340 metres from the camera. u becomes 277.1 times 0.06442 divided by 0.340, plus 160, which equals 212.5. v becomes 277.1 times negative 0.04110 divided by 0.340, plus 120, which equals 86.5. So the spot lands on pixel 212.5 comma 86.5. Now, if you double all three distances, the ratios x over z and y over z do not change, so the pixel does not change either. That is the ray from the earlier picture, written as arithmetic, and the division by z is exactly where the distance is lost.

This same rule can be written as one matrix. Libraries store the four lens numbers as a three-by-three grid called the intrinsic matrix, written as a capital K. The top row is fx, zero, cx. The middle row is zero, fy, cy. And the bottom row is zero, zero, one. Projection is then one multiplication followed by one division. That means you multiply K by the point to get three numbers, and then divide the first two by the third. For our point, multiplying K by the coordinates gives 72.25, 29.41, and 0.340. Dividing the first two by the third gives the pixel 212.5 comma 86.5. The third number is the depth, so the division by it is the step that loses distance. You will see this form in every library, and in papers, where it is often written with a scale factor: s times u, v, one, equals K times x, y, z, with s equal to z.

Back-projection runs the formula the other way, and it is also called deprojection. However, it needs one extra number, which is the depth reading for that pixel from a depth camera. The formula is: x equals u minus cx, times depth, divided by fx. y equals v minus cy, times depth, divided by fy. And z equals depth. For our pixel at 212.5 comma 86.5 with a depth of 0.340 metres, x becomes 212.5 minus 160, times 0.340, divided by 277.1, which equals 0.06442 metres. y becomes 86.5 minus 120, times 0.340, divided by 277.1, which equals negative 0.04110 metres. z is 0.340 metres. That is the point we started from, so the two formulas undo each other exactly. 

However, two details decide whether the answer is right in practice. First, the depth must be the distance straight ahead, along z, not the distance along the slanted ray. Nearly every depth camera reports it this way. If a sensor reports the slanted distance instead, you must divide it by the length of the ray direction first. For our pixel, that length is 1.0249, so a slanted reading of 0.3485 metres becomes a depth of 0.340 metres. Second, u and v should be the middle of the pixel, because pixel number 212 covers the strip from 212 to 213, and its middle is therefore 212.5. Using 212 instead moves the point by half a pixel, which is 0.61 millimetres at this depth.

Because a depth camera gives a depth for every pixel, back-projecting all of them gives a point cloud, which is a list of 3D points, one per pixel. The diagram shows one row of pixels across the table and the box. On the left is what the camera gives, which is one depth per pixel, and on the right is what back-projection makes, which is one point per pixel placed along its ray. The table pixels read 0.400 metres while the box-top pixels read 0.340 metres. Just to the left of the box, one pixel sees the box's side and reads 0.396 metres. To the right of the box there is a gap in the points, because the box hides that strip of table from the camera, so no pixel ever looks at it. This missing strip is called a shadow, and every point cloud from one camera has them. However, in a program you rarely loop over pixels one at a time. Instead, you compute the x and y factors once for every pixel, and multiply the whole depth picture by them in one step. The factors never change while the camera and resolution stay the same, so they can be stored.

Back-projection also tells you how much of the world one pixel covers. At depth z, moving one pixel to the side moves the point by z divided by fx metres. So one pixel covers depth divided by fx. At 0.340 metres, that is 0.340 divided by 277.1, which is 1.227 millimetres. At 0.400 metres, it is 1.444 millimetres. This is the most useful single number for judging whether a camera can do a job. If a gap you must measure is 1 millimetre wide, and one pixel covers 1.4 millimetres, no program can measure it. The diagram shows a chart of how much of the table one pixel covers against depth, for the simulated camera and for a sensor with twice the pixels across. Both lines are straight and rise as depth increases, because the width grows in proportion to depth. Doubling the resolution halves the width at every depth. The same rule also measures size. A box top that is 6 centimetres wide at 0.340 metres covers 0.060 divided by 0.001227, which is 48.9 pixels. Read the other way, a box 48.9 pixels wide at 0.340 metres is 6 centimetres wide.

If you have a colour-only camera, it gives no depth, but if you know the surface the object lies on, you can still find the point. The ray from the pixel is known and the plane of the table is known, so the point is where the two meet. The method has three steps. First, turn the pixel into a ray direction in the camera's frame. Second, turn that direction into the room's frame with the camera's rotation. The ray now starts at the camera's position and runs along the turned direction. Finally, find how far along the ray the table is. For a table at height zero, the point is the camera position plus t times the turned direction, where t is the negative camera z position divided by the turned direction's z value. Because the original direction had a 1 in its z place, t is also the depth that pixel would have read. 

The diagram shows a tilted camera 0.40 metres above the table and 0.20 metres back from its middle, looking forward and 60 degrees down. The middle pixel looks straight along the camera's axis, and its ray meets the table after a depth of 0.4619 metres. Another pixel meets the table after 0.4347 metres. Neither number came from a depth sensor, because both came from the pixel, the lens numbers, the camera's pose, and the fact that the table is flat. However, the method breaks the moment the object is not on the plane you assumed. For example, a pixel on top of a box 6 centimetres tall would give a point on the table behind the box instead.

A real lens bends light a little, so straight lines in the room come out slightly curved in the picture. The pinhole model assumes no bending, so real programs correct the bending first and then use the pinhole formulas on the corrected pixels. That correction step is called undistortion. This bending is described by a few more numbers, usually five, called the distortion coefficients. They are measured by calibration, which also shows how large the bending can be: several pixels near the corners of a wide lens. The simulated camera has no bending at all, so its five distortion numbers are zero.

The page provides pseudocode for the whole technique, which works in any language. The projection function takes a point in the camera frame. If the point's z value is zero or less, meaning it is behind or on the camera, it returns nothing. Otherwise, it calculates u and v using the formulas and returns them. The back-projection function takes u, v, and depth. If the depth is missing or zero or less, it returns nothing. Otherwise, it calculates x and y and returns the 3D point. To convert a whole depth picture to points, it loops over each pixel, calls back-projection with the pixel's column plus 0.5 and row plus 0.5, and adds the valid points to a list. To find where a ray meets a plane, it calculates the ray direction, checks if the ray runs parallel to the plane or if the plane is behind the camera, and if not, returns the intersection point. Note the checks in that logic. A missing depth reading is common on shiny or clear objects, and many cameras report it as zero. So back-projecting a zero puts a point right on the lens, which later code will happily treat as a real object.

The pinhole model is under almost every camera measurement on a robot arm. First, it is used for turning a detection into a grasp target. A detector gives a box around a mug. The program takes the middle pixel, reads its depth, and back-projects it. That point, moved into the base frame, is where the gripper goes. Second, it makes a point cloud for the rest of the pipeline. Plane removal, clustering, nearest-neighbour search and iterative closest point all start from back-projected points. Third, it is used for measuring sizes. The width of an object in pixels, times depth divided by fx, gives its width in metres. Fourth, it places a colour-only camera's detections on the table, by meeting each pixel's ray with the table plane. Fifth, it checks a plan by drawing it. Projection runs the other way. To check that the arm's planned grasp point is on the object, project it into the picture and draw a dot. If the dot misses the object, one of the frames is wrong. Sixth, it decides whether a pose can see an object. A planner that chooses camera views projects each object's corners. If they land outside the picture bounds, the view cannot see the object. Finally, it checks contact from above. When the gripper touches a part, a wrist camera can project the fingertips' known positions into the picture to see whether they line up with the part's edges.

The pinhole model is exact for an ideal camera, but real cameras differ from it in a few ways, and each difference shows itself with a sign you can look for. The page provides a table of what goes wrong, what you see, and what to do. If lens distortion is ignored, straight edges bow, and points near the picture's corners are off by several millimetres while the middle is fine. You should calibrate, then undistort the picture or the pixels first. If you use lens numbers from the wrong resolution, every point is too far out or too close to the middle, by the ratio of the two resolutions. You must scale fx, fy, cx and cy with the picture, or read them from the same stream. If colour and depth pictures are not lined up, the object's points are shifted to one side, worst at its edges. You should use the camera's aligned depth stream, or map depth into the colour camera with its own transform. If there is no depth reading on glass, chrome or black parts, you will see holes in the point cloud, or points on the lens at depth zero. You should skip zero depths or use the table-plane method. If there is depth noise at edges, points are smeared between the object and the table behind it. You can shave one pixel off each mask edge, or reject points far from their neighbours. If you have a very wide lens, such as a fisheye, the five usual distortion numbers cannot describe the bending. You must use a fisheye camera model, which libraries offer as a separate set of functions. Finally, if there is a rolling shutter on a moving arm, straight edges lean while the arm moves. You should stop the arm before taking the picture, or use a camera with a global shutter. The model works well for any ordinary camera lens with a field of view up to about 90 degrees, after calibration. That covers almost every camera that is fixed on or near a robot arm.

You rarely need to write these formulas yourself, although they are short enough that many projects do. Several libraries provide them. OpenCV provides functions like projectPoints and undistortPoints in C++ and Python. It is the reference for projection with distortion. Back-projection is undistortPoints followed by multiplying by depth. Open3D turns a whole depth picture into a point cloud in one call in Python and C++. ROS provides image geometry and depth image proc packages, which read lens numbers straight from a CameraInfo message and publish point clouds without any code. The Intel RealSense SDK applies the camera's own distortion model. You can also use plain array arithmetic in NumPy for Python, or Eigen for C++. Whatever you use, check which frame it expects. OpenCV and ROS image geometry use the optical frame, with z ahead and y down, while a robot model's camera link frame has x ahead and z up instead.

The pinhole model is the rule that a camera is one point with rays out of it, and that a pixel records only which ray. It lets you go from a point to a pixel and, with a depth, from a pixel back to a point, using four numbers that the camera reports. The obvious alternative is a lookup table: put a marker at many known places, record which pixel each lands on, and interpolate between them. Some old industrial systems did this, and it needs no model at all. However, it only works at the heights where you measured, it must be redone whenever the camera moves, and it needs hundreds of measurements. The pinhole model, in contrast, needs four numbers, works at every depth, and moves with the camera through one transform. The cost is this. The model is only as good as its four numbers and its distortion numbers, so you must calibrate. It also needs a depth reading or a known plane, because the camera alone never records distance. And it describes one camera only, so a stereo pair or a colour camera beside a depth sensor needs one model per lens, plus the transform between them.

No learned model replaces the pinhole model itself, because learned models that measure need the same rule. Models that guess a depth for each pixel from colour alone, or find named points on an object in the picture, still go through the projection or back-projection formula. The real alternative is to skip the camera model and train a policy, a network that turns pictures straight into arm movements. Such a policy absorbs distortion and odd lenses without anyone describing them. But it needs tens to hundreds of demonstrations for each task, and a camera moved by a few centimetres can confuse it. It also cannot tell you when it is wrong, while every number in the pinhole model can be checked with a ruler.

OpenCV is the right choice for a handful of pixels, because it applies the lens distortion as well as the pinhole formula. The code defines the intrinsic matrix K and a distortion array of five zeros. For projection, it passes a 3D point to the projectPoints function, which returns the pixel coordinates. For back-projection, it passes the pixel to undistortPoints, which undoes the lens bending and divides by fx and fy. What it returns is x divided by z and y divided by z, so multiplying those by the depth gives x and y.

The libraries do three useful things for you. They apply the distortion model, so a real lens works without you writing the polynomial. They keep the four lens numbers in one object, which is harder to mix up than four loose variables. And the Open3D function does the whole-picture loop in compiled code, dropping the pixels whose depth is zero rather than placing points on the lens. 

What you still write yourself is everything around the call. OpenCV's undistortPoints wants its input in an awkward three-deep array shape, so you will write a small wrapper. You also have to choose which pixel to back-project in the first place, which is a detector's job, and you have to add the half pixel mentioned earlier if your detector reports whole pixel numbers. Neither library knows which frame the answer is in, so the transform into the base frame is a separate step. 

What you have to decide or measure is small but unforgiving. The four lens numbers and the five distortion numbers come from calibration, and they must belong to the same resolution as the picture you are using. For Open3D you also have to know the unit the depth picture is stored in, because the depth scale parameter is 1000.0 for millimetres and 1.0 for metres, and getting it wrong moves every point by a factor of a thousand without any error message. Finally, the depth truncation parameter is a real decision: set it just beyond the far side of the table, because everything past it is wall and floor that the rest of the pipeline then has to throw away.
